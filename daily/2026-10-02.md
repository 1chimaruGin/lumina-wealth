### 1. Hyperbolic discounting, with the arithmetic
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

Hyperbolic discounting is how humans discount future value — and it bends sharply near the present, not smoothly throughout time.

Standard economic models assume exponential discounting: a constant-rate decay. The value of ¥100 in one year decays at the same proportional rate as ¥100 in two years. Reality is different. The curve bends sharply near now. Offered ¥100,000 today versus ¥110,000 in one week, most people take ¥100,000. Offered ¥100,000 in 52 weeks versus ¥110,000 in 53 weeks, most choose ¥110,000. Same one-week delay; different choices. That inconsistency is hyperbolic discounting.

The arithmetic is straightforward. Perceived value = A / (1 + k·D), where A is amount, D is delay in days, k is discount factor. Studies consistently find k ≈ 0.01–0.05 for money over days and weeks. Using k = 0.02: ¥100,000 in 1 day is worth ~¥98,000 today; in 7 days, ~¥87,000; in 365 days, ~¥21,000. Reverse the timeline: ¥100,000 in 365 days is ~¥21,000; in 372 days, ~¥20,000. Under exponential discounting, your preference should be identical in both cases. It won't be. The curve bends at the origin.

The operational cost is compounding death. A ¥100,000 investment at 7% annual return becomes ¥196,700 in 10 years. But hyperbolic discounting makes that future ¥196,700 feel far less valuable than ¥100,000 now. You're neurologically tuned to reject the trade — not because you are weak, but because the discount curve is sharpest at the start, exactly when investing matters most.

The curve is steeper for smaller amounts. Someone choosing between ¥5,000 now and ¥6,000 in one week discounts more steeply than someone with ¥500,000 choosing the same ratio. Time perception scales with reward size relative to existing wealth. This is why the poor discount most steeply and stay poor.

> **Key idea.** You discount near-term future rewards 10–20× steeper than exponential decay predicts, making compound growth mathematically invisible.

**Hard truth.** This is neurological, not a choice flaw. The curve bends at present, not future. For the capital-poor, it is devastating: you're forced to grab immediate small earnings exactly when your bias is strongest, ensuring you stay poor.

**What it changes for you.** Your discount curve makes you hunger for the small immediate win and devalue the compound return — exactly backward when capital is scarce.

### 2. The bid-ask spread is a tax you pay on entry and exit
<sub>`markets` · Markets & Trading — *Who is on the other side, and why do they expect to win?*</sub>

The bid-ask spread is the difference between what a buyer will pay and what a seller wants. On the Tokyo Stock Exchange, a liquid stock trading at 1,500 yen might have a bid of 1,499 and an ask of 1,501 — that 2-yen gap is the spread.

When you place a market order to buy, you pay the ask (1,501). When you sell, you take the bid (1,499). One round trip costs you 4 yen on a 1,500-yen position: 0.27% in pure friction. You've paid nothing in commission, but the spread has extracted a real cost.

The spread is not a bug; it is how market makers profit. They exist to provide liquidity. They buy at the bid and sell at the ask, capturing the spread as their revenue. They operate at scale and speed — they're processing thousands of orders per second across many instruments. Their marginal cost per trade approaches zero. Yours does not.

Spreads vary with liquidity. The most-traded Japanese stocks typically have spreads well under 0.1%. FX pairs have tighter spreads than equities because they have more volume. Illiquid stocks or crypto pairs can have spreads of 0.5% or higher. Day traders who execute 100 round trips per year are paying 27% of their capital in spreads alone if average spread is 0.27%, before any other costs.

The spread is unavoidable. Even if you hold for years, you pay it once on entry and once on exit. But its damage scales with frequency. A buy-and-hold investor on a liquid stock pays a negligible cost. A day trader pays a crushing tax.

Who is on the other side? Market makers with lower latency, better fills, and access to multiple venues simultaneously. When you cross the spread, you are systematically taking the worse price. That is not pessimism — that is the definition of the spread.

> **Key idea.** Crossing bid-ask spreads twice per round trip extracts a real tax, payable regardless of whether the market moves in your favor.

**Hard truth.** Market makers pocket the spread; you pay it. Frequent trading makes spreads your largest cost. Without capital or infrastructure, you systematically lose to market makers. This approach does not work for someone trading retail size against professionals.

**What it changes for you.** Spreads are why frequent trading destroys wealth for engineers without access to institutional pricing or technology. Understand the cost before choosing frequency.

**Check it yourself:** Look up the live bid-ask spread on Toyota (TYO:7203) on your broker. Multiply the spread by 2, divide by the mid-price, and convert to percentage. That is your round-trip cost in spreads alone.
### 3. Order types and what each one costs
<sub>`markets` · Markets & Trading — *Who is on the other side, and why do they expect to win?*</sub>

When you place an order, you're choosing between execution certainty and price certainty—you cannot have both.

A market order guarantees execution. You buy or sell immediately at the current market price. The cost: you always pay the full bid-ask spread, which is the gap between what buyers will pay and what sellers demand. On a typical liquid stock listed on the Tokyo Stock Exchange, this spread might be ¥1–¥3 per 100-share unit. On a 100-unit position, that's ¥100–¥300 in immediate losses just from the spread. You own the shares or cash instantly, but the price you get is the worst one currently available.

A limit order guarantees price but not execution. You specify a price and wait. If the stock moves away from your limit price, your order sits unfilled. You avoid paying the spread, but you might avoid execution entirely. This is structurally similar to posting a sign offering to buy milk at ¥150 when the store is selling it at ¥200—you're not going home with milk. The edge is worthless if you never execute.

A stop order (or stop-loss order) sets a trigger: when the price drops to your level, a market order is automatically placed. The intention is to limit losses. The reality: when a stop is triggered, the price is already moving against you. If a stock crashes overnight, your stop at ¥1,000 may execute at ¥950 or ¥800 because the market has gapped. During March 2020, many traders discovered that stops do not protect you in crashes—they execute you into them at terrible prices.

On each trade, you lose to the spread. On short-term trading, these losses compound. A trader making ten roundtrip trades faces 20 spread-crossing events. Even at ¥50 per event on a small position, that's ¥1,000 in costs before any actual losses or wins. Most retail traders only notice the fees charged by their broker, not the spreads they hand to the market-maker on the other side.

> **Key idea.** Market orders guarantee execution but pay the full spread; limit orders guarantee price but might not execute; stops trigger market orders when price moves against you.

**Hard truth.** Market makers profit from the bid-ask spread you pay on every market order. Stop orders protect nothing in crashes—they execute at whatever price exists, often worse than your trigger. Small positions lose meaningful percentages to spreads.

**What it changes for you.** Order types determine your hidden costs per trade; spreads consume significant percentages of small positions, a core constraint for capital-limited traders.

**Check it yourself:** On your brokerage platform, look up any stock you recognize. Note the bid price (what you'd receive if you sold now) and the ask price (what you'd pay to buy now). The difference in yen is the spread. Multiply by your position size to find the total cost of a market order.

### Akagi (アカギ)
<sub>Nobuyuki Fukumoto · 1991 · `time` · manga</sub>

Akagi dramatizes how money flows through decisions made under radical uncertainty. The protagonist's edge comes not from knowing outcomes but from calculating probabilities more accurately than opponents while staying emotionally stable when pressure mounts. In games with incomplete information and real stakes, returns concentrate among those who read psychology better than opponents and whose nerves don't break first.

> **The one idea.** Your edge in uncertain games is psychological stability plus probability reading, not information advantage. Stay calm when stakes rise; most players crack.
