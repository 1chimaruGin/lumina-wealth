### 1. Impermanent loss is neither
<sub>`crypto` · Crypto — *What is actually happening, underneath the price?*</sub>

A constant-product pool holds two assets with reserves x and y, keeping x × y fixed. When the external price moves, arbitrageurs trade against the pool until its ratio matches the market. The pool therefore sells the asset that is rising and buys the one that is falling. Nobody decides this; the curve does it.

Measure that against simply holding the same tokens. If one asset's price rises by a factor r relative to the other, the pool's value relative to holding is 2√r ÷ (1 + r). At r = 2 the pool is worth 94.3% of holding, a 5.7% shortfall. At r = 4 it is 80%, a 20% shortfall. At r = 0.25 the shortfall is also 20%. The pool loses whichever direction the price moves.

This is the 'impermanent' part. If the price returns to its starting ratio, the shortfall disappears. Withdraw at any other ratio and it is realized: the gap between your withdrawn value and the value of simply holding is now a loss you cannot recover by waiting. The name suggests a timing problem. It is a structural cost of the position.

Fees are the offset. A pool pays a percentage of each swap's volume, pro rata to your share. Fee tiers are set per pool, for example 0.05%, 0.30% or 1.00% on Uniswap v3, so a volatile pair must generate a lot of volume to cover a 20% shortfall. Whether fees win depends on volume relative to pool size and on how volatile the pair is. The academic framing, loss-versus-rebalancing (Milionis et al., 2022), is more useful: it measures the continuous cost of being picked off by better-informed traders, and it accrues even when prices end where they started, which the impermanent-loss figure cannot show.

> **Key idea.** Impermanent loss is the price of the pool selling winners and buying losers; it becomes permanent when you withdraw at a different ratio.

**Hard truth.** Most published studies of constant-product pools find LPs on volatile pairs trailed simply holding; figures vary by pool and period. You are the slow side of informed trades. Gas costs make small positions worse. At zero capital, this mostly does not work for you.

**What it changes for you.** With zero capital you cannot fund both sides, so for a salaried engineer in Tokyo this stays a mechanism to understand, not an income option.

**Check it yourself:** Using 2√r ÷ (1 + r), confirm the shortfall at r = 4 is 20%. Then state the annual fee yield a pool would need to offset that 20% shortfall within one year, and compare it with the fee-yield figure shown for one volatile pair on a public DEX analytics site.
### 2. Bills of exchange and the Champagne fairs
<sub>`history` · History & Stories — *Who did this before, and what happened?*</sub>

In the 1100s and 1200s, cloth merchants from Italy, Flanders and France had a settlement problem: coins were heavy, dangerous to carry, and worth different amounts at every mint. The Champagne fairs solved it by concentration. Six fairs a year, rotating among Troyes, Provins, Lagny and Bar-sur-Aube, gathered traders from across Europe, and each ran for several weeks.

Few merchants paid in cash. Picture a Genoese trader owing money to a Flemish supplier. He signed a bill of exchange: a written order to a banker to pay a named sum, in another currency, on a set date, often at a later fair. The payee could sell that bill to a third party before maturity. Debts were offset against each other through the fair system, and only the net balance moved in coin.

The Church condemned usury, and the Third Lateran Council of 1179 reinforced the ban on lending at interest. Bankers worked around it with 'dry exchange' (cambio secco): the exchange rate on the bill was set so the lender earned a return that looked like currency conversion. The return was real. It was interest under another name.

The system ran on reputation. A bill was only as good as the names on it, and a defaulting drawer lost access to future trade. The fairs faded from the late 1200s, when Genoese galleys began sailing directly to Flanders from 1277, moving goods and credit by sea and bypassing the Champagne route.

The mechanism is the lesson. Money was rarely moved; claims were recorded, netted, and cleared by parties who trusted each other. Metal was the fallback, not the medium.

> **Key idea.** Credit lets debts be cleared without moving metal: offset claims between trusted parties, and any price hidden in an exchange rate is still interest.

**Hard truth.** Bills worked because drawers were capitalised and trusted, and bankers earned on the hidden rate. Without capital or a credit record you were a customer of this system, never its operator. The same spread sits inside your bank's currency conversion today.

**What it changes for you.** Your salary and any foreign-currency transfers already run on the same logic: the conversion rate and the fee are where the lender's return hides, so read them.

**Check it yourself:** A bank quotes a two-month forward exchange rate 1% above the spot rate. Annualise it with simple interest: what yearly rate does that imply? Then find your own bank's current foreign-currency transfer fee and spread, and confirm them against the bank's published fee schedule.
### 3. Gross margin
<sub>`financing` · Financing — *Where does capital come from, and what does it cost?*</sub>

Gross margin = (revenue − cost of revenue) ÷ revenue. In a Japanese income statement this is 売上総利益 over 売上高. Cost of revenue (売上原価) includes only costs that rise with each sale: materials, goods bought for resale, per-customer server costs, payment fees, and labour that directly delivers the product. Rent, the salaries of people building the next version, and marketing sit lower down, under operating expenses.

Worked example. You sell for 100 JPY and the goods cost 70 JPY. Gross profit is 30 JPY, so gross margin is 30%. Staff, rent and interest must all be paid from that 30 JPY. Now sell software for 100 JPY with 20 JPY of hosting and fees. Gross margin is 80%, leaving 80 JPY to cover fixed costs. Each extra sale adds far more to profit.

This sets the ceiling on what a business can afford. Grocery and general retail typically run gross margins roughly in the 20–30% range (approximate; it varies by country and format), so they must win on volume and turnover. Listed software companies, particularly in the US, have commonly reported gross margins around 70–80% (approximate). They can spend heavily on sales and engineering because each extra dollar of revenue leaves more behind.

Gross margin also reveals pricing power. If you raise prices and customers stay, the margin holds. If a competitor forces price cuts to keep customers, the margin falls, and a cost base built for a high-margin business turns into a loss.

Gross margin describes the economics of each sale, not management quality or balance-sheet health. A business can have a 60% gross margin and still burn cash.

> **Key idea.** Gross margin is what each sale leaves after its direct costs, and it determines how much a business can spend on everything else.

**Hard truth.** A high margin draws competitors, and every investor reads the same filings, so a strong margin is usually already in the share price. Without customers, a 90% margin on zero sales is zero yen.

**What it changes for you.** Your salary is paid from your employer's gross margin, so the margin on what they sell caps what they can pay you and invest.

**Check it yourself:** Open the latest 決算短信 (TDnet or the company's IR page) for one listed Japanese company and the 10-K for one US software company on SEC EDGAR. From net sales and cost of sales, compute each company's gross margin and note the fiscal year used.
