### 1. Regret minimisation over a long horizon
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

Bezos's regret-minimisation framework is simple: imagine yourself at 80, looking back. Which choice would you regret more—attempting something ambitious and failing, or never trying at all? Pick the path that minimises retrospective regret.

The mechanism is narrative, not optimisation. You construct a backwards identity story: "I'm someone who took the shot." When a venture fails, regret minimisation lets you reframe it: "I'm someone who had the courage to fail." It's emotionally satisfying and hard to argue against, because you're not predicting outcomes. You're predicting how you'll feel about your choices at 80.

Here's what it actually optimises: the coherence and flattery of the narrative you'll tell later. Not expected value. Not wealth. Not even happiness—regret is one emotion among dozens you'll experience over 50 years.

The critical asymmetry: it assumes you can afford to fail. Bezos had approximately $250,000 from his parents and retained income to cushion Amazon's early years. When your existing wealth caps your downside, taking a 30% chance of catastrophic loss is survivable. Without capital, it's not.

For someone with no spare capital and eight hours a week, opportunity cost is brutal. That framework doesn't account for what you're not doing while a low-probability project runs. You're not accruing credentials. You're not building a track record that helps you hire people later or raise capital. You're not learning something with immediate labour-market value.

Regret minimisation lets you spend two years on a project with a 5% success rate, then tell yourself later the story justified it. Expected value asks: what did that cost you? For people like you, the answer is substantial.

> **Key idea.** Regret minimisation optimises for the story you'll tell at 80, not for expected value or opportunity cost.

**Hard truth.** This works if you have capital to burn. You don't. Two years chasing a 5% bet costs you credentials, income, and career momentum you won't recover. The framework mostly amplifies capital that already exists.

**What it changes for you.** You have negative carry: opportunity cost is high. Regret minimisation's logic breaks when capital is tight and feedback loops slow. Expected value frames work better.

**Check it yourself:** Calculate your real opportunity cost: 8 hours/week × 52 weeks × your hourly rate in yen. Compare against what any actual project generates. If you've tracked it before, pull a real number from a statement, not an estimate.
### 2. Stocks: what you own and what you are owed
<sub>`investing` · Investing — *What actually produces returns, and what only looks like it does?*</sub>

A share is a residual claim. If a company is liquidated, it pays in strict order: secured creditors, unsecured creditors, employees and tax authorities, then preferred shareholders if any. Common shareholders get whatever is left, which is often nothing. You cannot demand payment from anyone else in the queue.

Your return comes from two sources: dividends paid out of profit, and changes in the price other investors will pay for future profit. That price is a discounted guess, not a promise. Expectations move prices, so a stock can fall while the business earns more. No contract guarantees a dividend, a buyback or a recovery.

Unlimited upside is a mathematical property, not a typical outcome. Bessembinder (2018) studied US listed common stocks from 1926 to 2016: about 4% of firms accounted for all net wealth created above one-month Treasury bills. More than half of all stocks had lifetime buy-and-hold returns below T-bills.

Japan shows the timing risk. The Nikkei 225 closed at 38,915.87 on 29 December 1989. It first closed above that level on 22 February 2024, about 34 years later. That is a price index excluding dividends, so total return was higher, but a diversified stake still gave no guaranteed payback within a working life.

Tax: for listed shares, gains and dividends fall under separate taxation at a flat 20.315% (national and local, including reconstruction surtax), not added to salary. This rate applies to listed shares only; other instruments follow different regimes. Confirm with the NTA, as rules change.

Costs: the bid-ask spread, any commission, and FX if you buy foreign-listed shares. Each is small, but they compound against you on every trade.

> **Key idea.** A share is the last claim on a company's cash, paid only after everyone else; its upside is unlimited and its payment is never guaranteed.

**Hard truth.** More than half of US stocks lagged T-bills over their lifetimes, and roughly nine in ten active US large-cap funds trailed the S&P 500 over 15 years (SPIVA, approximate). Picking stocks without an edge means accepting those odds, minus costs and tax.

**What it changes for you.** Your wages are paid before shareholders receive anything, so your salaried job already holds the senior position; holding shares means accepting the junior one.

**Check it yourself:** Using the official Nikkei 225 closing values for 29 December 1989 and 22 February 2024 (Nikkei or JPX archive), compute the annualised price return over that period. Then look up the index's recent dividend yield and estimate how much higher the total return would be.
### 3. Convertible notes and SAFEs
<sub>`financing` · Financing — *Where does capital come from, and what does it cost?*</sub>

A convertible note is a loan. A SAFE is a promise to issue shares later. Both let a startup take money now without agreeing what the company is worth. The price is set at the next priced round, when a lead investor names a valuation.

A convertible note accrues interest, commonly a few percent a year, and has a maturity date, often 12 to 24 months out. If no priced round happens by then, the note is technically due. The company rarely has the cash to repay it, so holders renegotiate, usually taking more equity or pushing toward a sale.

The SAFE was created by Y Combinator in 2013. It has no interest and no maturity date. It converts into preferred stock at the next priced round, using whichever is better for the investor: a valuation cap, or a discount to the round price, often in the 10 to 25 percent range.

The post-money SAFE, YC's version since 2018, fixes the holder's percentage against capitalization that includes all SAFEs. Later SAFEs therefore dilute the founders, not earlier holders. Founders who stack SAFEs at different caps often find at the priced round that they own less than they modelled.

Before conversion, the holder is not a shareholder: no vote, and no information rights unless negotiated separately. SAFEs are not debt, so they carry no creditor claim. In a sale before conversion, a post-money SAFE entitles the holder to the greater of its purchase amount or its conversion value.

Uncapped SAFEs are the sharpest surprise. They convert at the next round's price with no ceiling, so an early holder's stake depends entirely on how a future investor values the company, a number nobody negotiated with them.

> **Key idea.** A SAFE or convertible note postpones the price, not the risk: early money buys a claim priced later by others, with no seat at that table.

**Hard truth.** Retail access is minimal; these sit in angel and VC portfolios. Most seed-stage companies never reach a priced round, and returns concentrate in a few winners. Japanese deals rarely use SAFEs; check the nearest analogues, 新株予約権 and 新株予約権付社債, with a lawyer.

**What it changes for you.** If you join a startup on options, SAFEs stacked ahead of you decide how much of your grant survives to a priced round; ask for the cap table.

**Check it yourself:** Open YC's published SAFE documents and find how the post-money SAFE defines 'Company Capitalization'. Does it include the SAFEs being issued? Then compute: a $100,000 SAFE at a $10,000,000 post-money cap gives what percentage, before any priced-round dilution?

### Scarcity
<sub>Mullainathan & Shafir · 2013 · `psychology` · book</sub>

Mullainathan and Shafir argue that scarcity of anything, whether money, time, food or attention, captures the mind and shrinks the mental bandwidth left for other problems, a process they call tunneling. Tunneling pulls attention to the immediate shortfall, so people neglect future costs and trade-offs, and the resulting costly choices, such as fees, high-interest borrowing and missed deadlines, can deepen the scarcity. They propose that slack, a buffer of spare time or money, restores bandwidth, so the problem is partly situational rather than a matter of character.

> **The one idea.** Scarcity is situational: it consumes mental bandwidth needed for planning, so tight budgets make short-term choices more likely, and buffers of slack matter as much as income.

**Verdict.** Worth the hours in a limited form for someone learning how money works. The summary plus the early chapters that introduce tunneling and slack gives you the mechanism; read the rest only to check how strong the evidence is. Watch for how much of the persuasion comes from vivid anecdotes rather than controlled data. It…

**Caveat.** Its bandwidth mechanism is contested: multi-lab replications of ego-depletion work (Hagger et al., 2016) found weak effects. Framing poverty as a thinking problem can sideline cash and price fixes. Popular readings invert it into 'try harder at willpower'; the book says scarcity erodes willpower.
