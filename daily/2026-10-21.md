### 1. The Islamic golden age of finance
<sub>`history` · History & Stories — *Who did this before, and what happened?*</sub>

Under Abd al-Malik, around 696–697 CE, the Umayyad caliphate replaced Byzantine and Sasanian-style coins with Arabic-inscribed ones. The gold dinar settled at roughly 4.25 grams, the silver dirham at roughly 2.97 grams. Treat these as approximate and confirm them against numismatic catalogues. What mattered was a known weight and purity usable across a realm reaching from Central Asia to North Africa.

Coin travels badly. Moving thousands of dinars meant bullion, escorts and theft risk, so merchants settled debts on paper. The sakk was a written order to pay, drawn against money deposited with a banker, and it could be cashed in another city through a correspondent. The suftaja was a bill of exchange: a trader in Baghdad could hand over cash and receive a note payable in Cairo, avoiding the road altogether.

Most of what we know comes from the Cairo Geniza, a storeroom of the Ben Ezra synagogue in Fustat (Old Cairo) that kept discarded documents, with most material from roughly the 10th to 13th centuries. It holds letters, contracts and bills from Jewish merchants trading across the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean. S.D. Goitein's studies of these papers are the main window onto how the system worked day to day.

Explicit interest on loans, riba, was prohibited, so much finance ran on partnership contracts: the qirad, or mudarabah. One party supplied capital, the other labour and travel, and profit was split by agreed shares. A loss of capital fell on the investor, while the manager lost his unpaid labour. Jurists also tolerated legal workarounds, called hiyal, that disguised interest. Risk went into the share, not into a rate.

The word 'cheque' is generally traced through medieval Latin and French back to the Arabic sakk, though the intermediate steps are debated.

> **Key idea.** Credit grew from trust between merchants: paper orders moved money across distance, and profit-sharing replaced explicit interest, so risk was priced into partnership shares.

**Hard truth.** The surviving record is elite merchant paperwork; almost nothing documents ordinary household credit. These instruments ran on personal reputation and trusted networks, which a lone individual lacks. Nothing here gives an individual an edge.

**What it changes for you.** Your salary transfer and any cheque you write descend from these bills, which settle debts between distant parties by written order, the same logic behind cross-border payments.

### 2. Risk of ruin and position sizing
<sub>`markets` · Markets & Trading — *Who is on the other side, and why do they expect to win?*</sub>

Take a bet that wins 51% of the time at even money: a 2% edge per bet. If you risk 1% of your bankroll each time, the chance you ever hit zero, with unlimited bets, is (49/51)^100 ≈ 1.8%. Risk 2% and it is (49/51)^50 ≈ 13.5%. Risk 5% and it is (49/51)^20 ≈ 45%. This is the gambler's-ruin result. The exponent is your bankroll measured in bet-sized units. The edge never changed, only the size did, and ruin rises steeply with it. An edge tells you where the average outcome goes. Sizing decides whether your path survives long enough to get there. Growth has its own ceiling. Kelly (1956) showed that long-run log growth peaks at a bet fraction equal to the edge divided by the odds. For our 51% bet that is 2%. For a 60/40 even-money bet the peak is 20%. Betting 40% on that bet gives roughly zero long-run growth. Betting 50% gives about −3.4% expected log growth per bet, so the bankroll shrinks over time even though every bet has positive expectancy. Practitioners bet well below full Kelly, because the edge is itself an estimate and overestimating it pushes you past the peak. Real market edges, where they exist, are far smaller than 2% per trade, so estimation error is the bigger practical danger. Leverage removes the sizing constraint. Japanese retail FX (店頭FX) is capped at 25x for major pairs under FSA rules, so a 4% adverse move wipes out the margin, and spreads make it sooner. The cap is a ceiling, not a sizing rule. Confirm the current limit with the FSA.

> **Key idea.** A positive edge only pays if you survive its variance; bet size, not the edge, decides whether you ever reach the long run.

**Hard truth.** Few retail traders have an edge against market makers and funds. In a 2019 Brazilian study (Chague et al.), roughly 97% of day traders who persisted beyond 300 days lost money. Sizing cannot create an edge; it only sets how long you survive without one.

**What it changes for you.** With no capital, sizing is theory for now; the binding question is whether any edge exists, testable on historical data before a yen is at risk.

**Check it yourself:** Using the formula in the lesson, compute (49/51)^20 on a calculator. Confirm it is about 0.45, then check the FSA's current retail FX leverage limit and compute the adverse move that exhausts margin at that multiple.
### 3. Gas and fees
<sub>`crypto` · Crypto — *What is actually happening, underneath the price?*</sub>

Ethereum produces a block roughly every 12 seconds, and each block has a fixed capacity measured in gas. A plain ETH transfer uses 21,000 gas. A typical token transfer or swap uses several times that. Every transaction states how much it will pay per unit of gas. Since EIP-1559 went live in August 2021, the protocol sets a base fee that moves with demand: if the previous block was fuller than its target, the base fee rises by up to 12.5%; if emptier, it falls by up to 12.5%. The base fee is burned, which destroys the ETH rather than paying it to validators. Above it sits a priority tip, paid to the block producer. Tips are the lever you pull to get included sooner.

Your cost is gas used multiplied by (base fee plus tip), denominated in ETH. You pay this whether the transaction succeeds or reverts, since a failed transaction still consumed computation.

Think of it as a continuous auction for scarce space, with a price that resets block by block. When demand is low, fees are trivial. When a popular mint, liquidation cascade or market crash hits, the base fee compounds upward within minutes, because each full block raises the next block's price.

Who is on the other side? Mostly other users with time-sensitive needs, plus automated searchers that pay tips to reorder or sandwich trades for profit. This extraction is called MEV, and it is a real transfer of value from ordinary traders to whoever controls ordering. Bitcoin uses a simpler version: users bid satoshis per virtual byte, and the miner keeps the lot.

> **Key idea.** Blockchain fees are a repricing auction for scarce block space, so urgency sets the price.

**Hard truth.** Fees rise exactly when everyone else is panicking, so the people who must act fastest pay the most. Bots skim value from ordinary trades through transaction ordering, and a higher tip mostly buys a faster lane, not safety.

**What it changes for you.** On-chain costs float with global demand and are paid in ETH, so a yen budget for any transfer must allow for spikes you cannot schedule around.
