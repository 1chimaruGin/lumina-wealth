### 1. The narrative fallacy
<sub>`psychology` · Psychology — *How do I behave with money?*</sub>

The narrative fallacy is accepting a causal story because it is coherent, then treating that coherence as evidence. Nassim Taleb popularised the term. Daniel Kahneman's work explains the mechanism: the feeling of understanding rises with how tidy a story is, not with how accurate it is.

Markets are where this costs most. A share price already aggregates many buyers and sellers who have read the same famous story: a product launch, a charismatic founder, a sector tailwind. Because the story is public, its effect is usually in the price before you encounter it. A story only helps if it contains something the price does not: a figure you have verified, a fact others have overlooked, or a forecast that differs from consensus and is right more often than chance.

Test any bullish article with a simple exercise. List each causal claim, such as margins will expand because of X. For each, name the number that would confirm or refute it, then find that number in the company's annual securities report on EDINET. Most claims will have no figure attached. That gap is where the narrative is doing the work the data should do.

Scale matters too. Professional managers have research teams, data feeds and full-time analysts. S&P Dow Jones Indices' SPIVA scorecards repeatedly find that most active funds trail their benchmark. In the US large-cap category, roughly nine in ten funds underperformed the S&P 500 over 15 years. These figures are approximate and change with each report.

The practical lesson is not that stories are false. It is that a story you can repeat is probably already priced, so ask what you know that the price does not.

> **Key idea.** A coherent story feels like evidence, but the price already contains the story everyone knows; only numbers the crowd hasn't priced can help you.

**Hard truth.** The seller of a famous story is often someone with faster data and a reason to sell. Without a research edge, your odds of beating that counterparty are poor, and trading costs compound against you.

**What it changes for you.** Your income already depends on one employer's sector, so a story-driven stock pick in the same field adds correlated risk you may not notice.

### 2. Separating business and personal money
<sub>`management` · Management — *How do I run the money I have?*</sub>

A single account makes every number a mixture. Salary, rent, groceries, a brokerage transfer and a software subscription all pass through one balance, so the balance describes nothing in particular. You cannot tell whether an investment made money, because the cash that funded it also paid for dinner. ### 3. Availability bias
<sub>`psychology` · Psychology — *How do I behave with money?*</sub>

Availability bias: people judge how likely something is by how easily examples come to mind. Tversky and Kahneman described it in 1973. Ease of recall tracks recency, drama and repetition, not frequency.

In 1978, Lichtenstein and colleagues asked people to estimate how often US deaths came from each cause. Respondents rated homicide and accidents as more common than they are, and rated stroke and diabetes as rarer, though the diseases kill far more people. Coverage, not counts, set the ranking.

Markets show the same pattern. Barber and Odean (2008) studied US retail brokerage trades and found individual investors were net buyers of stocks that drew attention: those in the news, with unusual volume, or with extreme one-day moves. Barber and Odean also found that the most active households in their sample earned about 11.4% a year against 17.9% for the market, over 1991–1996. Attention decided where the money went, not fundamentals.

Recent experience is weighted heavily. After a sharp fall, crashes feel likely and cash feels safe. After a rally, the sense of risk drops just as prices have risen. Your estimate moves with the chart, not with the odds.

Long memory is rare. The Nikkei 225 closed at 38,915.87 on 29 December 1989. It did not close above that level until 22 February 2024, about 34 years later. Someone who only experienced the 2000s or 2010s would read Japanese equity risk very differently from someone who watched the 1990 crash.

The corrective is structural, not psychological: set rules before the news arrives and judge by base rates rather than anecdotes. Read base rates from a long index history, not the last year's headlines. A headline is a sample of one, chosen because it was unusual.

> **Key idea.** Your sense of how likely something is tracks how easily examples come to mind, and recent or dramatic events come to mind most easily.

**Hard truth.** Knowing the bias does not cure it. Brokers, apps and media profit from attention and routinely show you the vivid thing. For a small account, chasing headlines mostly means paying costs and buying after rallies.

**What it changes for you.** Your news feed is a biased sample of market history, so a multi-decade index record is a better guide to risk than this year's headlines.

**Check it yourself:** Find a free source of Nikkei 225 daily closes, locate the first close above 38,915.87 (the 29 December 1989 level), and compute the years and months between 29 December 1989 and that date.
