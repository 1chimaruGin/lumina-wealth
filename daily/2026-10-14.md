### 1. Sequencing your financial decisions
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

The return on your first decisions before you invest a single yen is not measured in percent. It's measured in avoiding compound losses.

If you have high-interest debt — credit card debt in Japan typically runs 15–18% APR — paying it off returns 15–18% guaranteed. There is no investment that delivers that with certainty. A 20% portfolio return is a good year; a 15% debt payoff happens every month you don't skip a payment. The math is not subtle: ¥100,000 in credit card debt costs ¥1,500–1,800 per month in interest alone if you pay only the minimum. That is ¥18,000–21,600 a year evaporating. A ¥100,000 Nikkei 225 investment returns roughly ¥6,000–8,000 annually on average — not ¥18,000 in losses you've already locked in.

The sequence is mechanical. First: emergency fund. A 3–6 month buffer of living expenses in a savings account earning near-zero interest. You will hate holding cash in a 0.05% account while stock markets run. That hatred is the signal you understand the cost. The cost is sleep. If you have zero buffer and your laptop dies or your contract ends, you reach for the credit card. Now you have borrowed at 18% to fix what an emergency fund would have covered at 0%.

Second: debt elimination. Pay off anything above 5–6% APR. Aggressively.

Third: tax-advantaged accounts. In Japan, the iDeCo (individual defined-contribution pension) and NISA (Nippon Individual Savings Account) exist specifically to let you invest without the standard 20.315% tax on gains. A ¥10,000 gain in a regular brokerage account costs ¥2,031.50 in tax. The same ¥10,000 in NISA costs you zero. Over twenty years, that tax difference swallows returns.

Only then: broad market investing.

Most people skip steps two and three. They invest while carrying debt and missing tax shelter contributions. The math works against them from the start.

> **Key idea.** Debt payoff and tax shelter optimization return more than stock picking before you own a single share.

**Hard truth.** Without capital to deploy, investing while carrying 18% credit card debt is mathematically irrational. You ensure a 12%+ annual loss versus the guaranteed return of debt payoff. This ordering is not optional, it is forced on you by arithmetic.

**What it changes for you.** For a salaried engineer in Tokyo with limited spare capital, the sequence determines whether small earnings compound or evaporate.

**Check it yourself:** Look up a standard Japanese credit card APR online (typically 15–18%). Calculate the monthly interest on ¥100,000 at that rate. Compare to the Nikkei 225's historical monthly return of roughly ¥600–700.
### 2. The solidus: seven centuries of stable money
<sub>`history` · History & Stories — *Who did this before, and what happened?*</sub>

In about 309–312 AD, Constantine I set the solidus at 1/72 of a Roman pound of gold, roughly 4.5 grams. For the next several centuries the Byzantine state kept that weight and very high purity nearly unchanged. Justinian issued essentially the same coin, and it became the standard across the Mediterranean; in Western Europe it was called the bezant.

Three mechanisms held it there. First, the state had a monopoly on minting and tested the coins, and counterfeiting and clipping were punished severely, sometimes by mutilation or death, though enforcement was uneven. Second, taxes were assessed and largely paid in gold, so the state needed the coin to be accepted at face value. Third, the state paid its officials and soldiers in solidi, so it had every reason to keep the unit trustworthy.

The stability broke slowly. Debasement began around the 1040s, under emperors facing rising military and fiscal strain, and accelerated after the loss of much of Anatolia following Manzikert in 1071. By the 1080s the gold content had fallen far below its original level. The exact figures vary by study and are approximate, but the fall was severe. Meanwhile Arab dinars, which were lighter, circulated widely in the same trade networks.

The lesson is that the coin's stability was a fiscal and political arrangement, not a property of gold. The metal weighed the same throughout. What changed was the state's ability and willingness to keep its promise.

> **Key idea.** A currency stays stable only while the state needs it stable, collects taxes in it, and controls who may mint it.

**Hard truth.** Gold held its value here only because one state enforced and needed it. Holding gold yourself gives no such guarantee. Its price has swung by large multiples over decades, with long flat stretches in between.

**What it changes for you.** Yen purchasing power rests on Japan's fiscal and central-bank commitments, not on any metal, and your salary is a claim on that promise.

**Check it yourself:** Find the recorded weight of a Constantine I solidus and of a late 11th-century Byzantine gold coin in a catalogue such as the Dumbarton Oaks Byzantine coin collection. Compute the percentage change in gold weight and note any fineness figures given.
### 3. Stablecoins and what backs them
<sub>`crypto` · Crypto — *What is actually happening, underneath the price?*</sub>

A stablecoin holds a $1 price only because something enforces it. Three designs try, and each breaks in its own way.

Fiat-backed tokens such as USDC and USDT are issued against reserves, mostly cash and short-dated US Treasury bills. The issuer promises redemption at $1. Arbitrage holds the peg: if the token trades at $0.99, buyers take it to the issuer for $1. Reserves are usually checked through attestations by accounting firms, which is weaker than a full audit. Tether paid a fine to New York's attorney general in 2021 over misleading statements about its reserves.

Over-collateralised tokens such as DAI lock more crypto than they issue. At a 150% minimum ratio, $100 of DAI needs $150 of collateral. A one-third fall in the collateral's price takes the position to 100%, and it is liquidated. This works in calm markets. In the March 2020 crash, liquidation auctions broke down when network fees spiked, and some collateral sold for almost no DAI.

Algorithmic designs replace reserves with a sister token. Terra's UST, which collapsed in May 2022, was pegged through LUNA: when UST traded below $1, holders could burn it to mint $1 of LUNA. The Anchor lending protocol paid around 19.5% on UST deposits, a rate the system could not sustain. When confidence broke, LUNA was minted in enormous quantities as its price collapsed, and UST fell to around 10 cents. Tens of billions of dollars disappeared within days.

The pattern repeats: the peg is credible only as long as redemption is credible. Japan's yen stablecoins fall under the Payment Services Act, so issuer registration and reserve rules are the first things to check.

> **Key idea.** A stablecoin is a liability with a promise; its real value is whatever its assets fetch when everyone asks for redemption at once.

**Hard truth.** Holding a stablecoin makes you an unsecured creditor of a company whose books you cannot fully inspect. The issuer keeps the reserve interest; you carry the peg risk. Runs test the peg exactly when redemption is hardest. Your realistic gain is zero; your tail loss is large.

**What it changes for you.** As a Tokyo salaried engineer, a yen stablecoin is a claim on a registered issuer under Japan's Payment Services Act; check that registration before treating it as cash.

**Check it yourself:** Find the latest reserve attestation or report for USDC (Circle) or USDT (Tether) on the issuer's website. Add cash plus short-dated US Treasuries as a share of total reserves, then note what remains. Also record whether the document is an attestation, an audit, or neither.
