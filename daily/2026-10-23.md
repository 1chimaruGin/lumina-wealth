### 1. Margin calls
<sub>`markets` · Markets & Trading — *Who is on the other side, and why do they expect to win?*</sub>

A margin call is a demand for more collateral. Under Japanese 信用取引 (margin trading on listed shares), you post a 委託保証金 (deposit) of at least 30% of the value of shares you buy on credit, and the broker lends the rest. Your 維持率 is your equity divided by the position's market value, and the broker checks it continuously. When it falls below the broker's minimum, often somewhere between 20% and 30% depending on broker and stock, you receive a 追証 (additional margin) demand with a short deadline. If you do not pay, the broker closes positions itself, choosing timing and price without regard to your view.

Run the arithmetic. Deposit 30%, borrow 70%, and let the price fall by d. Per unit of original position, equity becomes 0.3 minus d and position value becomes 1 minus d. At a 20% minimum, the call arrives after a 12.5% drop. Interest, fees and haircut rules shift the figure, but the structure holds: your leverage sets how small a move ends your position.

The forced seller is not reconsidering anything. It sells whatever bid exists, in size, at the moment the rule trips. Other leveraged holders face the same rule, so falling prices raise the call rate, which forces more sales, which pushes prices lower. Brunnermeier and Pedersen (2009) modelled this feedback loop. The buyer on the other side is whoever has cash and can take the position at a discount: a market maker or a patient investor who knows the liquidation is mechanical.

The scale is not hypothetical. Long-Term Capital Management ran balance-sheet leverage of roughly 25 to 1 in 1998. After Russia's default its positions could not be unwound quickly, and a group of banks put in about $3.6bn, coordinated by the New York Fed, to avoid disorderly sales.

> **Key idea.** Leverage turns a price move into a deadline: a margin call sells you out on the lender's schedule, at whatever bid the market offers.

**Hard truth.** Borrowing adds interest and a second risk: your exit is set by the lender's rules, not your thesis, and the buyers who absorb that exit usually pay a discount. Margin is closed to you anyway; the 30% deposit is cash you lack.

**What it changes for you.** With no spare yen you cannot post the 30% deposit, so margin is closed to you; knowing its mechanics is how you read leveraged products marketed to retail investors.

**Check it yourself:** From your broker's published 委託保証金率 and 最低維持率 for 一般信用 (or its margin-trading page), compute the drop d = (initial minus maintenance) divided by (1 minus maintenance) that triggers a call, then find the 追証 deadline in its terms. Confirm both against the broker's current rules, since they change.
### 2. Japan: 個人事業主 versus 合同会社 versus 株式会社
<sub>`management` · Management — *How do I run the money I have?*</sub>

Three forms, one question: when does the extra legal layer pay for itself? A 個人事業主 costs nothing to create. You file a 開業届 with your tax office within one month of starting, and that is the whole entry fee. A 合同会社 costs roughly 6万円 in government fees if filed electronically, more on paper, because the registration tax has a 6万円 floor. A 株式会社 costs more: a notary must certify the articles, and the registration tax floor is 15万円, so expect around 20万円 before any agent fees. These are approximate. Check the 法務局 and 公証役場 pages for current figures.

The running costs matter more. A 法人 files its own return and pays a local levy every year even when it makes a loss (in Tokyo, roughly 7万円 a year for a small 合同会社; confirm with the Tokyo Metropolitan Government). Salary paid to the owner is deductible only if it is fixed in advance. Personal income for a 個人事業主 is taxed progressively at 5% to 45%, plus a 2.1% reconstruction surtax, plus about 10% resident tax. Small 法人 pay corporate tax at roughly 15% on the first 800万円 of profit and 23.2% above that, per NTA rules, which you should confirm. The corporate rate only beats the personal route once profit is large enough to absorb the fixed costs, levies and owner salary. Below that, incorporating adds cost and paperwork without cutting tax.

A cheaper step-up exists within sole proprietorship. 青色申告 keeps you a 個人事業主, and with double-entry bookkeeping and e-Tax filing it unlocks a 65万円 deduction against profit. Confirm the conditions with the NTA, since they change.

> **Key idea.** Incorporating adds fixed costs to save tax on profit, so it only pays once profit is large enough to clear those costs.

**Hard truth.** Most small side businesses never reach the profit where a company saves tax. Without capital, the sole-proprietor form is the only one with no fixed cost.

**What it changes for you.** For a salaried engineer, incorporation only becomes a question after side profit is large and recurring, so the sole-proprietor route is the sensible starting point.

**Check it yourself:** Using the 法務局 and 公証役場 official fee pages, add up the electronic-route government fees for a 合同会社 and for a 株式会社 with 資本金 of 1円, then note the difference.
### 3. Short selling
<sub>`markets` · Markets & Trading — *Who is on the other side, and why do they expect to win?*</sub>

A short sale is a loan of shares you do not own. You borrow shares, sell them at today's price, and must buy them back later to return them. You profit only if the price falls before you cover.

In Japan, retail short selling runs almost entirely through 信用取引 (margin trading). Under 制度信用 (system margin), shares come from the exchange-linked lending pool and the position must be closed within six months. Under 一般信用 (general margin), the broker lends shares it holds, on terms set by each firm.

Two costs fall only on the short side. The first is the lending fee (貸株料), charged daily while the position is open, whatever the price does. The second is 逆日歩 (reverse daily interest): when a stock is scarce to borrow, the exchange charges shorts a premium that can be large relative to the share price.

The payoff is lopsided. Short a share at ¥1,000 and the best case is ¥1,000 profit, when the company goes to zero. If it rises to ¥3,000, you lose ¥2,000 per share, and nothing caps further losses. A stop-loss order is not a guarantee: an overnight gap fills at the opening price.

Collateral sits in a margin account. When losses erode it below the required level, the broker issues a margin call or closes your position for you.

Who is on the other side? Market makers quoting prices, securities-lending funds earning fees on their holdings, and institutions with research edges. In a study of US data (Boehmer, Jones and Zhang, 2008), heavily shorted stocks subsequently underperformed, and the informative shorting came from institutions, not individuals.

For listed-stock margin trades, gains are taxed under the 20.315% separate taxation regime. Confirm the current rule with the NTA and your broker, because rules change.

> **Key idea.** Shorting caps your gain at zero, uncaps your loss, and charges a daily fee, so you must be right on both direction and timing.

**Hard truth.** You cannot short without a funded margin account, so this is closed to you in practice. Fees and gap risk hit the short seller first, and your counterparties have better data and cheaper financing.

**What it changes for you.** Your zero-capital constraint means you cannot open a short in practice, so treat shorting as market mechanics to understand, not a route to income.

**Check it yourself:** On a Japanese broker's margin terms page, find the current lending fee for a general-margin stock and the minimum margin deposit for 制度信用. Compute the six-month lending cost on a ¥1,000,000 short at the stated annual rate.
