### 1. Myopic loss aversion: the cost of checking
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

Thaler and Benartzi's 1995 study of US market returns from 1926–1990 found that equities returned roughly 20% per year, while bonds returned roughly 5%. A portfolio split 60% stocks, 40% bonds should have generated ~13% annual returns. But the researchers discovered something else: that same 60/40 portfolio showed daily losses on approximately 25% of trading days. Stocks alone showed losses on roughly 48% of days.

When people look at their portfolio and see a loss, loss aversion activates. Researchers found the pain of a loss outweighs the pleasure of an equal gain by roughly 2.25 to 1. See enough daily losses, and investors rationally respond by shifting to safety—moving money from stocks to bonds. The data showed that frequent checkers drifted to portfolios of only 30% stocks, 70% bonds. This reduced their expected annual return from 13% to approximately 8%.

The arithmetic is brutal: 8% compound growth over 30 years multiplies your starting capital by 10×. 13% multiplies it by 27×. The gap is not a rounding error. It is the difference between retirement and continued work.

The mechanism is not mysterious. A 2% daily drop is ordinary noise in a 20% annual return. But noise you see becomes signal you react to. Checking monthly instead of daily, or annually instead of monthly, correlates with holding higher equity exposure and capturing that 5% annual premium. Checking obsessively—multiple times daily, as technology now permits—correlates with drifting to allocations that feel safe but guarantee lower returns.

This is not about market timing or stock picking. It is about whether the act of looking wrecks your ability to hold what you need to hold.

> **Key idea.** The more you look, the more losses you see; loss aversion then forces you to safer assets, which costs you the equity premium over decades.

**Hard truth.** You have no capital, so you cannot yet be harmed by checking a portfolio. But this predicts what happens if you save and invest—the pain makes you quit. Most quit. Knowing this now is your only advantage.

**What it changes for you.** If you eventually save capital for investment, checking frequency will determine whether you hold enough risk to build wealth or abandon the attempt.

**Check it yourself:** Download 1 year of S&P 500 daily closing prices (freely available online). Count how many days closed lower than the previous day. Divide by 252 to get the daily loss frequency. Why does this frequency (roughly 50%) not match the annual return being positive?
### 2. Inflation
<sub>`intelligence` · Intelligence — *How does money actually work?*</sub>

Three mechanisms cause inflation, often confused as one problem.

Demand-pull inflation: Money supply grows faster than real output. Central banks lower rates or expand credit, governments deficit-spend, consumers have more yen than sellers have goods. Prices rise. Milton Friedman's framing—"inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon"—captures this, though it oversimplifies. Japan's 1980s asset bubble involved substantial demand-pull.

Cost-push inflation: Input costs rise independently: wages, energy, raw materials. Firms raise prices to preserve profit margins. If workers then demand higher wages to match rising prices, and firms raise prices again, you get a wage-price spiral that persists even if money supply stays flat. The 1970s oil shocks drove this in Western economies.

Inflation by expectations: If people expect prices to rise, they raise them preemptively. Workers demand higher wages anticipating inflation; firms raise prices expecting wage pressure. This becomes self-fulfilling. Central banks fight it through credibility—the belief that they will keep inflation low actually keeps it low.

Each occurs independently. A real shortage requires different policy than monetary excess. Expectations can drive inflation with stable money and stable costs. Real inflations involve all three; identifying which dominates matters for policy.

Raising interest rates kills demand-pull and anchors expectations. It worsens cost-push—higher borrowing costs drive up business expenses, which become prices. Wage controls suppress cost-push but distort labour markets. No policy cures all three without painful tradeoffs. This is why central banks fight inflation but rarely eliminate it: the cure—unemployment or deflation—costs more than the disease.

> **Key idea.** Inflation has three distinct sources—money, costs, expectations—and no policy fixes all three without damaging tradeoffs.

**Hard truth.** Fixed salary earners lose real income as prices rise faster than wages. Employers rarely match inflation in raises. Wealth transfers from you to debtors and asset holders. Central banks accept this loss to avoid worse outcomes.

**What it changes for you.** Your income is nominally fixed; inflation reduces purchasing power unless you renegotiate aggressively or switch jobs, both difficult in practice.

**Check it yourself:** Look up Japan's CPI for the past five years (Statistics Bureau website) and compare to nominal wage growth (METI data). Calculate whether your salary has kept pace with inflation. If not, you have experienced real wage loss.
### 3. Fees, and the third of your money they take
<sub>`investing` · Investing — *What actually produces returns, and what only looks like it does?*</sub>

A 1% annual fee removes approximately one-third of your final balance after 40 years. Here is the mathematics:

Assume ¥1,000,000 invested with 7% average annual returns. After 40 years, without fees: ¥1,000,000 × (1.07)^40 = ¥14,974,000. With a 1% fee reducing your return to 6% net: ¥1,000,000 × (1.06)^40 = ¥10,286,000. The difference is ¥4,688,000, or 31% of the final balance.

Fees compound in your disfavour because they reduce the base that then earns the next year's returns. A 0.1% fee (the cost of some Japanese index funds like SPDR S&P 500 ETF) removes roughly 3% over 40 years. A 1% fee on an actively managed fund removes roughly 31%. A 2% fee (common for Japanese domestic equity funds) removes roughly 55%.

Real fees in Japan: A listed equity ETF on the Tokyo Stock Exchange might charge 0.1–0.3% annually. An actively managed domestic equity mutual fund averages 1.2–1.8%. Retail FX accounts charge effective fees through spreads that can reach 2–4% annually on small positions. The FSA does not regulate fee structure uniformly across instruments; confirm any fee against the prospectus, because these change.

The mechanism: you pay fees whether the market rises or falls. If your fund loses 10%, you pay the fee on the remaining 90%. The fee is not a transaction cost you pay once—it is a percentage extracted every year, compounding against you.

Fees also disguise themselves. You see the expense ratio in the prospectus, but you do not see the bid-ask spread your broker charges, the trading costs the fund incurs, or the performance drag if the fund manager trades frequently and loses to the index anyway.

Most actively managed funds in Japan underperform a low-cost index fund by approximately their fee difference. Over 40 years, that gap becomes your ¥4,688,000 loss—roughly a third of your wealth—gone to fund management companies, not to you.

> **Key idea.** A 1% annual fee compounds to remove roughly one-third of your final balance over forty years because fees shrink the base earning returns each year.

**Hard truth.** Fees compound downward invisibly. A 1% fee removes roughly a third of your wealth, but you never see it in statements—only annual charges of roughly 1% appear. Most people do not calculate the full 40-year damage until wealth is already lost.

**What it changes for you.** When you eventually have capital to invest, fee selection determines whether your money compounds or slowly bleeds away to fund managers.

**Check it yourself:** Look up the annual expense ratio (fee percentage) for the SPDR S&P 500 ETF and for an average Japanese domestic equity mutual fund via your broker's website. Compute: ¥1,000,000 × (1.07 - fee_rate_1)^40 vs. ¥1,000,000 × (1.07 - fee_rate_2)^40 for each. The difference is the fee impact over 40 years.
