### 1. When acting fast IS correct
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

Credit card debt in Japan legally maxes at 18% APR (確認: verify on your statement). At 18% compounded monthly, a ¥1,000,000 balance costs approximately ¥196,000 in interest in year one. In year two, it costs more—interest compounds on itself. By year five, without any payment, the original million grows to approximately ¥2,440,000. The cost of waiting multiplies exponentially.

Japanese fixed-income returns for comparison: savings accounts ~0.1%, 10-year JGBs ~1.2%, equities (Nikkei 225, long-term) ~8–10% nominal. None beat 18%. The closest is equities, and they carry downside risk; 18% is guaranteed cost, not hoped-for gain.

Mechanism: if you have capital that could either pay debt or invest, paying debt is mathematically superior. A ¥500,000 debt at 18% costs ¥90,000 annually in interest. The same capital invested in equities at 10% return would earn ¥50,000 pre-tax, or roughly ¥40,000 post-tax (20.315% on Japanese equities in a standard taxable account). Paying debt saves ¥90,000; investing nets ¥40,000. The advantage is ¥50,000 per year, and it compounds: next year's ¥90,000 is even larger.

The principle applies to positions, not just debt. You set a rule to sell a stock at ¥10,000. It falls to ¥8,000. Waiting for recovery "costs" you ¥2,000 of capital that could be deployed elsewhere, plus the opportunity cost of that capital earning 3–5% annually in a safer instrument. Every month you hold a losing position you've decided to exit extends your cost.

Speed is correct when the cost of delay—measured in compound interest or compounding opportunity cost—exceeds your transaction cost (trading fees, emotional friction, tax on exit). For debt above 15%, speed is almost always right. For positions that violate your rule, speed is nearly always right: the math does not reward waiting.

> **Key idea.** When compound interest works against you, speed is correct. Waiting on debt or a broken position compounds your cost, not your gains.

**Hard truth.** Compound interest on debt is invisible—you don't watch ¥90,000 leave your account annually; you just see debt persist. Invisibility is why people wait. But the cost is compounding.

**What it changes for you.** When you receive your first meaningful capital (bonus, raise), if you carry debt over 10%, pay it before investing. The math is unambiguous.

**Check it yourself:** Find a credit card statement (yours, a friend's, or from your bank's website). Calculate: (monthly interest charge shown) × 12 ÷ (balance shown that month) = APR. Verify it matches the quoted APR on the card's terms.
### 2. Liquidity disappears exactly when you need it
<sub>`markets` · Markets & Trading — *Who is on the other side, and why do they expect to win?*</sub>

Quoted depth is a fair-weather number. An order book shows what people will trade at right now, and the price on your screen is the midpoint between the best bid and best offer. A market order to buy costs you half the spread, and a market order to sell costs you the other half. On a liquid stock that round trip is small. On a thin one it is not, and it gets worse as the book empties. The depth is supplied mostly by market makers and high-frequency firms. They earn thin margins per trade and quote only while their risk limits allow. When volatility jumps, they widen spreads or pull quotes to protect their own capital. This is rational for them, and it arrives exactly when you need liquidity. Three episodes show the pattern. On 6 May 2010 the Dow fell about 9% intraday and recovered most of it within minutes. Some large-cap stocks briefly printed at a penny because no bids stood nearby. On 1 August 2012 Knight Capital lost roughly $440 million in about 45 minutes after a faulty software deployment, then had to unwind positions into a thinning market. On 5 August 2024 the Nikkei 225 fell 12.4% in one session, its largest one-day fall since 1987, as yen-funded carry trades unwound after the Bank of Japan's 31 July rate rise and weak US jobs data. The mechanism is reflexive. Falling prices trigger margin calls and stop-losses, those force sales, and the sales drain the bids further. Brunnermeier and Pedersen (2009) modelled this as a loop linking market liquidity to traders' funding liquidity.

> **Key idea.** Quoted depth is conditional: it vanishes when many holders want out at once, so size positions to the exit, not the entry.

**Hard truth.** Your exit is priced by whoever is still quoting when you sell, which in stress is the fewest people. Retail sells at the bid, which in a crash can sit far below the last price shown. Without spare capital, a 10% gap can force a sale you did not plan.

**What it changes for you.** Your steady salary does not stop margin calls or forced selling, so any position you cannot hold through a 10% index day is one you may be pushed to sell at the worst bid.

**Check it yourself:** Download daily closes for the Nikkei 225 for 2 August and 5 August 2024 from JPX or any public data source. Compute the one-day percentage change and confirm it matches the 12.4% fall reported at the time.
### 3. Tracking net worth
<sub>`management` · Management — *How do I run the money I have?*</sub>

Net worth is assets minus liabilities, measured at market value on one fixed date. Assets include yen bank balances, brokerage holdings at their closing value, and pension-type accounts such as iDeCo at their reported value. iDeCo cannot be withdrawn before retirement age under current rules, so confirm that with the provider, since rules change. A car counts at resale price, not purchase price. Liabilities include loan balances and unpaid card debt. Future salary is not an asset. A home you live in is ambiguous: include it only if you decide to, and write that choice at the top of the sheet so the trend stays comparable.

Pick the last business day of each month and copy each balance directly from the statement or app, never from memory. Use one row per account and one column per month. Sum assets, sum liabilities, subtract. Once the sheet exists, the monthly update takes about ten minutes.

The monthly change has two sources: money you added or withdrew, and price movement in what you hold. Record deposits and withdrawals in a separate column so both are visible. Once the portfolio is large relative to monthly saving, price movement often outweighs saving within a single month, so the line can fall in a month you saved steadily. Read the trend over twelve months, not one reading.

The figure is an outcome, not a score. Income, spending and savings rate are the inputs you control. Net worth shows whether those inputs are accumulating after market returns and debt costs. A salary figure cannot show that, and neither can a single good month.

> **Key idea.** Net worth is assets minus liabilities at one fixed date; its monthly change splits into what you saved and what markets did to what you hold.

**Hard truth.** Once invested, markets move this number more than your saving does in many months. Over long periods most actively managed funds trail their index after costs (S&P SPIVA reports). Expect growth from saving and time, and do not read a down month as a verdict.

**What it changes for you.** Yen bank accounts, Japanese brokerage holdings and iDeCo balances belong on one sheet, so the figure reflects everything you own rather than whichever account you check most.

### When Genius Failed
<sub>Roger Lowenstein · 2000 · `markets` · book</sub>

LTCM's partners, a team with serious academic finance credentials, ran convergence trades on small price gaps between related bonds and borrowed heavily so that thin spreads would pay. Their models assumed these gaps would close and that losses would stay within recent historical volatility. When Russia defaulted in August 1998 and investors fled to safety, the spreads widened instead of closing, forcing sales into a market that would not absorb them, and a Fed-brokered rescue by private banks followed in September.

> **The one idea.** Leverage turns a small mispricing into a fatal liquidity problem: a position can be right eventually and still kill you if you cannot fund it until it converges.
