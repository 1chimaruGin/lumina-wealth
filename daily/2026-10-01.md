### 1. Base rates for how long things take
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

Base rates are the actual frequencies something happens. A small business takes an average of 18–36 months to first profit, assuming it survives; 70% fail before year five. A technical skill requires roughly 10,000 hours to genuine competence—at 8 hours weekly, that's roughly 25 years of consistent work. A diversified equity portfolio compounds at roughly 5–7% annually in real terms; Japan's Nikkei 225 averaged around 4% nominal over three decades. Zero capital means compounding never starts.

The mechanism: compounding requires both capital and time. At 6% real annual return, capital doubles every 12 years. At zero starting capital, it stays zero. A business requires months or years of zero income while you guess what customers want; when the guess is wrong—the typical case—you burn time and restart. Skill acquisition is nonlinear: the first 1,000 hours produce almost nothing, the next 1,000 build momentum, the next 1,000 approach competence.

Your constraints: 8 hours weekly and zero capital, combined with a pattern of dropping projects. Income streams usually die where timeline meets boredom—6–18 months in, before the idea proves itself, before grind becomes competence. Most bootstrapped projects signal whether they work within 18–36 months. An engineer switching technical specializations needs 2–3 years at 40+ weekly hours to hire at market rate in the new skill. At 8 hours weekly, add 3–5 more years.

Historical returns: Japan's stock market returned roughly 4% nominal annually over three decades. US equities averaged 10% nominal. In Japan, listed equity gains face flat 20.315% taxation (confirm with current NTA 所得税法); cryptocurrency gains are 雑所得 and taxed progressively—different instruments, completely different tax regimes. Real interest rates in Japan have been near-zero for decades. Any strategy based on market returns requires: capital, years untouched without selling, and acceptance of 70%+ of years where you lose money.

> **Key idea.** Most things that look fast on the surface—business profit, skill mastery, wealth accumulation—follow predictable timelines that are longer than people expect.

**Hard truth.** Without capital, compounding does not start. With 8 hours weekly and a history of dropping projects, an income stream needs 2–3 years minimum of grinding dull work before money arrives. Most people quit before the timeline resolves.

**What it changes for you.** As a salaried engineer with limited capital, you cannot compete on speed—plan projects with longer timelines or accept you will abandon them.

**Check it yourself:** Calculate: 10,000 hours ÷ (8 hours weekly × 52 weeks yearly) = your timeline to baseline skill competence at your stated pace. Write down the number.
### 2. Why index funds beat most professionals
<sub>`investing` · Investing — *What actually produces returns, and what only looks like it does?*</sub>

The mechanism is arithmetic. If the market collectively returns 10% in a year, that 10% includes every manager—the best and the worst, the ones who picked correctly and those who didn't. Before fees and taxes, the average active manager's return equals the market return, because active managers collectively *are* the market. After their management fees—typically 0.5% to 1% annually, plus trading costs—the average active dollar returns less than the index.

S&P Dow Jones Indices publishes the SPIVA scorecard annually. Over 15 years to 2023, approximately 88% of large-cap US equity active managers underperformed the S&P 500 after fees. Over 20 years, it approaches 92%. The longer the timeframe, the worse active management looks. International developed markets show similar patterns: roughly 80–85% of active managers trail their benchmarks over 15 years. Even emerging-market equities—where information asymmetry might favour active research—see roughly 70–75% of managers underperform over 15 years.

This is not opinion. It is not saying active managers lack skill. It says that the average active manager's skill (or luck) does not exceed their costs and slippage. For every manager beating the index, another is lagging it by roughly twice their fee, because the math is zero-sum before costs and negative-sum after.

The reason past winners don't predictably outperform is survivor and selection bias: the funds that are published in league tables are the ones that survived. Dead funds were more likely to underperform. And the fees charged by the best-performing funds tend to rise, eroding future returns.

Passive index funds charge 0.03% to 0.2% annually. That gap—0.5% to 1% versus 0.03%—compounds dramatically. On $10,000 invested for 20 years at 10% average returns, the fee difference means roughly $2,700 more retained with indexing.

> **Key idea.** Before fees, the average active manager returns the market return; after fees, they underperform the index by the amount of their costs.

**Hard truth.** Knowing this changes almost nothing: most intelligent investors still chase active funds convinced they'll find the one that beats the odds. With little capital, you cannot afford those odds.

**What it changes for you.** When you eventually save, this difference compounds into thousands of yen over decades—the single largest return lever you control, and most people ignore it entirely.

**Check it yourself:** Find the annual expense ratio for an active Japanese equity fund and an index fund tracking the same market. The percentage-point gap between them is your annual fee disadvantage of active management.
### 3. Bitcoin's design
<sub>`crypto` · Crypto — *What is actually happening, underneath the price?*</sub>

Bitcoin's core innovation is solving double-spending without a bank: proving you own a coin by proving you did computational work to create the ledger entry. Satoshi Nakamoto published the whitepaper in October 2008, during the financial crisis, to address the specific problem of digital cash requiring trusted intermediaries.

The mechanism: every 10 minutes, a new block is added to the ledger. Miners compete to solve a computational puzzle; the first to solve it gets to add the block and receives newly minted bitcoin as reward. The puzzle difficulty adjusts every 2,016 blocks (roughly 2 weeks) to keep block time constant. This is proof of work — the entire chain's history is secured by the cumulative computational effort required to rewrite it. To alter a single transaction from a year ago, you would need to re-solve every puzzle from that point forward, faster than the network adds new blocks. This is computationally infeasible.

Supply is algorithmically capped at 21 million coins. Early blocks generated 50 coins; the reward halves every 210,000 blocks (approximately every 4 years). The first halving was November 2012 (25 coins). Second: July 2016 (12.5 coins). Third: May 2020 (6.25 coins). Fourth: April 2024 (3.125 coins). This schedule is baked into the protocol — no vote, no negotiation, no exceptions. By 2140, virtually all coins will be mined.

As of mid-2025, approximately 21.4 million bitcoins exist. The network consumes roughly 150–200 terawatt-hours annually, more than some nations produce.

The fixed supply and proof-of-work mechanism create a system where control is distributed and history cannot be retroactively changed. This solves the ledger problem. It does not solve how you get the coins in the first place, or why anyone accepts them in payment.

> **Key idea.** Fixed supply and proof-of-work create a ledger no single entity can falsify, solving digital-cash double-spending without a bank.

**Hard truth.** Bitcoin's design solves a 2008 ledger problem, not zero-capital income generation. Fixed supply creates zero-sum transfers: your profit requires someone else buying higher. Without capital, this mechanism offers nothing to deploy.

**What it changes for you.** As a learner without capital, understanding Bitcoin's mechanics (proof of work, fixed supply, the problem it solves) matters more than price speculation.

**Check it yourself:** Look up current Bitcoin circulating supply on blockchain.com or a similar explorer; verify it matches the 21 million cap and identify the scheduled date of the next halving.
