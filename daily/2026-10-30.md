### 1. Implied volatility
<sub>`markets` · Markets & Trading — *Who is on the other side, and why do they expect to win?*</sub>

Take a stock at 100 with 30% implied volatility and one month to expiry. An at-the-money straddle (one call and one put, same strike) costs about 6.9. The common approximation is straddle ≈ 0.8 × σ × √T × price, with T in years: 0.8 × 0.30 × √(30/365) × 100. That price is the market's forecast. The straddle roughly equals the average move, in either direction, that the market expects over the month.

Implied volatility (IV) is the σ you must plug into a pricing model, usually Black-Scholes, to reproduce the traded price. Options are quoted in IV because it makes prices comparable across strikes and expiries. The VIX is the 30-day S&P 500 implied volatility, calculated by CBOE from SPX option prices.

Known events get priced in. Before earnings, IV for expiries spanning the report rises. Afterwards it typically collapses, the so-called vol crush. A call can be right on direction and still lose money, because the realized move must exceed what the premium already priced in, and the IV drop subtracts value on top.

The premium tends to be rich. For decades, S&P 500 implied volatility has on average exceeded subsequently realized volatility. This gap, the variance risk premium, is documented by Carr and Wu (2009). Coval and Shumway (2001) found that at-the-money straddles on S&P 500 futures options earned negative returns on average. The size of the gap varies by year and by stock.

The other side is dealers hedging customer flow, institutions selling covered calls, and funds writing puts for yield. They are paid to warehouse the jump. Japan's listed equivalent is Nikkei 225 options at Osaka Exchange (JPX), with the same mechanics.

> **Key idea.** Option prices encode the market's forecast of volatility, so buying options before a known event means paying for the move the crowd already expects.

**Hard truth.** Event options are sold by professionals who hedge and collect a premium that has historically exceeded realized moves on average. Before fees and spreads, buyers start behind. Naked selling means losses far larger than the premium, and it needs margin you do not have.

**What it changes for you.** For you, a stock's event option is usually a priced-in bet against professional sellers who hedge, and your 8 weekly hours give you no informational edge.

**Check it yourself:** Compute the straddle for a 100-unit stock at 30% IV over 30 days using straddle ≈ 0.8 × σ × √(days/365) × price; you should get about 6.9. Then read the CBOE VIX white paper and state the target maturity it uses (30 calendar days) and which options it is built from.
### 2. Where the yield actually comes from
<sub>`crypto` · Crypto — *What is actually happening, underneath the price?*</sub>

Every yield is a payment from somewhere. There are four sources, and only some of them are paid from real economic activity.

1. Borrowers. In DeFi lending (Aave, Compound), people post collateral and borrow, paying interest. Rates rise when borrowing demand exceeds the deposits available; the rate is set by a utilisation curve, not a promise. Yield here is a real transfer from leveraged traders and borrowers. It is the most honest of the four because a named party pays it in a named asset.

2. Staking. Ethereum pays validators newly issued ETH plus transaction fees. Issuance is set by protocol rules; the reward rate falls as more ETH is staked. Recently roughly 2.5–3.5% a year (approximate; check a live dashboard). Most of it is newly minted ETH, so it is paid by diluting other holders rather than by a borrower.

3. Token emissions. A protocol pays its own token to depositors. The advertised APY is quoted in that token, so it stays high while the token falls. Your real return in yen is roughly (1 + APY) × (price change) − 1. Emissions are the main source of triple-digit figures on protocol dashboards. They are a transfer from new buyers to earlier holders, and they last only while new money keeps arriving.

4. Someone else's deposit. A custodian lends your coins to others and pays you a share. You now hold an unsecured claim on that company. Celsius and BlockFi paid depositor yields, then filed for bankruptcy in 2022. FTX's collapse the same year showed customer funds lent to an affiliate. Anchor promised about 19.5% on UST until Terra collapsed in May 2022.

Test any yield by asking who pays it and whether they can keep paying.

> **Key idea.** Every crypto yield is a transfer from a specific payer: borrowers, validators through dilution, new token buyers, or a custodian's risk-taking. Name the payer.

**Hard truth.** The sustainable sources pay roughly bond-like returns before smart-contract and custody risk, and with no capital they earn you nothing. The high advertised numbers are paid by new buyers or someone else's risk; when inflows stop, retail holders are last in line.

**What it changes for you.** Crypto staking and lending income is taxed as 雑所得 at progressive rates on top of your salary, up to about 55% combined at the top bracket; confirm the current rates with NTA.

**Check it yourself:** Calculate (1.40 × 0.45) − 1 for a 40% APY paid in a token that falls 55% over the year. Then pull a live protocol's token emission rate and fee revenue from a public dashboard and compare them.
### 3. Working capital and the cash conversion cycle
<sub>`financing` · Financing — *Where does capital come from, and what does it cost?*</sub>

The cash conversion cycle (CCC) counts the days cash is tied up in running a business: CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO. DIO (days inventory outstanding) is inventory ÷ cost of goods sold × 365: how long stock sits before it sells. DSO (days sales outstanding) is receivables ÷ revenue × 365: how long customers take to pay. DPO (days payables outstanding) is payables ÷ cost of goods sold × 365: how long you take to pay suppliers.

Worked example: revenue 10M JPY, cost of goods sold 6M, inventory 1.2M, receivables 1.5M, payables 0.9M. DIO is 73 days, DSO 55, DPO 55, so CCC is 73 days.

Each yen of cost is funded for about 73 days before the cash returns. The gap scales with volume. Every extra day of DSO on 10M JPY of revenue ties up about 27,000 JPY (10M ÷ 365). Double sales at the same days and you need twice the funding, even though every sale shows a profit on the income statement. Profit is booked at the sale; the cash arrives weeks or months later.

Japanese payment terms widen DSO. A month-end close with payment the following month averages about 45 days from sale to payment: sales spread across the month average 15 days to close, then 30 days to pay. That single term can dominate the cycle.

Shortening the cycle means collecting faster, holding less stock, or paying suppliers later. Each moves the funding burden onto a customer, a supplier, or your own balance sheet. Banks and supplier credit are the usual funders, and their terms are the real price. None of these is free.

> **Key idea.** Profit is booked at the sale, but cash is tied up until customers pay, so growth consumes cash.

**Hard truth.** Someone funds those days: a bank, a supplier's credit, or owners' savings. Profit does not close the gap. Growing faster than cash arrives is a way profitable firms fail. For you, it describes other firms' balance sheets, not income you can capture.

**What it changes for you.** You will not run working capital yourself, but computing a company's CCC from its filings shows whether reported profit is turning into cash.

**Check it yourself:** Download a listed Japanese company's annual securities report (有価証券報告書) from EDINET. Using year-end balances, compute its DIO, DSO and DPO with the formulas above, then its CCC. Check the current-year and prior-year figures and say whether the CCC rose or fell.

### Rogue Trader
<sub>James Dearden · 1999 · `markets` · film</sub>

The film dramatises a single trader in Singapore who, after taking on both trading and settlement oversight, used an error account to park losses from short Nikkei options and futures positions instead of reporting them. The mechanism is that concealment does not stop a loss, it converts a reportable loss into a compounding one that the bank's capital cannot absorb.
