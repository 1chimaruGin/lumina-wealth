### 1. The half-life of an edge
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

When a trading edge is published—whether in academic research, a book, a financial blog, or Twitter—a countdown begins. The edge starts decaying the moment it becomes known.

This decay follows a rough half-life. Simple edges (moving average crossovers, calendar anomalies like the "January effect") lose observable predictive power within 6–24 months of widespread publication. More complex edges—those requiring specific capital structures, proprietary data, or computational resources—last longer, sometimes 2–5 years. But they decay nonetheless.

The mechanism is straightforward: each trader who learns the edge and deploys capital to exploit it narrows the opportunity. Price moves against subsequent traders faster. Transaction costs consume a larger fraction of returns. Volatility drops. Bid-ask spreads tighten. Within a few years, the edge converges toward zero.

Real example: the "January effect"—the historical observation that stocks returned more in January than other months—was formally documented in academic research in the 1970s–1980s. Traders and funds built strategies around it. But by the 1990s, as the strategy became widely known and implemented, the January premium shrank. Statistical tests from the 2000s onward show the effect either absent or too small to trade profitably after transaction costs.

The decay rate depends on how easily the edge can be copied and how much capital can be deployed against it. A strategy that requires proprietary data or unusual market access decays slower. A strategy published in a book decays faster.

This has a cost: researchers and traders spend thousands of hours hunting published edges, backtesting them, and building systems to trade them—only to discover that by the time they finish, the opportunity has shrunk below the threshold of profitability. Time spent chasing published edges is time you cannot spend building something durable.

> **Key idea.** Published edges decay into noise within months to years as traders exploit them; time spent hunting them is opportunity cost with median payoff near zero.

**Hard truth.** If you read about an edge in a blog or book, capital has already been deployed. Without capital to front-run, you cannot profit before the edge closes. For you: chasing published edges costs evening hours you could spend learning or on side income that compounds.

### 2. Time horizon mismatch
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

A three-year withdrawal date and a 10-year equity allocation is a mismatch. The inverse—a 30-year horizon and a savings account paying 0.25% while inflation runs 2.5%—costs as much, just invisibly.

If you need the funds in two years and broad equities fall 40% in month 14, you must sell at loss. This scenario occurred in 2008 (recovery took until 2013) and March 2020 (recovery in months). For a two-year investor, a downturn near withdrawal is not temporary volatility; it is realized loss you cannot recover.

Volatility matters only relative to your timeline. A broad equity index down 35% today typically recovers in four to seven years—history supports this. If you sell in year two, you lock in the loss. If you sell in year eight, you likely see gain. Volatility stays constant; your timeline determines whether it ends in loss or gain.

Most portfolios fail here: people invest "long-term" without stating when they withdraw. They panic-sell when the market drops because they realize mid-crisis that they actually need money in three years.

Inverse failure: Japanese savings at 0.25% APY with a five-year horizon. No crash risk, no loss scenario—but 2.5% inflation annually erodes 11–14% of real purchasing power over five years. No volatility, no gain, just slow purchasing-power loss.

### 3. Market makers and payment for order flow
<sub>`markets` · Markets & Trading — *Who is on the other side, and why do they expect to win?*</sub>

A commission-free trade is paid for by the spread and by where your order goes. Every quote has a bid, the price buyers pay, and an ask, the price sellers accept. A market maker quotes both sides, buys at the bid, sells at the ask, and keeps the gap. Its edge depends on who it trades against. A typical retail order is uninformed: the customer is not reacting to information the market maker lacks. Filling that flow is cheap and safe enough that a market maker can afford to pay for it.

Payment for order flow (PFOF) is the arrangement where a broker routes your order to one market maker, which pays the broker for the right to fill it. In US equities the payments have been fractions of a cent per share. When major US brokers cut commissions to zero in October 2019, PFOF and interest on customer cash became core revenue. Robinhood's PFOF revenue was close to a billion dollars in 2021; treat that as approximate and check its filings.

Your fill can still be good. Brokers must seek best execution, and US Rules 605 and 606 require public reports on execution quality and routing. Those reports show that price improvement is usually small per share. They do not show what a different venue would have paid you, so the disclosure answers less than it appears to.

Japan differs in detail. Listed domestic stocks trade on the Tokyo Stock Exchange or on proprietary trading systems, while retail FX is typically a dealer quote where the dealer is your counterparty and the spread is its revenue. The principle is the same: a firm prices your flow, and your cost is the spread plus any payment you never see.

> **Key idea.** Commission-free means the cost moved into the spread and the routing, paid to whoever takes the other side of your order.

**Hard truth.** Retail flow is valuable to dealers because it is uninformed, and frequent traders underperform. Barber and Odean found the highest-turnover US households earned about 11% a year against 18% for the market. Brazilian day traders who persisted mostly lost money.

**What it changes for you.** For you, the spread and routing are the real fee on every trade, so compare total execution cost rather than the commission line when choosing a broker.

### Misbehaving
<sub>Richard Thaler · 2015 · `psychology` · book</sub>

The book argues that standard economics models people as rational, self-interested and unbiased 'Econs', and that documented departures such as mental accounting, the endowment effect, sunk costs and present bias are systematic and predictable rather than random noise. Applied to money, it claims people sort cash into separate mental accounts, so saving depends heavily on defaults and commitment devices rather than on stated intentions. Its finance material holds that markets are roughly efficient but that 'limits to arbitrage' let mispricings persist, so spotting a bias does not reliably mean you can trade on it.

> **The one idea.** Money is not fungible in people's heads: mental accounts, defaults and self-control shape savings and spending, and mispricings persist because exploiting them is costly and risky.
