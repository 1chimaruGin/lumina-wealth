### 1. What you can actually control
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

Three factors determine long-term wealth: contribution size, how long you stay invested, and what you pay in fees and taxes. Return rates do not belong on this list. Markets move on geopolitics, central bank policy, and earnings surprises—none of which you control—and historical data shows that trying to time them costs more than staying put.

The math is merciless. A portfolio earning 6% annually with 0.5% costs (fees plus taxes plus spreads) nets 5.5% actual return to you. One with 2% costs nets 4%. Over 20 years, contributing ¥100,000 annually: at 5.5% you accumulate ¥3.8 million; at 4% you reach ¥2.9 million. Cost drag alone erased ¥900,000. That money came straight from factors you could have controlled.

Now flip the scenario: same 6% market return, same 0.5% costs, but ¥150,000 annual contribution instead of ¥100,000. Final result: ¥5.7 million. You added ¥10 million in contributions; your wealth is ¥5.7 million higher. Contribution rate moved the outcome more than market performance did.

The four levers you own: contribution amount (how much each month), contribution frequency (regularity), holding period (how long before you touch it), and cost discipline (choosing a 0.3% annual fee vehicle instead of 1.5%). Return rates live outside your fence.

Right now you have no capital to deploy, so this is pure framework. When spare yen arrive, the framework only matters if you have internalized what actually drives outcomes. Most investors chase return predictions while ignoring cost cuts of 1–2% annually and contribution discipline they control completely. That gap costs millions over a career.

> **Key idea.** Wealth grows on contribution rate and cost discipline, not market timing. Three levers are yours; return rates are market noise.

**Hard truth.** You cannot control market returns. You can control costs (shave 1–2% annually) and contribution discipline. Most investors optimize the wrong lever. With zero capital now, this is framework-building for when stakes appear.

**What it changes for you.** Your future wealth depends more on controlling costs and contributions than on predicting stock direction or market timing.

**Check it yourself:** Find the annual expense ratio (%) of a TOPIX-tracking ETF or S&P 500 ETF available through a Tokyo broker. Multiply by ¥1 million to calculate your annual fee cost in yen.
### 2. Lifestyle creep
<sub>`psychology` · Psychology — *How do I behave with money?*</sub>

Lifestyle creep is arithmetic, not character. A raise adds income, and a cluster of costs rises to meet it: a larger flat, a car lease, a better phone plan, more dinners out, another subscription. Most of these are sticky. Once signed or habitual, they are hard to reverse, so your baseline spending ratchets up toward your new income. What builds wealth is your savings rate, the share of income you do not spend. A raise changes your wealth only if it changes that share.

Worked example. Suppose a raise adds ¥100,000 a month after tax, and within six months ¥100,000 a month of new commitments appear. Your savings are unchanged, and you have a bigger life on the same surplus. Income growth alone produces nothing you can invest. The increase only reaches your portfolio if it is routed before it is spent.

The habit that stops this is dull. On payday, move a fixed amount to a separate account, then spend only what remains. Fix the savings figure, not a spending limit. Limits are renegotiated every month, and each renegotiation favours the new, higher baseline. A transfer that happens automatically is not a decision you have to win again. Nothing about this requires willpower on the day, which is the point.

Returns matter less than most people assume at this stage. At an assumed 5% annual return, ¥1,000 a month for ten years grows to roughly ¥155,000, against ¥120,000 actually contributed. The 5% is an assumption, not a forecast. Most of the outcome comes from how much you save, not from what the savings earn.

> **Key idea.** A raise only builds wealth if the share of income you save rises with it; otherwise your costs absorb the extra.

**Hard truth.** Automatic transfers remove a decision; they don't stop you spending what remains. Bonuses are lump sums, easy to absorb unless routed first. With no capital, your savings rate is the whole game for years. No return fixes a rate that never moved.

**What it changes for you.** Bonuses and annual pay rises here land on a predictable schedule, so set the transfer on the payment date, before new spending absorbs the increase.

### 3. Ethereum and smart contracts
<sub>`crypto` · Crypto — *What is actually happening, underneath the price?*</sub>

Ethereum is a shared ledger where anyone can deploy a program, called a smart contract, that holds balances and executes rules without a bank or operator. Every node runs the same code, so a transaction's outcome is whatever the code says. The only trusted party is the network's consensus. Reading the source tells you what the code does, not whether it is safe.

Computation is metered in gas. Each operation costs a fixed number of gas units, and you pay units times a price, settled in ETH. Since the London upgrade in August 2021 (EIP-1559), part of each fee is burned and the base fee rises and falls with block demand. Since The Merge in September 2022, the chain is secured by validators who lock 32 ETH each, earning issuance and fees and losing stake for provable misbehaviour.

Tokens are contracts too. An ERC-20 token is essentially a mapping from addresses to balances plus transfer rules. Each transfer is a state change recorded by the chain, with no intermediary to call when something goes wrong. Stablecoins, lending pools and decentralised exchanges are also contracts, so one bug can move real value in seconds.

The catch is that the rules are public and automatic but often immutable. In June 2016 an attacker drained roughly 3.6 million ETH from The DAO through a reentrancy flaw: the contract sent funds before updating its balance, so the attacker's callback withdrew again and again. The community hard-forked to return the funds, while holdouts kept the original chain as Ethereum Classic. 'Code is law' lost in practice; humans edited the ledger.

> **Key idea.** A smart contract is a public program that moves value by its code, so its bugs and its governance are the real risk to understand.

**Hard truth.** Ethereum pays you nothing for using it; yield comes from fees paid by other users, new issuance, or speculation. Validators need 32 ETH. Smart-contract losses usually fall on depositors, with no insurer or court to reverse them.

**What it changes for you.** Your backend skills transfer to reading contracts, but ETH you hold is taxed as 雑所得 (progressive), not the flat 20.315% used for listed shares; confirm with NTA.

**Check it yourself:** Open any recent ETH transfer on etherscan.io. Record gas used and the effective gas price, multiply them, and convert the result to yen at that day's rate. Then find the 'Burnt Fees' line and state what share of the total fee was burned.
