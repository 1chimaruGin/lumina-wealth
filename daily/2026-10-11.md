### 1. Seasonality and calendar effects
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

"Sell in May and go away" is the oldest calendar pattern in markets: historical data from UK and US markets show May–September typically underperforming other months. January is supposed to see abnormal returns. These patterns are real in historical datasets. They are almost entirely absent in new data.

How they worked: traders noticed May–September had lower average returns across 100+ years of data. Some posted the pattern, argued it had economic explanations (tax-loss harvesting, fund manager holidays). The pattern encouraged people to sell in May and buy back in October.

Why they vanished: once published, any exploitable pattern gets arbitraged away. If many traders buy in October and sell in May, those trades become crowded. October buying pressure drives prices up earlier. May selling comes sooner. The seasonal pattern erodes. Schwert (2003) documented this decay: publicized anomalies weaken or disappear within a year of publication.

The numbers: over the S&P 500 from 1990–2025, May does show slightly lower average returns than June–October, but the difference is roughly 1–2% annualized and sits entirely within noise. January averages 1.8% since 1950, but so do several other months. Out-of-sample, these effects do not reliably predict future returns.

Why they persist in conversation: data-mining bias is powerful. Given enough historical data and enough calendar combinations, you will find patterns by accident. Testing the same data that generated the hypothesis biases results upward. True out-of-sample tests—applying a rule discovered in 1950–2000 data to 2000–2025 data—show these effects collapse.

The cost: even if a 1% edge existed, transaction costs in Japan (20.315% tax on listed equity gains, plus brokerage fees and bid-ask spread) would erase it instantly. Your actual return from "sell in May" would be negative.

> **Key idea.** Calendar patterns exist in old data but vanish under forward testing; trading costs make them unprofitable even if they were real.

**Hard truth.** Effect magnitude is 1–2% in-sample, zero out-of-sample. Arbitrageurs and trading costs destroy any remaining edge. For someone without capital, this is irrelevant—you cannot trade to generate returns.

**What it changes for you.** Calendar trading is noise. With no capital, studying seasonal patterns wastes time better spent understanding what actually builds wealth.

**Check it yourself:** Compute S&P 500 average May return minus other-month average (2015–2025). Subtract: brokerage, spread, and 20.315% Japanese tax on listed gains (confirm current rate with NTA). Is edge positive?
### 2. Dilution and cap tables
<sub>`financing` · Financing — *Where does capital come from, and what does it cost?*</sub>

Ownership is your share count divided by total shares issued. A funding round creates new shares and sells them to investors. Your count stays fixed and the total grows, so your percentage falls. The rule: stake after = stake before × (1 − new money ÷ post-money valuation).

Worked example. Two founders hold 10,000,000 shares, 100%. Seed round: investors pay 100M JPY at a 400M JPY pre-money valuation. Post-money is 500M JPY, so investors take 20% and the founders drop to 80%.

Option pool: investors usually require an employee option pool, here 10% of post-money, created before they invest. Because it sits inside the pre-money, the founders bear all of it. After the seed: founders 70%, pool 10%, seed investors 20%.

Series A: 200M JPY at 800M JPY pre-money, so 1,000M post. The new investor takes 20%, and everyone else is multiplied by 0.8. Founders: 70% × 0.8 = 56%. Pool 8%, seed 16%, Series A 20%.

On paper, 56% of 1,000M JPY is 560M JPY. Value rose while ownership fell. That is the core mechanic: dilution only costs you relative to a valuation that eventually turns into cash.

Preference terms change the arithmetic. Investors typically hold preferred shares with a liquidation preference, often 1x, so in a sale they recover their money before common holders. Percentage ownership is not the same as proceeds. Japanese companies use 種類株 for preferred shares and 新株予約権 for options; the dilution maths is identical.

Dilution compounds. Three rounds that each sell 20% leave a founder with 0.8³ = 51.2% of the stake they started with.

> **Key idea.** Ownership is a fraction of a pie that grows; each round sells new shares, so your percentage falls even when your stake's yen value rises.

**Hard truth.** Most early-stage companies never reach a priced later round. In modest exits, preferred liquidation terms pay investors first, so a 10% common or option stake can be worth very little.

**What it changes for you.** Before valuing a startup stock option grant, ask for the pool size and latest post-money valuation, then compute your percentage after the next round.

**Check it yourself:** Open a Japanese listed company's latest 有価証券報告書 on EDINET, find 発行済株式総数 for two consecutive years, and compute the percentage change. Then check whether any 新株発行 or 新株予約権 explains the difference.
### 3. Compound interest and the rule of 72
<sub>`intelligence` · Intelligence — *How does money actually work?*</sub>

Compounding means each year's growth is earned on the whole balance, including last year's growth. The rule of 72 estimates doubling time: divide 72 by the annual rate in percent. At 6%, money doubles in about 12 years (exact figure: 11.9). At 8%, about 9 years. The estimate is accurate between roughly 5% and 12%; at 1% it says 72 years when the true answer is 69.7. Doubling time is the fastest check you can run on any growth claim.

Year-by-year gains show the shape. Assume 1,000,000 yen invested at 7% a year. Year 1 adds 70,000 yen, year 10 about 128,700, year 20 about 253,200, year 30 about 498,000. The first decade of a 30-year run adds roughly 967,000 yen in total; the last decade adds roughly 3,743,000, about 57% of all growth. Each gain is 7% of a balance that keeps getting bigger, so the early years look flat only because the base is small.

The rate matters more than intuition suggests. Over 30 years, 6% turns 1,000,000 yen into about 5.74 million; 7% turns it into about 7.61 million. One percentage point is worth roughly 1.9 million yen from the same starting sum. The formula is balance times (1 + rate) to the power of years, so every extra point compounds too. Quoted returns before fees and tax overstate what you keep.

Compounding creates no return of its own. It only reinvests what the asset pays: interest from a borrower, earnings from a company, or a higher price from the next buyer. The 7% here is an illustration. Long-run studies of developed-market stocks have found real (after-inflation) returns in the mid-single digits, approximately, and they vary by decade.

> **Key idea.** Compounding's output depends on rate and time together, and most of the result arrives in the final years, not the early ones.

**Hard truth.** Fees and tax come off the rate every year, and you don't set it. Japan's 20.315% on listed-share gains outside NISA is one drain (confirm with NTA). Ordinary deposits have mostly paid well under 1%, so doubling takes decades or longer. With zero capital, nothing compounds.

**What it changes for you.** With no savings invested, this rule has no yen to work on yet; its use for you is checking what fees and taxes do to any future balance.

**Check it yourself:** Compute 72 divided by 6, and ln(2) divided by ln(1.06). Repeat for 1%. Which gap between the two methods is bigger, and by how many years?
