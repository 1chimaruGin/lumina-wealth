### 1. Illiquidity as a feature
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

The average equity investor realizes 10–15% annual losses through panic selling—exiting at market bottoms and missing recoveries. Illiquidity prevents this through mechanics, not willpower. If your money sits in a 企業年金 (corporate pension) with no withdrawal until age 60, you cannot sell when panic hits. You are forced to hold through the crash.

The global equity market declined approximately 57% from peak to trough during the 2007–2009 financial crisis. Retail investors who panic-sold near the bottom in March 2009 locked in losses. By 2013, the market had recovered; by 2020, it was up roughly 250% from the bottom. An investor structurally unable to access funds—trapped by illiquidity—captured that entire recovery. One who could sell chose to exit and never recovered.

The return from illiquidity is the return from NOT making that choice. For Japanese savers, retirement accounts in 企業年金 or iDeCo (locked until age 60) create that friction. Research suggests retail accounts in liquid instruments turn over at 70–100% annually, while locked pension accounts typically turn over at 5–15% annually—roughly a 5–10x difference in trading frequency. Each extra trade introduces timing risk and transaction costs. The locked account's forced hold is an edge.

But illiquidity requires capital to lock up. You cannot benefit from an inability to access money you do not have. This is wealth protection for those who already saved, not a tool for building wealth from zero.

The mechanism is real: forced holding prevents catastrophic market-timing errors. But illiquidity costs access; if you need funds before expiration, you pay penalties. This is only a feature if stable income and time horizons align with the lock.

> **Key idea.** Forced inability to access money prevents panic selling; that structural discipline is a measurable return most investors do not count.

**Hard truth.** Illiquidity only protects those with existing capital who hold through crashes. With zero spare yen, you cannot lock anything away. This advantage is for wealth already accumulated, not for earning a first yen.

**What it changes for you.** Illiquidity's math matters when you save capital—but right now, with zero spare yen, this is not your constraint. Understanding it means choosing the right vehicle when savings accumulate.

**Check it yourself:** Find the published annual turnover rate for a Japanese corporate pension fund (企業年金) in FSA data and for a Japanese retail stock trading account. Subtract. That percentage-point difference is the structural reduction in panic-sell opportunities between the two.
### 2. Anchoring
<sub>`psychology` · Psychology — *How do I behave with money?*</sub>

The first number named in a negotiation anchors acceptable outcomes. In salary talks, whoever goes first shapes the range; studies of negotiators show the final agreement typically falls closer to the first number proposed than basic logic would predict.

If an employer offers 5M yen, you counter at 5.5M, leaving you adjusting from their baseline. If you name 6M first, they anchor there instead. This advantage belongs to whoever has alternatives—a backup offer, a different job lined up, or simply the ability to walk away. If you're competing hard for a position, the employer names the first number. You adjust upward because this is your best, sometimes only, realistic option.

In any job market, whoever has alternatives gets to anchor first. Early-career engineers typically don't have alternatives, so they name their ask after hearing the employer's figure. The same dynamic holds in freelance work. When a client asks "what do you charge?" before you've named a rate, you're anchoring downward from their implicit range, not upward from your own number. Without capital or reputation, you lack leverage to speak first.

Understanding anchoring doesn't change your position. You'll watch yourself adjust from numbers you didn't set. The person with options—the employer, the established client—speaks first. They can walk away; you need the work. That asymmetry is why they anchor and you don't.

But understanding anchoring explains why salary transparency matters. Publishing rates removes the first-mover advantage. It also explains why email negotiations (where you see all offers simultaneously) feel different from sequential conversations where numbers are named one at a time. When everyone sees the same figures, anchoring power flattens.

> **Key idea.** The first number spoken in a negotiation constrains the outcome; whoever can walk away gets to speak first.

**Hard truth.** As a junior engineer without capital, you'll almost always adjust from others' numbers—not because you lack skill, but because you need the work and they don't need you. This power imbalance is why understanding anchoring doesn't fix your position.

**What it changes for you.** As a salaried engineer without capital, understanding why you'll anchor last explains what you're actually building toward—the alternatives that let you anchor first.

**Check it yourself:** In your next three salary or rate conversations, track which number gets named first and what the final number is. Compare: is the final figure consistently closer to the first anchor or to your counter-offer?
### 3. Custody: not your keys, not your coins
<sub>`crypto` · Crypto — *What is actually happening, underneath the price?*</sub>

Self-custody means you hold the private cryptographic key that authorizes all transactions from an address. When you own that key, only you can move the money. When you do not own it—when it's held by an exchange, a custodian, or a bank—they control the money, and you have a claim against them.

The appeal is clear: no intermediary, no counterparty risk, money that moves only with your permission. The reality is operational: you become responsible for every aspect of the infrastructure.

Cryptocurrency losses from custody failures run into billions annually—lost or compromised private keys contribute substantially to total crypto losses. Exact figures remain unclear because many losses are never disclosed.

The failure modes are mundane. Forgotten seed phrases (the mnemonic backup of your key). Hardware wallets failing without readable backups. Typos in wallet addresses, sending coins to inaccessible addresses forever. Malware or phishing stealing keys from devices where they were first generated. Fires or water damage destroying physical backups. A spouse finding a seed phrase after death, with no way to verify its validity.

Here is the hard part: there is no recovery mechanism. No freeze, no reversal, no password reset, no insurance. A lost or stolen key is gone. Permanently.

The operational burden is substantial. Safe key generation requires an air-gapped machine or at minimum a clean device. Backups must be stored in multiple locations but kept secure—a contradiction in practice. Verification that coins arrived at the correct address requires reading a blockchain yourself. Using a key means exposing it on a device that might be compromised.

For someone with limited capital, the risk is catastrophic. A single operational mistake—a malware infection, a typo, a destroyed backup—means total loss of whatever you have invested.

Most people without professional security infrastructure should not self-custody significant amounts.

> **Key idea.** Self-custody means you become your own bank, securing everything yourself; a single operational mistake is permanent and irreversible.

**Hard truth.** Securing this yourself requires infrastructure and discipline most people lack. A single mistake—forgotten password, phishing, typo—means permanent loss. If your entire net worth fits in one wallet, you cannot afford self-custody. Most cryptocurrency losses come from operator error, not theft.

**What it changes for you.** With limited capital, a single custody mistake costs you everything your yen has bought. You have zero margin for operational error.

**Check it yourself:** On a Bitcoin or Ethereum block explorer (blockchain.com, etherscan.io), locate a wallet address that received funds more than five years ago but has never initiated a transaction. Verify the current balance. Confirm whether any recovery mechanism exists if the private key is lost.
