### 1. The planning fallacy applied to wealth
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

The planning fallacy: you estimate tasks take less time than they actually do. Applied to wealth, you think five years gets you to serious money. The outside view—people who actually accumulated it—shows something different.

Here's the mechanism. A Tokyo engineer earning 6 million yen annually, saving 2 million per year, reaches 10 million in five years with zero returns. But 10 million invested doesn't double every five years. If real returns (adjusted for inflation and taxes) average 2–3% annually, as Japanese equity returns have roughly shown, then 10 million becomes approximately 11.3 million in five more years. You planned five years; it's now ten, and you've reached only 11.3 million.

Push further: reaching 50 million takes roughly 20–25 years at this savings rate and return. The calculation is math, not optimism. Yet most individual plans assume it happens in 7–10 years.

The outside-view correction comes from tracking actual wealth builders. People who accumulated significant capital reported their actual timelines were 1.5 to 3 times longer than initial plans. This is consistent across professions and countries. Wealth accumulation surveys show the same pattern: substantial sums take decades, not years.

What waiting costs: inflation. A yen earned and invested today beats one earned and invested five years from now, because prices have risen. The longer your timeline stretches, the more inflation erodes your target. Delay three years, and you've forfeited compound growth on three years of savings, plus you're now saving toward a larger nominal number.

What it buys: realistic strategy choices now. Do you increase income faster? Accept a longer timeline? Stop pursuing this and invest time in other returns? Most people avoid this decision and drift instead, wondering why they're behind.

> **Key idea.** Most people plan for five years when outside-view data shows twenty is typical. This gap is the real planning fallacy.

**Hard truth.** Your timeline is 20+ years at aggressive savings rates. Inflation reduces this value constantly. People who succeeded either earned significantly more or accepted decades-long horizons. Most shortcuts failed.

**What it changes for you.** A 20-year timeline contradicts your preference for fast feedback. This gap demands a strategy choice now: start with discipline, pursue higher income first, or abandon the goal.

**Check it yourself:** Calculate how much you'd accumulate by age 45: (annual savings after expenses) × 20 years, plus 2.5% annual compounding. Does this match your wealth goal? If not, the timeline is longer.
### 2. Dollar cost averaging as patience technology
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

DCA—dollar cost averaging—means investing a fixed sum at fixed intervals, regardless of price. Invest 50,000 yen every month; the index rises or falls, but your calendar doesn't care. You buy fewer shares when prices are high, more when prices are low. Your average cost per share ends up lower than the average price you observe.

Mathematically, this is suboptimal. Vanguard studied seven decades of US equity returns: lump-sum investing (putting all your capital in on day one) outperformed DCA roughly two-thirds of the time. Markets rise more often than they fall, so deploying capital sooner typically wins.

Why does DCA exist? Because it solves a psychological problem. It removes the timing decision from you. The debit is automatic; the anxiety of "is now the right time?" disappears. You're not watching a lump sum evaporate 30% in month two. The discipline happens without willpower. For people with steady income and years of patience, this psychological support has real value—comfort can be worth some basis points of returns.

But this technology is built for someone different than you. You've stated plainly that long-term motivation doesn't work for you, that you drop projects without short feedback loops, that you chase new ideas. DCA requires the opposite: commit for decades, tolerate months of zero visible progress, stay disciplined without external wins.

There is also a capital constraint. DCA assumes you have spare income to deploy. You have zero yen of spare capital. DCA doesn't help you build capital—it assumes you've already solved that problem. You cannot average down on nothing.

Finally, there is an invisible cost: money sitting in a checking account waiting for next month's deployment earns zero. In rising markets, that's a real cost you pay for psychological comfort. You don't get to avoid the timing question; you just push it back thirty days, and the market moved.

> **Key idea.** Lump-sum investing outperforms DCA about two-thirds of the time; you pay for comfort with returns.

**Hard truth.** DCA is suboptimal mathematically (lump-sum wins roughly two-thirds of the time) and behaviorally wrong for you (you abandon multi-month commitments without short feedback). Money in checking waiting for deployment earns zero.

**What it changes for you.** Since you have no spare capital and drop multi-month commitments without fast payoff, DCA is premature; build capital first.

### 3. Credit and what it costs
<sub>`management` · Management — *How do I run the money I have?*</sub>

Credit is priced as an annual rate, but interest accrues monthly on whatever you still owe. Japanese card revolving payments (リボ払い) often carry around 15–18% a year, though the exact figure depends on the product and your issuer's terms. Statutory limits set the ceiling: the Interest Rate Restriction Act caps rates at 15–18% depending on principal size, and the 出資法 sets an absolute maximum of 20%. Confirm current limits with the FSA, since they change.

Take 100,000 yen at 15% a year. The monthly rate is 1.25%, so the first month's interest is 1,250 yen. Repay 10,000 yen a month and the balance clears in about 11 months, costing roughly 7,500 yen in interest. Repay only 1,500 yen a month and, at the same rate, it takes about 12 years. Payment size decides how long the debt lasts; the rate decides what it costs each month.

Revolving debt compounds. Unpaid interest is typically added to the balance and charged interest in turn, which is why a payment that barely covers the monthly charge keeps the balance nearly flat. By contrast, a one-time payment (翌月一括払い) is usually interest-free, so the cost depends on the product, not on credit as such. Check which one you are actually using.

The comparison that matters is between repaying the balance and investing the cash. Interest you stop paying is not taxable income, while gains on listed Japanese shares are taxed at a flat 20.315% (confirm the current rule with the NTA). So a 15% card balance is a guaranteed, untaxed return on repayment, and no ordinary investment offers that with certainty. The debt question comes first in any sensible ordering of decisions.

> **Key idea.** A balance you carry costs its interest rate on the outstanding amount, compounded, so the cheapest credit is the balance you clear each month.

**Hard truth.** Issuers profit most from partial payers, so these products are built for people who carry balances. At 15%, the cost is certain, and no major asset class has reliably beaten that after costs over long periods.

**What it changes for you.** Any card balance you carry is a fixed, avoidable cost, so it is the first line to check against your monthly surplus before you consider investing.

**Check it yourself:** Find the revolving-payment 実質年率 in the published terms of any major Japanese card issuer, then compute one month's interest on a 100,000 yen balance (rate × 100,000 ÷ 12). Check whether your result matches the issuer's own worked example.

### Influence
<sub>Robert Cialdini · 1984 · `psychology` · book</sub>

Cialdini argues that people rely on a few automatic shortcuts when deciding whether to say yes: reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, liking, and scarcity. These shortcuts respond to cues (a free gift, a crowd, a title, a deadline) rather than to the merits of the request. He supports this with laboratory experiments and his own undercover observation of sales, fundraising, and advertising work, and presents each principle as a lever that persuaders routinely pull.

> **The one idea.** A cue that says others, experts, or scarcity already vouch for something can produce yes before you have evaluated the offer. Ask what the cue is standing in for.

**Verdict.** Worth reading the six principle chapters once, because it is short and the core is clear: it explains pricing pages, fundraising appeals, and deal pressure in psychological terms. For your money-theory track it is a psychology of purchase decisions, not economics, so it says nothing about markets, prices, or investing…

**Caveat.** Mostly small lab studies and anecdotes from undercover sales work; replication has shrunk some classic effects. Popular reading treats it as a sales playbook, but its own lesson is defensive. Marketing examples predate online dark patterns and scarcity timers. Written from the persuader's side.
