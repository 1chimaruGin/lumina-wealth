### 1. Unit economics
<sub>`financing` · Financing — *Where does capital come from, and what does it cost?*</sub>

Three numbers decide whether a customer-acquisition business can work. CAC is total sales and marketing spend in a period divided by new customers won in that period. LTV is the gross profit one customer generates before leaving. For a subscription, that is roughly monthly price times gross margin, divided by monthly churn. Payback period is CAC divided by monthly gross profit: the months until a customer repays what it cost to win them.

Worked example, with invented inputs you can swap: price ¥2,000 a month, 80% gross margin, so ¥1,600 gross profit monthly. Churn of 5% a month implies an average life of 20 months, so LTV is ¥32,000. If CAC is ¥15,000, LTV:CAC is about 2.1 and payback is about 9.4 months.

Cash goes out first. The ¥15,000 leaves on day one and returns at ¥1,600 a month, so every customer is financed for about nine months, and that gap multiplies with each month's new customers.

Two rules of thumb circulate in SaaS: LTV:CAC of at least 3:1 and payback under 12 months. They are practitioner conventions, not laws, and work only as a first screen.

The formula is fragile. Halving churn doubles LTV, and churn is the input most often guessed, because a new product has few customers old enough to have left. Undiscounted LTV ignores that ¥32,000 arriving over twenty months is worth less today. CAC is usually measured blended across channels, which hides the one channel that actually works. Ad platforms price clicks in auctions, and the marginal customer usually costs more than the average one. Ask what each number is based on, and whose data it is.

> **Key idea.** Acquisition pays only if lifetime gross profit per customer exceeds acquisition cost, and payback is short enough to fund the next customer.

**Hard truth.** Most LTV figures rest on churn nobody has measured yet. Ad platforms sell the clicks, so you pay their price, not yours. With no capital, the payback gap is paid in your own time and savings. Most small attempts never measure LTV.

**What it changes for you.** Acquisition costs you cash before any yen returns, so with no spare capital, payback period decides viability before any ratio does.

**Check it yourself:** Find the monthly price of a subscription you could buy in Japan on its official pricing page. Assuming 60% gross margin and 5% monthly churn, compute its LTV, then the maximum CAC that still gives a 3:1 LTV:CAC ratio.
### 2. The yield curve
<sub>`intelligence` · Intelligence — *How does money actually work?*</sub>

The yield curve plots government bond interest rates against their maturity. Normally a 10-year bond yields more than a 3-month bill, because lenders want extra pay for locking money away. Traders also watch the 10-year against the 2-year, the spread most often quoted in headlines. When short rates rise above long rates, the curve is inverted. Short rates follow the central bank's policy rate and what markets expect it to do. A 10-year yield is roughly the average expected short rate over ten years plus a term premium, the extra compensation for uncertainty about future rates and inflation. So an inversion means bond buyers are pricing cuts ahead, which usually signals expected economic weakness and easing. The transmission is mechanical as well. Banks fund themselves at short rates and lend at long ones. When that spread goes near zero or negative, lending margins shrink and credit tightens. This is why the curve matters to bank share prices and credit conditions, not just to economists. The record: in the US, the 10-year minus 3-month spread, which the New York Fed uses in its recession probability model, has inverted before nearly every recession since the 1960s. Reported lead times run roughly 6 to 24 months, and 1966 is the standard false alarm. These figures are approximate; check the Fed's own papers. The model output is a probability, not a verdict. Japan works the same mechanically, but the BOJ kept short rates near zero for years and ended negative rates only in March 2024. The JGB curve therefore reflected policy choices as much as growth expectations, so read any Japanese curve signal with that distortion in mind.

> **Key idea.** An inverted yield curve means bond markets expect lower rates ahead, a probabilistic warning about the economy, not a dated forecast.

**Hard truth.** Roughly eight recessions is a tiny sample, so this is a probability, not a timer. Equities often kept rising for months after inversion. The signal is public and priced within minutes; profiting needs correct timing, which almost nobody demonstrates.

**What it changes for you.** For a Tokyo salaried engineer, the curve matters mostly through yen loan and deposit rates, which set mortgage and savings costs more directly than any trade could.

**Check it yourself:** On FRED, load series T10Y3M from 2022-01-01 to 2024-12-31, count the business days with a negative value, and note the first date it turned negative.
### 3. Gold and commodities produce nothing
<sub>`investing` · Investing — *What actually produces returns, and what only looks like it does?*</sub>

A share pays dividends and earns profit. A bond pays coupons. A rental pays rent. Gold pays nothing. Its return is the price someone pays you later, minus every cost you paid to hold it. The arithmetic is short: price change, minus dealer spread or fund fees, minus storage or insurance.

The history shows how little that adds up to. Gold was $35 an ounce when the US ended dollar convertibility in 1971. It peaked near $850 in January 1980 and did not regain that nominal high until about 2008. It then reached roughly $1,920 in September 2011, before falling about 45% by late 2015. Erb and Harvey's study of gold's long history put its real return over very long horizons close to zero, with large swings along the way.

Commodity futures add mechanics. A futures return is the spot price change, plus the roll yield from replacing expiring contracts, plus interest on the cash collateral. In contango, where later contracts cost more, each roll can lose money even when the spot price is flat. Index returns therefore depend on the shape of the futures curve, not only on the commodity's price.

Gold is a store of value, not a compounding asset. Nothing you hold grows on its own. Any gain comes from a later buyer paying more, so the return is set by someone else's demand, not by anything the asset produces. Gold also has no earnings to anchor a valuation, so its price moves with real interest rates, the dollar and sentiment. Those forces can stay unfavourable for decades. A yen-based holder also carries USD/JPY exposure, a second bet nobody asked for.

> **Key idea.** Without cash flow, an asset's return is only the next buyer's price, minus every cost you pay to hold it.

**Hard truth.** Retail spreads can run several percent each way, so you start behind. The other side is a miner hedging or a buyer hoping for a higher price. After costs, expected real return is roughly zero or negative, and nothing pays you while you wait.

**What it changes for you.** Yen-priced gold moves with USD/JPY as well as gold, and it pays you nothing while you wait, so it cannot fund anything in your 8-hour week.

**Check it yourself:** Look up the annual average USD gold price for 1980 and for the latest full year (World Gold Council or LBMA), plus the BLS CPI-U for both years. Compute gold's annualised inflation-adjusted return over that span.

### The Mom Test
<sub>Rob Fitzpatrick · 2013 · `financing` · book</sub>

The book argues that founders learn the wrong things from customer conversations because people are polite, so they offer encouraging words about an idea that do not reflect real demand. Its fix is a set of interviewing rules: talk about the customer's actual life and recent concrete episodes, avoid pitching or asking whether they would buy, and count only actions, time, money or firm next steps as evidence. It frames this as early-stage customer discovery, where a specific past behaviour or a real commitment is data and a polite 'that sounds great' is none.

> **The one idea.** Ask about specific past behaviour and what someone has already spent or done, never whether they would like your idea; compliments and hypotheticals are noise.

**Verdict.** The summary above is enough for most purposes. Read the book only if you are about to run customer interviews, and then read it once for the examples of weak versus strong questions. **Caveat.** Built for early software and consumer startups with identifiable buyers. It says little about markets where customers have no past behaviour to report, and recall of past behaviour is itself error-prone, a point survey researchers stress.
