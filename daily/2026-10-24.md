### 1. Overconfidence and the illusion of control
<sub>`psychology` · Psychology — *How do I behave with money?*</sub>

Svenson (1981) asked students in the US and Sweden to rate their own driving. Roughly 80 to 90 percent put themselves above the median on safety. For a large group that is impossible, because at most half can sit above the median. Money produces the same error. Barber and Odean (2000) tracked 66,465 US discount-brokerage households from 1991 to 1996. The market returned 17.9% a year. Households returned 11.4% net, and the most active traders did worst. Every trade pays a bid-ask spread and often a commission, and the trades themselves tended to be poorly timed.

The illusion of control comes from memory. Wins get credited to skill, losses to bad luck or a missed signal, and activity itself feels like control even when outcomes are random. In a 2001 follow-up, Barber and Odean found men traded about 45% more than women and earned roughly 2.65 percentage points less a year, which they attributed largely to overconfidence. Professionals show it too: over 15 years, more than 90% of US large-cap active funds trailed the S&P 500 in SPIVA's 2023 data (approximate; check the current report). A strategy must beat its costs before it beats anyone.

Overconfidence is the default output of a brain that rewards action and remembers hits. The corrective is measurement: log each decision, its stated reason and its outcome, then compare the whole record with a low-cost index over years, not weeks. Include fees, spreads and tax, because the comparison is only fair after them.

> **Key idea.** Feeling skilled at investing is not evidence of skill; frequent trading usually adds costs and poor timing, and the record decides, not confidence.

**Hard truth.** Your counterparty is usually a professional with faster data and lower costs. Most self-directed traders, with no capital to absorb mistakes, end up below a low-cost index fund. For you, the honest base case is that trading costs money.

**What it changes for you.** Engineering skill does not transfer to markets. Log any strategy against an index, net of fees and the 20.315% tax on listed-share gains (confirm at NTA).

**Check it yourself:** Open Barber and Odean's 2000 Journal of Finance paper, find the annual return table, and confirm the 17.9% market and 11.4% household figures. Then compute the yen gap: 1,000,000 yen compounded for 20 years at 17.9% versus at 11.4%.
### 2. AMMs and how a DEX prices things
<sub>`crypto` · Crypto — *What is actually happening, underneath the price?*</sub>

A constant-product AMM holds two token reserves, x and y, and keeps their product fixed: x × y = k. There is no order book. The price is the ratio y/x. Example: a pool holds 100 ETH and 200,000 USDC. So k = 20,000,000 and the price is 2,000 USDC per ETH. Buy 1 ETH. To keep k, the pool must end with 99 ETH and 20,000,000 ÷ 99 = 202,020.20 USDC. You pay 2,020.20 USDC, an average 1% above the quoted 2,000. Larger trades move the price further: buying 10 ETH costs 22,222.22 USDC, an average 11% above quote. Each swap also pays a fee into the pool; Uniswap v2 charged 0.3%, so k grows slowly as fees accrue. Liquidity providers deposit both tokens and collect those fees. Now suppose the outside market moves ETH to 4,000. Arbitrageurs trade against the pool until its ratio matches 4,000. The pool ends with about 70.71 ETH and 282,843 USDC, worth 565,685 USDC at 4,000. Holding the original 100 ETH and 200,000 USDC would be worth 600,000. The 5.7% gap is impermanent loss. It is not a fee. It is the pool selling the asset that rose and buying the one that fell. The loss depends only on the price ratio r: 2√r/(1+r) − 1. Direction does not matter, only the size of the move. A fourfold rise, 2,000 to 8,000, gives 20%. Fee income must exceed this loss, and it scales with trading volume relative to pool size, not with the price move. The pool's price comes from arbitrage, not from its own demand: whoever trades against the stale price first takes the difference.

> **Key idea.** An AMM sets price by keeping the product of reserves constant, so the pool's price comes from arbitrage, and liquidity providers pay for being tradable.

**Hard truth.** Fee income must beat impermanent loss, and analyses of Uniswap v2 pools have generally found that many liquidity providers earned less than simply holding the tokens; treat that as approximate. Arbitrageurs capture the gap from stale prices, while as a swapper you pay fees and slippage.

**What it changes for you.** For a salaried engineer with zero capital, this is a mechanism to understand, not a route to income: you cannot fund a position, and as a swapper you pay the pool's fee and slippage.

**Check it yourself:** Using the formula loss = 2√r/(1+r) − 1, where r is the new price divided by the old, confirm the 5.7% loss for a 2x move and compute the loss for a 4x move. Then find the swap fee tier for Uniswap v2 in its official documentation and confirm it is 0.3%.
### 3. Song dynasty paper money
<sub>`history` · History & Stories — *Who did this before, and what happened?*</sub>

Around 1000 AD, Sichuan had a shortage of copper cash. Iron coins were too heavy and bulky for regional trade, so merchants began holding deposits of coin and issuing paper receipts redeemable for it. That private scheme was called jiaozi (交子). By 1024 the Northern Song state had taken over issuance in Sichuan, and later in the century it centralised paper production.

The mechanism was simple. A note promised redemption in cash, but the state held only a partial reserve against the notes in circulation, not full backing. The arithmetic depended on the state not spending the paper, and it did. Notes were issued to fund military campaigns, including the wars against the Western Xia in the 1040s. When more paper circulated than the economy needed at the old prices, the notes lost value against copper, and merchants and officials began discounting them.

The state responded with redemption rules, exchange-rate controls and reissues at new face values. Its later paper, the huizi (會子), was issued on a much larger scale under the Southern Song, which faced pressure from the Jin and then the Mongols. By the late 1200s it had lost most of its value. The Yuan dynasty then issued inconvertible paper (寶鈔, chao) and over-issued that too. Its collapse is commonly cited as one contributor to the unrest before the dynasty fell in 1368.

The pattern is that the issuer gets spending power first and holders pay the loss later. Historians call the issuer's gain seigniorage.

> **Key idea.** Paper money is a claim whose value depends on how restrained its issuer stays, and issuers gain first from over-issuing.

**Hard truth.** The gain went to the issuer and the loss fell on holders. You cannot be the issuer. Holding yen cash is also a bet on the BOJ: at its 2% target, purchasing power halves in roughly 35 years. Most paper experiments ended in inflation or redenomination.

**What it changes for you.** Your yen salary and savings are claims on how the BOJ manages the currency, so its issuance decisions set what you hold, not just the interest you see.

**Check it yourself:** Compute ln(2)/ln(1.02) to find how many years 2% annual inflation takes to halve purchasing power. Then confirm the BOJ's current inflation target on boj.or.jp.

### Enron: The Smartest Guys in the Room
<sub>Alex Gibney · 2005 · `financing` · documentary</sub>

Enron let its traders book the estimated value of multi-year energy contracts as profit when they were signed, using internal price forecasts rather than observable market prices.
