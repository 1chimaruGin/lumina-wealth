### 1. Mental accounting
<sub>`psychology` · Psychology — *How do I behave with money?*</sub>

Mental accounting is the tendency to treat money differently depending on its source, intended use, or how you acquired it. Richard Thaler formalized this in the 1980s through experiments showing that the origin of money shapes spending decisions far more than rational economics predicts. A ¥5,000 bonus is rarely used like ¥5,000 from your regular salary; a ¥2,000 bill found on the street enters a different mental category than ¥2,000 you earned through work.

This happens because your brain assigns money to separate "accounts" even though money is fungible—one yen is one yen, regardless of source. Someone who refuses to spend ¥50,000 from savings on a meal will happily do so from a bonus. A tax refund disappears on discretionary purchases while an equivalent paycheck increase gets saved. The "jar problem" captures this: putting money in jars labeled "rent," "groceries," or "emergency" makes the same money feel constrained or available depending on which container it occupies.

The mechanism is psychological. Your brain creates spending rules for each mental account—a savings "jar" feels protected from casual withdrawal, while found money feels like license to spend. This is partly protective: mental accounts prevent you from rationalizing away your entire emergency fund. But they also create economic inefficiency. You might leave money in a low-interest account labeled "vacation" while paying credit card interest on "general" spending, even though moving money between accounts costs nothing and currency is identical.

For side income specifically, mental accounting cuts both ways. If you mentally segregate earnings from freelance work, consulting, or side projects as "growth money," you're more likely to reinvest them. But you might also treat side income as "found money" and spend it reflexively, when reinvestment would accelerate your goal. The critical question is not whether you earn side income—it's which mental account you place it in.

> **Key idea.** People assign money to separate mental accounts by source and use, creating spending rules that override fungibility—the core mechanism behind why bonuses feel different from salary.

**Why it matters to you.** Whether your side income compounds or disappears depends on which mental account it enters—growth reinvestment or discretionary spending—especially when starting with zero capital.

**Ask yourself:** Your present_bias preference for immediate wins will test your first side income. Will you mentally treat it as 'fun money to spend now' or 'capital to reinvest'?

### 2. Cost of capital
<sub>`financing` · Financing — *Where does capital come from, and what does it cost?*</sub>

The concept of "cost of capital" is that money is never free—even your own. When a venture capitalist invests $100,000 in a startup, they expect roughly 30% annual returns. Why not 10% or 50%? Because they're pricing what that capital could earn elsewhere: perhaps 5% in bonds or 8% in index funds. The difference—the spread between what you got and what you could have gotten—is the true cost of deploying capital to this opportunity instead of that one.

This applies directly to you, even without borrowing. When you invest three weeks of evenings building a product, you're not spending yen, but you're spending time with market value. At your backend skill level, freelance work pays roughly ¥2,500 to ¥3,500 per hour. Three weeks at 20 hours weekly is 60 hours—worth ¥150,000 to ¥210,000 in foregone earnings. That's your implicit capital cost. If that product generates zero income for three months, you've burned ¥150,000+ in opportunity. This explains your pattern of abandoning unfunded projects: you're not impatient, you're calculating cost correctly.

Why does capital cost anything? Because money compounds over time. A yen today can earn interest, deploy to a venture, or sit in bonds. A yen next year is worth less. A venture capitalist quoting 30% required return isn't greedy—they're pricing what capital costs to tie up: its earnings elsewhere, plus a risk premium for total loss. You see that number; you're reading the market's statement of what capital costs right now.

Inflation adds another layer. Japan's inflation averaged 2–3% annually since 2023—modest globally but real locally. Savings lose purchasing power; borrowed money nominally costs less, but real cost adjusted for inflation remains substantial.

The practical takeaway: don't feel guilty for refusing projects with slow payoff. A project demanding 60 hours with no income for six months costs you ¥150,000+ in opportunity. Reject it. But one that nets ¥150,000 in two months? That cost is justified. You're not scattered; you're allocating scarce resources to the highest-return stream, which your focused pattern demands.

> **Key idea.** Capital always has a cost: the return you forfeit by deploying it here instead of there.

**Why it matters to you.** Without capital, your scarcest resource is time. Pricing its opportunity cost in yen tells you which side-income idea to chase first—and which to skip entirely.

**Ask yourself:** In your last abandoned project (your scattered_focus pattern): did opportunity cost—the yen-value of hours you could have freelanced—actually determine your decision to quit, or was the reason something else?
