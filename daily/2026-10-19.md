### 1. Boredom as a signal
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

The average investor underperforms the broad market by approximately 3-4 percentage points annually. Dalbar Inc., which has tracked this since 1984, found the gap comes almost entirely from trading too much and at the wrong times. When markets rise, people buy in. When they fall, they sell. The mechanical cost is significant: a 7% annual return can become 4-5% after you trade 12 times instead of once. Transaction costs (brokerage, spread, slippage) typically run 0.1-0.3% per trade on listed Japanese equities; multiply by frequent buying and selling and you erode 1-2 percentage points just in fees.

The psychological mechanism is simpler. A boring strategy—buy a low-cost index, hold it, rebalance once a year—feels like nothing is working because you see no movement, no wins, no active validation. A strategy that trades daily or weekly feels like it *is* working because you see feedback every session. That feedback is almost always distraction. Markets move noise at high frequency and signal at low frequency. The more frequently you check, the more noise drowns the signal, and the more you tinker.

What makes it worse: boredom is a signal of correctness, not failure. Researchers including Odean and Barber have shown that individual investors who trade most actively consistently underperform those who trade least. You can verify this in academic literature on investor behavior.

For you without capital to deploy, this is perverse luck. You cannot afford to trade frequently. That constraint, which feels like a limitation, is actually your structural advantage if you have the discipline to see it that way. Most people with capital cannot tolerate watching it sit. You're forced to. The danger is that the psychological weight—doing nothing while others appear busy—will eventually crack you. That's the real cost of boring. It is cheap in money; expensive in willpower.

> **Key idea.** Frequent trading erodes returns; boredom signals a working strategy, but most quit from impatience before gains compound.

**Hard truth.** Without capital, forced inactivity is your only real advantage. The cost: psychological—watching others trade while you wait. Most people cannot endure this and abandon. The game has one rule: you win if you stay still.

**What it changes for you.** Your salary lets you dollar-cost-average; boredom proves the strategy is working. Don't confuse inactivity with failure.

**Check it yourself:** Check SBI or Rakuten's current fee table for listed equity trades. Note the percentage cost per trade, then calculate the annual cost impact on a ¥500,000 portfolio with 10 trades per year versus 1 trade per year.
### 2. The sunk cost fallacy
<sub>`psychology` · Psychology — *How do I behave with money?*</sub>

Sunk cost means money, time or effort already spent that cannot be recovered whatever you do next. Rationally it should carry zero weight in the next decision. In practice it dominates, because stopping converts an unrealised loss into a recorded one, and people will pay to avoid that.

The effect has been studied for decades. In a 1985 study by Arkes and Blumer, theatre subscribers who had paid full price attended more performances than those who had received a discount, even though the price was gone either way. Staw's 1976 work on escalation found that people who had personally committed to a failing course of action then invested more in it when given a second round, not less.

The forward test is simple: what does the next unit of money or hour buy, compared with its best alternative? A project with three months of work done and two months left is judged on those two months. A position that is down 40% is judged on its expected future return, which is set by today's price, not by what was paid.

Projects follow the same rule. Months of build time are gone whatever happens next. The live question is whether the remaining work beats the same hours spent on the next option, and that comparison can be written down before committing.

Markets make this costly. Prices largely reflect public information, so your entry point gives you no edge over the next buyer. Holding a loser because you have too much in it is a bet that the past carries information about the future. It usually does not.

> **Key idea.** Only the cost of the next step should decide the next step; money and time already spent are gone whatever you choose.

**Hard truth.** Knowing the rule does not switch the bias off. Avoiding sunk cost also will not rescue an active strategy: SPIVA reports show roughly nine in ten US active equity funds trail their index over 15 years (approximate; check the latest scorecard).

**What it changes for you.** Your salary arrives regardless, so the trap sits in holding losers and abandoned projects; run the forward test before each further hour or yen.

**Check it yourself:** Look up the NTA page on 上場株式等の譲渡損失の繰越控除 (carry-forward of losses on listed shares) and note how many years a realised loss can be carried forward and whether a return must be filed. Confirm against the current NTA source, since rules change.
### 3. Valuation: discounted cash flow
<sub>`financing` · Financing — *Where does capital come from, and what does it cost?*</sub>

A discounted cash flow (DCF) model answers one question: what is a stream of future cash worth today? Each year's cash is divided by (1 + r) raised to the number of years away, where r is the return you demand for bearing the risk. Add the results and you have a value.

Almost all of that value sits in the terminal value, a single lump standing in for every year after the forecast window, usually years five to ten. The usual formula is next year's cash flow divided by (r minus g), where g is perpetual growth. Assume 100 of cash next year, r of 6% and g of 2%: the terminal value is 100 / 0.04 = 2,500. Drop r to 5% and it becomes 3,333. One guessed input, moved by a single point, changed the answer by a third.

The inputs are where the real uncertainty lives. Cash flows depend on margins and capital spending you cannot know for 2035. The discount rate is usually built from a government bond yield, plus an equity risk premium, scaled by beta, and every piece is an estimate. A perpetual growth rate above long-run nominal GDP growth implies the company eventually becomes the whole economy, which no company does. In Japan, the 10-year JGB yield has moved far from its near-zero levels in recent years, so any discount rate anchored on it has not stayed still.

What the method honestly does: it forces assumptions into the open so they can be argued with. It does not produce a fair value. Market prices are the aggregate of many such models, each with different guesses, and the disagreement between them is large. A DCF tells you what a price implies, which is more useful than what a price should be.

> **Key idea.** A DCF is future cash divided by a chosen rate; its precision is borrowed from assumptions you cannot verify.

**Hard truth.** Most of the number is terminal value: your guess about growth and discount rate. Over 80% of US active large-cap funds trail the index over 15 years (S&P SPIVA scorecard, approximate). With no capital, a DCF explains prices; it does not beat them.

**What it changes for you.** It changes how you read a broker's target price: treat it as a sensitivity table, not a fact, and check which discount rate and terminal growth it assumes.

**Check it yourself:** Using next-year cash of 100 and a discount rate of 6%, compute the terminal value at growth of 2% and at growth of 3%. Report the percentage increase, then find the S&P SPIVA US scorecard's 15-year underperformance figure for large-cap funds and state it.


## 2 · Book

### Fooled by Randomness
<sub>Nassim Nicholas Taleb · 2001 · `psychology` · book</sub>

Taleb argues that in markets and careers, randomness produces large, visible outcomes, so a long run of profits can come from luck rather than skill, and people reliably confuse the two. He says we judge results rather than the probabilities behind them, and we build causal stories after the fact to explain outcomes that were partly chance. He uses survivorship, selection effects and his own trading experience to show why track records are weak evidence of ability.

> **The one idea.** Judge decisions by their probabilities and process, not their outcomes: a good result can be lucky, and a sound bet can lose.

**Verdict.** Worth a bounded read, not a full one. The core idea fits in a summary, and the sections on probability versus outcome and survivorship bias are the parts that repay effort; read those and stop. The rest is repetitive, anecdote-heavy and digressive, much of it about his career and his disputes with critics. It works be…

**Caveat.** Dismisses all success as luck, which is unfalsifiable. Evidence on fund skill is contested: Fama and French (2010) find little skill in most funds, while Kosowski et al. (2006) find some top managers' alpha survives luck tests. Predates 2008 and later fat-tail research; anecdotes lean on his own career.
