### 1. The Medici bank
<sub>`history` · History & Stories — *Who did this before, and what happened?*</sub>

In 1397 Giovanni di Bicci de' Medici founded a Florentine bank that earned much of its money from bills of exchange rather than from simple loans. Church law condemned usury, so lenders hid interest inside currency conversion. A client paid cash in Bruges and received a bill redeemable in Florence weeks later, at an agreed rate that carried the interest. No one called it a loan.

The bank scaled this through correspondence. Partners ran branches in Rome, Venice, Geneva, Bruges, London and Avignon, and settled balances through internal ledgers rather than moving coin. Branch partners were paid from profit shares, which rewarded volume. Their books used double-entry accounting, the method Luca Pacioli described in his 1494 Summa, the best-known printed account of it.

The papacy was the anchor client. Papal revenues passed through Medici accounts, and the bank also handled sales of papal alum, a mordant essential to cloth dyeing. That gave it float, fees and access to Church officials and courts across Europe. Politics and banking were fused: Cosimo de' Medici was exiled in 1433 and recalled in 1434, and the family ruled Florence through informal influence over its elected offices.

The weak point was concentration. Large sums went to a few sovereigns, and loans to English and Burgundian rulers went unpaid in the 1470s. After Sixtus IV turned against the family in 1478, papal business was moved elsewhere, and the London and Bruges branches collapsed. Partners carried unlimited liability, so losses reached family wealth. Piero de' Medici was expelled from Florence in 1494, and the banking business never recovered its former scale.

> **Key idea.** Financial power comes from controlling the plumbing of money, but leveraged lending to unstable borrowers can destroy even the dominant firm.

**Hard truth.** Most Italian banking houses of this period failed, and the Medici's survival was partly protection from papal and political patrons. Their collapse came from concentrated loans to unstable sovereigns. Without capital or connections, you have neither edge.

**What it changes for you.** For a salaried engineer in Tokyo, your salary is your main cash flow, so one leveraged position concentrated in a single asset could threaten it.

**Check it yourself:** Open Pacioli's 1494 Summa (free scans exist on Google Books and archive.org) and find the accounting section, whose heading begins with Particularis de computis. Note the heading, then write one sentence defining double-entry bookkeeping in your own words.
### 2. Bonds: price, yield, duration
<sub>`intelligence` · Intelligence — *How does money actually work?*</sub>

A bond is a fixed stream of payments: coupons plus face value at maturity. Its price is the present value of those payments, discounted at the current market yield. When market yields rise, the discount rate rises and the same fixed payments are worth less. The cash flows never change; only the discount rate does. Quote a bond by its yield rather than its price, because yields compare across different coupons and maturities.

Worked example. A 10-year bond with 100 face value and a 1% annual coupon is worth exactly 100 when the market yield is 1%. Raise the market yield to 2%. Each 1 coupon is discounted by 1.02 to the power t, and the 100 principal by 1.02 to the power 10, which is 1.2190. Coupons are worth 8.98 and principal 82.03, so the price is 91.02. The bond still promises the same cash, yet its price fell about 9%.

Duration gives the sensitivity. This bond's modified duration is about 9.35, so the rule of thumb says a 1-point rise in yield cuts price by roughly 9.35%. Duration rises with maturity and falls with coupon size. A 30-year zero-coupon bond has duration near 30, so a 1-point rise at a 2% yield cuts its price by roughly 29%.

The relationship is curved, not straight. Going from 2% to 3% drops the price from 91.02 to 82.94, about 8.9%, a little less than duration predicts. This curvature, called convexity, cushions large moves. If you hold to maturity, the loss is recovered by reinvesting the higher coupons, which takes roughly the bond's duration in years, about nine or ten here.

> **Key idea.** A bond's price is the present value of fixed payments, so when yields rise, the price must fall until the yield on the old price matches the market.

**Hard truth.** Rate risk is the main risk of most bonds, not a side effect. The US aggregate bond index lost roughly 13% in 2022, a year many called safe. Institutions set these prices with better data; a retail buyer gets the market price and no edge.

**What it changes for you.** Rising rates cut the market value of any JGB or bond fund held before maturity; a salary carries no such exposure, so this matters only if you own bonds.

**Check it yourself:** Price a 10-year bond with 100 face value, 1 annual coupon, discounted at 2% per year, using an online bond calculator set to annual coupons. You should get about 91.02. Then compare that to a 3% yield to confirm the roughly 8.9% fall.
### 3. Japan: NISA and iDeCo, mechanics and limits
<sub>`investing` · Investing — *What actually produces returns, and what only looks like it does?*</sub>

NISA is a tax wrapper, not an investment. Inside it, dividends and realised gains skip the 20.315% separate tax that applies to listed-equity income outside it. A 100,000 yen gain costs about 20,315 yen outside NISA and nothing inside. Since 2024 NISA is permanent, with no end date and no age limit. It has two frames: つみたて投資枠 at 1.2 million yen a year, limited to funds the FSA has screened for low cost and no sales charge, and 成長投資枠 at 2.4 million yen a year, covering individual stocks and ETFs. The lifetime cap is 18 million yen of cost basis, of which 12 million may be growth-frame. Selling restores the sold cost basis to your room from the following calendar year. You may use only one financial institution per year for NISA, and crypto is excluded. There is no lock-up. Losses inside the account cannot be offset against anything. iDeCo works differently. Contributions are deductible from taxable income, cutting income tax and residential tax at your marginal rate, and gains inside are untaxed. The cost is a lock-up: money stays until age 60 for most people. A lump sum is taxed under the retirement-income deduction, and annuity-style payouts under the public-pension deduction. The monthly ceiling depends on whether your employer runs a corporate pension. For an employee without one it has been 23,000 yen, but the rules are under revision. Both are shelters over whatever you hold. They change what you keep from a return, never the return itself, and fund fees still come out first. Confirm every limit against the FSA NISA page and the NTA, because these rules have changed recently.

> **Key idea.** A tax wrapper changes what you keep from a return, never the return itself; read the wrapper's rules before the fund.

**Hard truth.** A shelter only saves tax on gains, so with no capital it saves nothing. Roughly 90% of active US large-cap funds trailed the S&P 500 over 15 years (SPIVA, approximate). iDeCo money is locked until 60 whatever happens.

**What it changes for you.** Your salary already bears tax, so the iDeCo deduction and NISA exemption matter most at your marginal rate; iDeCo locks money until 60.
