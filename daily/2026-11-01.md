### 1. The negative-sum arithmetic
<sub>`crypto` · Crypto — *What is actually happening, underneath the price?*</sub>

A crypto trade pays three intermediaries before the price moves in your favour. The exchange charges a fee: on spot order books, takers commonly pay roughly 0.1% to 0.6%, and simplified 'buy' buttons usually bake in more. The market maker keeps the bid-ask spread, a few basis points on large pairs and several percent on small coins. On Ethereum, bots can reorder transactions around yours, buying just before and selling just after, which Daian et al. (2019) named MEV. Flashbots' trackers log its cumulative total in the hundreds of millions of dollars, approximately.

Now the arithmetic. Bitcoin pays no coupon or dividend, so trading gains are just price moves between counterparties: one trader's gain is another's loss, before costs. Subtract the fees and spreads and the traders as a group are negative by exactly what they paid. Buy and sell with a 1% total spread and a 0.5% fee per side and you have lost about 2% of notional before the asset has moved. Recovering that requires a price rise of roughly 2% on top of whatever the market does.

Who collects? Exchanges and market makers earn on volume whichever way prices go, so their income does not depend on a forecast. Retail traders tend to trade hardest during news and sharp moves, exactly when spreads widen and better-informed flow is heaviest. Volume figures also mislead: Cong, Li, Tang and Yang (2023) estimate roughly 70% of reported volume on unregulated exchanges was wash trading. Headline volume is not evidence of liquidity you can trade into.

The mechanism is simple. Price is a transfer between participants, and every transfer pays a toll. The pool of traders starts behind by the total cost of every round trip, before anyone is right or wrong about direction.

> **Key idea.** Trading is a transfer between counterparties, so before any price move, the traders as a group lose exactly the fees and spreads they pay.

**Hard truth.** Every trade pays the spread and fees, so activity guarantees the aggregate loss, and your edge must clear that hurdle each time. Chague et al. (2019) found about 97% of persistent Brazilian day traders lost money.

**What it changes for you.** With zero spare capital, every round trip is a fixed percentage cost you must fund from income, so the spread matters more than any price forecast.

### 2. Double-entry bookkeeping
<sub>`history` · History & Stories — *Who did this before, and what happened?*</sub>

Every transaction is written twice, once as a debit and once as a credit, and the two sides always sum to the same total. Buy 100,000 yen of shares with cash: the investments account rises by 100,000 (a debit) and the cash account falls by 100,000 (a credit). Nothing is created or destroyed, so any error that breaks the equality shows up at once. That self-checking structure is the whole trick.

Pacioli did not invent it. Merchants in Venice, Genoa and Florence were keeping double-entry ledgers by the 14th century, and the method was an established craft. What Luca Pacioli did was publish a description of it. His Summa de arithmetica, geometria, proportioni et proportionalità, printed in Venice in 1494, contains a long section on accounting, the best-known printed account of the method. Print made it cheap to copy, and it spread across Europe within a few decades.

The mechanism mattered because it turned a business into numbers that could be checked. Assets minus liabilities gave net worth on a given date; comparing two dates gave profit or loss. An owner, a partner or a lender could audit the same record rather than trust memory or the merchant's word. Joint-stock companies, whose owners were strangers to the managers, later depended on exactly this kind of legibility.

Be precise about its limit. The method records and balances. It does not tell you whether a venture is profitable, whether prices are fair, or whether a stock is cheap. A perfectly balanced ledger can faithfully record a business losing money every month. Accounting describes the past; markets price the future, and that gap is where investing returns are made or lost.

> **Key idea.** Records that must balance both sides catch their own errors, making strangers' ownership and lending possible.

**Hard truth.** Double-entry tells you what happened, not what to do. SPIVA scorecards show roughly nine in ten US large-cap active funds trailing the S&P 500 over fifteen years (approximate; check the current edition). Every trade has a counterparty, and their spread and fees come out of your return.

**What it changes for you.** If you earn side income in Japan, 青色申告 requires double-entry bookkeeping for its larger deduction; confirm current amounts and conditions with NTA.

### 3. Runway and burn
<sub>`financing` · Financing — *Where does capital come from, and what does it cost?*</sub>

Runway is cash divided by net burn. Net burn is the monthly amount your cash falls: spending minus incoming money. Gross burn is spending alone, and it is the wrong number to plan with once revenue exists.

Example: ¥30,000,000 in the bank, ¥4,000,000 monthly outflow, ¥1,000,000 monthly receipts. Net burn is ¥3,000,000, so runway is 10 months. Gross burn gives 7.5 months, which is too pessimistic while revenue is real. Net burn gives 10 months only if receipts stay flat.

Two things make the figure lie. First, cash is not profit. A business can book a profit while its cash falls, because customers pay in 60 or 90 days, inventory sits on shelves, or tax and payroll fall due before receipts arrive. Timing decides when cash hits zero, even when the average looks fine. Second, burn drifts upward. Each hire, tool and contract adds to it, so today's runway is the optimistic one.

The decision number is different from the headline number. Work backwards from the next milestone: how many months of cash does it take to reach it, plus a margin for the thing that goes wrong? Then ask what happens if the milestone slips by a quarter, because it usually does. If the milestone needs nine months and runway is ten, you have no room for a slipped payment or a lost client.

Plain arithmetic, applied honestly, shows how little margin most ventures carry. Failed startups are typically analysed for cause, and running out of cash regularly ranks among the top causes; in CB Insights' post-mortems it has appeared in roughly the 30–40% range, which is approximate and worth checking against their current report. Burn that is cut late rarely recovers the lost months.

> **Key idea.** Runway is cash divided by net burn, and the number that ends a venture is when cash hits zero, not when the average looks fine.

**Hard truth.** With zero capital, your real runway is your salary. A side project with no income has no cash burn to measure, only hours, and many never reach a paying customer. Expect zero returns for months.

**What it changes for you.** Your salary is your only cash inflow, so your personal runway is savings divided by monthly spending; a zero-income project burns hours, not cash.

**Check it yourself:** Open the 有価証券報告書 of any listed Japanese company with negative operating cash flow, found on EDINET. Take the 営業活動によるキャッシュ・フロー figure for the year, divide it by 12 to get monthly burn, then divide year-end 現金及び預金 by that figure to get its runway in months.

<sub>Oliver Stone · 1987 · `markets` · film</sub>

The film dramatises a takeover business in which a corporate raider makes money by buying control of companies, breaking them up and selling the pieces, while a junior broker gets rich by trading on non-public tips that flow from him.
