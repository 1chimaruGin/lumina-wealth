### 1. "This time is different" is usually said late
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

"This time is different" surfaces near market peaks because it represents the moment when smart money has finished rewriting the story to justify current prices. By the time you hear it widely—from fund managers, business journalists, retail investors—the bull run has already priced in the new fundamentals. What's left is just extraction.

Reinhart and Rogoff examined financial crises across eight centuries and found that peak leverage and peak optimism always coincide. The crash does not come when everyone is nervous; it comes when everyone is *convinced*. "This time is different" is that conviction. In the dot-com bubble, VCs and funds genuinely believed the internet changed capitalism forever—and it did, just not at the valuations they paid. The Nikkei peaked at ¥38,915 in December 1989 on the belief that Japanese companies had escaped the boom-bust cycle. It fell over 50% in the decade that followed.

The phrase marks a transition: from discovery of genuine fundamentals to justification of prices that have overshot them. Early in a bull run, the phrase doesn't exist—fundamentals are weak, the story is uncertain. Middle stages see the genuine insight emerge. Late stages see the phrase everywhere because it is the *last argument* before everyone realizes valuation still matters.

For you as an observer: the phrase signals that expected future returns have compressed. The bull run has already captured the upside. Crashes follow, but not reliably on a timetable. Bitcoin fell 76% from its November 2021 peak and took over two years to recover to previous highs.

Hearing "this time is different" widely means you are late to the trend, not early. That's all the timing signal tells you.

> **Key idea.** When everyone says 'this time is different', the bull run's upside is already priced in and expected returns have collapsed.

**Hard truth.** The phrase appears at tops because smart money needs new buyers to sell to. You can recognize the signal but profit nothing from it without capital. Knowing the top is near doesn't help if you must eventually deploy savings.

**What it changes for you.** If you invest lump sums when the phrase is everywhere, you're buying at compressed expected returns. Being aware protects you from bad timing when your first savings appear.

**Check it yourself:** Search Google Trends for 'this time is different' and compare search volume during November 2021 (Bitcoin peak) versus November 2022 (post-crash). The phrase should peak before crashes, not after.
### 2. Social comparison and the hedonic treadmill
<sub>`psychology` · Psychology — *How do I behave with money?*</sub>

Brickman and Campbell (1971) named the hedonic treadmill: a gain in satisfaction fades as the reference point moves up. Richard Easterlin (1974) found that average reported happiness in the United States barely rose over decades of real income growth. The best-known test is Brickman, Coates and Janoff-Bulman (1978). They compared 22 lottery winners with 22 controls and found no significant difference in everyday happiness, and the winners took less pleasure from ordinary events than the controls did. Later work finds adaptation is real but partial and uneven.

Comparison is the sharper mechanism. Luttmer (2005), using US survey data, found that people reported lower happiness when their neighbours earned more, even after controlling for their own income. Clark and Oswald (1996) found the same pattern in British data: satisfaction falls as the pay of a comparison group rises. The relevant group is rarely the average person. It is the colleagues you see weekly, and the people whose gains reach you through social media.

Mechanically, a raise lifts you for a while. Then spending, expectations and your sense of normal move up to meet it, usually without any decision being made. Each time a peer does better, the reference point shifts and the same income starts to read as a shortfall. Your balance sheet did not change. The yardstick did.

The test that matters is whether your satisfaction tracks your absolute position or your rank. If it tracks rank, you are on a treadmill, and its speed is set by other people's news. Nobody can stop their peers' results, so the only variable you control is the benchmark you choose to measure against.

> **Key idea.** Satisfaction tracks your position against a moving reference group, so a peer's gain can lower your own satisfaction even when your income rose.

**Hard truth.** Fund marketing, brokerage feeds and return screenshots all resize your benchmark. Over 15 years, roughly nine in ten US active large-cap funds trailed the S&P 500 (SPIVA, approximate), and last year's winners rarely stay ahead. Comparison feels like information; mostly it is noise.

**What it changes for you.** Your reference group is colleagues and feeds, so your benchmark for enough rises unless you fix it, and that shapes every spending and investing decision in Tokyo.

**Check it yourself:** Open the latest S&P SPIVA U.S. Scorecard (spglobal.com) and find the percentage of US large-cap active funds that underperformed the S&P 500 over 15 years. Note the report year.
### 3. Paying down debt
<sub>`management` · Management — *How do I run the money I have?*</sub>

Two debts, minimum payments on both, and one spare yen a month to direct. Avalanche sends it to the highest interest rate. Snowball sends it to the smallest balance. Take 300,000 yen at 15% and 50,000 yen at 4%. Monthly interest is roughly balance × rate ÷ 12: the 15% debt costs about 3,750 yen a month, the 4% debt about 167. Every yen of principal removed from the 15% debt stops compounding at the highest cost, which is why avalanche minimises total interest. Snowball clears the 50,000 yen debt first, cuts the number of open accounts and produces visible progress sooner.

Under a standard model with no fees, avalanche always wins on total interest when rates differ. The size of the gap depends on how far apart the rates are and how large the balances are. With similar rates the two methods land close together. The behavioural evidence cuts the other way: Amar and colleagues (Journal of Marketing Research, 2011) found people tend to favour closing small accounts, a preference that can cost them interest. Treat the psychology as a real input. A plan abandoned in month four saves nothing. The method you will actually keep running beats the theoretically optimal one you drop.

Practical order: list every debt by annual rate (実質年率 on Japanese loan and card statements), not by balance. Check whether each rate is fixed or variable, and whether early repayment carries a fee, because a fee can erase the saving from clearing a debt early. Revolving card balances often carry the highest rates a household holds, so they usually lead the list. Re-run the order whenever a rate changes.

> **Key idea.** Pay debt in order of its interest cost; the method that cuts cost most is avalanche, but only the one you keep running works.

**Hard truth.** Clearing a 15% debt is a guaranteed 15% pre-tax return, above long-run average stock returns. The lender earns that spread every month the balance stays open. For someone with no capital, the debt is the investment.

**What it changes for you.** For a salaried engineer in Tokyo, card and consumer-loan balances are usually the costliest money you hold, so clearing them outranks any investing decision.

**Check it yourself:** Pull your latest statement for every debt. Write down each annual rate and current balance. Compute each monthly interest charge as balance × rate ÷ 12, then rank the debts by that charge. Does that ranking match ranking by balance?
