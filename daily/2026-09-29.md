### 1. Being right too early is indistinguishable from being wrong
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

A 2% move liquidates you.

Take 100,000 yen to an FX broker. Leverage it 25:1—the maximum typical for yen pairs in Japan. You now control 2.5 million yen of notional exposure. The maintenance margin threshold is approximately 2% of notional, or 50,000 yen. Your equity drops below that if the yen moves 2% against your position. The broker liquidates you automatically. You lose your 100,000 yen.

This is not a risk. It is a certainty given enough time.

Yen volatility is approximately 50–150 pips per day (0.5–1.5%). A 2–4% move happens every few weeks during normal market conditions. Your margin call arrives before your thesis is validated.

Contrast this with a non-leveraged investor. The same 100,000 yen in TSE-listed equities can drawdown 30% and still be held. No forced sale. No liquidation. The investor waits.

This is the core asymmetry: leverage trades clock; capital accumulates.

Historical base rates from the past 30 years are clear. During 2008, 2020, and 2022—major market troughs—peak-to-trough drawdowns in Japanese equities reached 50%+. Recovery took 2–4 years. A leveraged position at 2:1 was margin-called in weeks. A non-leveraged investor who dollar-cost-averaged through the trough made 100%+ returns within five years.

Salaried engineers with zero spare capital cannot absorb a margin call. Your income is 300,000–500,000 yen monthly. A forced liquidation wipes out savings. A second margin call is impossible. You are out.

This is why large capital wins. Not because traders trade better, but because drawdowns do not kill them. They have runway to be right.

Without capital, leverage is a certainty machine for losses. The market does not care if you are right. It cares if you can afford to wait.

> **Key idea.** Leverage replaces investment risk with timing risk. Without spare capital, you lose before being proven right.

**Hard truth.** Without reserve capital, leverage doesn't accelerate gains—it guarantees losses. A 2% yen move liquidates a 25:1 FX position. Salaried income cannot recover. For you, leverage-based trading is a timer, not an investment strategy.

**What it changes for you.** Leverage accelerates losses on your salary faster than gains. Without spare capital, salaried income becomes a liability to trading positions, not an asset.

**Check it yourself:** Log into a real FX broker (DMM FX, GMO Click, SBI FX Trade). Find the maintenance margin requirement and forced close threshold. Calculate: with 100,000 yen at 25:1 leverage, how many pips until liquidation? Compare to typical daily yen volatility (50–150 pips).
### 2. Michael Burry's two years
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

Michael Burry's fund, Scion Capital, began shorting mortgage securities in 2005. He identified the housing market collapse correctly. The market agreed in 2008—three years later.

From 2005 to 2007, Scion Capital declined in mark-to-market value. Investors watched their portfolios fall quarter after quarter. By 2007, redemptions had accelerated severely. Burry closed the fund to outside investors and continued trading with personal capital only.

Burry was correct about direction. His investors were also correct about direction. Neither experienced the same outcome. The original investors saw losses and redemptions. Burry saw vindication. The difference was not thesis quality. It was capital depth.

This reveals the market's brutal capital hierarchy. Burry owned Scion Capital outright. When investors redeemed, he absorbed those losses using personal wealth. He faced no external pressure to liquidate. Outside investors faced mounting pressure: quarterly losses visible in statements, no known end date, margin calls or personal cash-flow stress, fund restrictions, forced redemptions. Being correct on direction does not overcome this pressure when capital is finite.

Arithmetic makes this precise. An investor watching a portfolio fall 15% mark-to-market while employed full-time will face genuine pressure to exit. They cannot afford to wait three years for vindication that may or may not arrive. Burry could afford to wait. His personal capital was deep enough to sustain years of losses. Outside investors' capital simply was not.

The cost of being early is paid in real time. Burry's thesis was vindicated completely. Most of his investors never saw it. They were right about the market. They were destroyed by the timeline. Capital reserves alone determine who survives until vindication.

> **Key idea.** Correct market thesis is worthless without capital reserves to survive mark-to-market losses until vindication.

**Hard truth.** Being early without capital reserves gets you liquidated before vindication. Burry's investors were right on direction—the market proved it—but destroyed anyway. Capital runway, not insight, determines outcomes.

**What it changes for you.** Zero capital means any correct long-term thesis will liquidate you before profit. Runway matters more than direction.

### 3. The three sources of return
<sub>`investing` · Investing — *What actually produces returns, and what only looks like it does?*</sub>

Your return from any investment breaks into three parts. The first is income: what the asset throws off annually. A stock's dividend. A bond's coupon. A rental property's rent. This is real cash you receive. The second is growth: the asset itself becoming more valuable. A company's earnings expand at, say, 5% per year. A property appreciates. This is harder to measure in real-time but observable over years. The third is multiple expansion: the market deciding to pay more for the same earnings or cash flow.

A concrete example: Japanese bank stocks have historically paid dividends. That is income. If those banks grow earnings at 3% annually, that is growth. But if the market reprices them—the valuation multiple shifts—that is multiple expansion.

The critical asymmetry: only the first two are earned. Income is generated by the asset. Growth comes from improvements in fundamentals. Multiple expansion is pure sentiment: other people changing their minds about what something is worth.

Here is why this matters. If you own an asset generating 3% income and growing at 3%, you capture 6% annually before any multiple change occurs. Multiple expansion, if it happens, adds to this. But sentiment can reverse. The Japanese stock market from roughly 1990 to 2010 experienced severe multiple compression: stock prices fell dramatically despite some companies maintaining or growing earnings. The same company could generate steadier income and some growth while the price halved, because investors collectively decided to pay less per unit of earnings.

Most retail investors focus obsessively on multiple expansion—hunting the re-rating, the sentiment shift, the next growth story. This is the speculative part. And it is typically the smallest contributor to real, long-term wealth for investors without large capital.

> **Key idea.** Total return comes from three sources: income earned by the asset, growth in fundamentals, and multiple expansion—only the first two are earned within the business.

**Hard truth.** Multiple expansion requires substantial capital to move the needle. A stock's multiple doubling on a ¥100,000 position gives ¥100,000 gain. But multiples compress: Japanese equities compressed 50+ percent 1989–2012. For people building capital slowly, chasing multiples is gambling.

**What it changes for you.** Most of your return will come from income and growth compounding over decades, not from capturing valuation swings with minimal capital.

**Check it yourself:** Look up the dividend yield for Mitsubishi UFJ Financial on your broker. Compare its P/E ratio today (2026-09-28) to its P/E ratio on 2021-09-28. The percentage change in P/E is multiple compression or expansion over that five-year period.
