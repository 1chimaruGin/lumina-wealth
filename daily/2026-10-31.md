### 1. Equities: what a share actually is
<sub>`intelligence` · Intelligence — *How does money actually work?*</sub>

A share is a legal claim on a company's residual value. Creditors, employees, suppliers and tax authorities are paid first; what remains belongs to shareholders. You own a fraction of that remainder. The company owes you no coupon and no repayment date. Your return comes from two sources: cash paid out as dividends or buybacks, and the change in the price at which someone else will buy your claim. So a price is a bet on future cash flows, discounted for time and risk. Example: a company earning 100 billion yen a year, valued at 2,000 billion yen, offers an earnings yield of 5 percent before any growth. Everything above that must come from growth, or from investors paying more per yen of earnings later.

Shareholders rank last. In liquidation, equity holders get whatever creditors leave behind, so equity should demand a higher expected return than bonds. That gap is the equity risk premium. Dimson, Marsh and Staunton's global dataset puts long-run real equity returns at roughly 5 percent a year (approximate), with multi-decade stretches of little or nothing. Japan is the cautionary case: the Nikkei 225 closed at 38,915 on 29 December 1989 and did not close above that level until February 2024, about 34 years later, before counting dividends.

Price and value are different things. The gap between them is where all the returns and all the losses come from. A business can keep paying its bills while its share price falls by 80 percent.

> **Key idea.** A share is a residual claim on cash flows, so its price is a bet on expected future cash, and that bet is where all returns and losses come from.

**Hard truth.** Prices already reflect what professional buyers know. Bessembinder (2018) found about 4 percent of US-listed stocks from 1926 to 2016 accounted for all wealth created above T-bills; most single stocks lost to T-bills over their lives.

### 2. Raising your rate
<sub>`management` · Management — *How do I run the money I have?*</sub>

Start with the arithmetic, not the conversation. Take a 6,000,000 JPY gross salary. Each 1% is 60,000 JPY a year. Compounding is what matters: 3% a year for ten years gives 8.06M by year ten; 4% gives 8.88M. The gap at year ten is roughly 820,000 JPY gross a year, and it widens from there.

Take-home moves far less. For a typical salaried worker, a marginal raise is hit by income tax (所得税, progressive, about 20% at this salary), residence tax (住民税, roughly 10% of taxable income) and employee social insurance (roughly 15% combined). Roughly half of a gross raise reaches net pay. Illustration: a 3% raise (180,000 JPY gross) keeps roughly 100,000 JPY a year at the rates above. Exact shares depend on bracket and prefecture; confirm rates with NTA and your insurer.

Base pay usually moves through the annual review and the spring wage round (春闘). Rengo publishes the annual tally, and employers often anchor to it. An off-cycle request works only when it names a concrete change: expanded scope, a new title, or a market rate you can document. State the number, state the evidence, and propose the date you will revisit. Without a documented change, the common answer is "next review cycle". Set the ask before the meeting: a number, the evidence behind it, and what you will do if the answer is no. A request without a stated fallback is one you can decline without consequence.

Then test the result against inflation using the Statistics Bureau's CPI series. A raise below inflation is a real-terms cut, even when the payslip rises.

> **Key idea.** A raise compounds for years and nets to roughly half its gross, so price any ask in both terms first.

**Hard truth.** Asking alone usually returns the increase already in the budget. Your real lever is an outside offer or a documented change in scope, not persistence. Without either, the request is a negotiation with no alternative on the table.

**What it changes for you.** Your raise is usually set by an annual budget, so a credible outside option moves it more than a well-timed request.

### 3. Defaults and status quo bias
<sub>`psychology` · Psychology — *How do I behave with money?*</sub>

Status quo bias is the tendency to keep whatever option is already set, even when switching is cheap and better. In personal finance the default is the most powerful setting most people never touch.

The clearest evidence is Madrian and Shea (2001). A US employer moved new hires into its 401(k) by automatic enrollment, with a 3% contribution rate unless they opted out. Participation rose from about 37% to about 86%. Most auto-enrolled employees stayed at the 3% rate and the default fund for as long as the study tracked them.

Thaler and Benartzi's Save More Tomorrow design (published 2004) went further. Employees agreed in advance to direct part of each future pay rise into savings. Among those who stayed in, the saving rate rose from about 3.5% to 13.6% of pay over roughly 40 months, and very few opted out.

The mechanism is simple. Doing nothing costs effort for the person who wants a change and nothing for the person who stays put. So whoever writes the default usually writes the outcome.

Japan works the same way. Corporate DC plans and iDeCo require you to act, and the products on offer are chosen by employers or providers. Ordinary deposit accounts pay close to zero, so inertia there is costly too. Bank of Japan flow-of-funds data put household cash and deposits at roughly half of financial assets. That figure is approximate and changes each release, so confirm it against the current data.

The number that matters is the fee and expected return of the default product. Those are set by someone else.

> **Key idea.** Whoever sets the default usually sets the outcome, because most people never change it, even when changing is free.

**Hard truth.** Defaults act only on money you already have. A default that parks savings in a low-yield deposit or a high-fee fund costs you every year, and the provider collects that fee. Inertia works for them, not you.

**What it changes for you.** Your employer's default retirement settings and any iDeCo product you open will shape your outcome unless you actively change them, so check both.

**Check it yourself:** Compute 1,000,000 yen growing at 5% a year for 30 years, then at 4.5% a year, and subtract. Then find the 信託報酬 (annual fund fee) of the default product in your employer plan or iDeCo provider's 目論見書 and calculate its yearly yen cost on 1,000,000 yen.

### Money: The Unauthorised Biography
<sub>Felix Martin · 2013 · `intelligence` · book</sub>

Martin argues that money is a social unit of account, a measure of credit and debt set by a community or authority, rather than a commodity that evolved to fix barter's inconveniences. He traces this through Mesopotamian accounting, tallies, medieval and early-modern banking, and state-backed currencies, arguing that money's value rests on institutional acceptance and enforcement rather than on any metal. He concludes that the textbook sequence of barter, then gold, then paper misdescribes both the history of money and how the modern system works.

> **The one idea.** Money is a socially enforced relationship of credit and debt, measured in a unit of account. Its value comes from institutions that accept it, not from any intrinsic commodity.
