### 1. Optionality and the value of waiting
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

Real options theory says a choice—the right but not obligation to act later—has monetary value. If you pay $3 to buy a call option on a $100 stock (right to purchase at $105 in one year), you've bought the right to wait. If the stock falls to $80, you don't exercise it; the $3 cost you less than buying at $100. If it rises to $120, you wish you'd bought outright.

This applies everywhere. Japanese real estate: buy now or wait. Waiting costs you rental yield (roughly 3–4% annually in central Tokyo) and price appreciation if the market rises. It also saves you from overpaying in a bubble. The value of waiting depends on what actually happens—which you don't know.

Here's the cost: holding cash for optionality erodes wealth. Japanese interest rates are near zero, so cash yields almost nothing. If you hold ¥1 million waiting for a better opportunity, and the market rises 8% that year, you've foregone ¥80,000. If inflation runs 2%, cash loses ¥20,000 in purchasing power.

Retail investors routinely overpay for optionality they never use. Dalbar's annual study shows retail investors underperform index funds by 1–3% yearly; timing decisions—waiting for "the right entry"—account for much of it. The S&P 500 rose 24% in 2023, 18% in 2024 year-to-date. Investors holding cash waiting for a dip missed both. They paid the optionality cost without exercising the option.

For someone with no spare capital, this is theoretical. You can't afford to hold cash for optionality when you have no cash to hold. Optionality is a problem only once you have capital to decide what to do with it.

**Hard truth.** Optionality costs money to maintain: foregone gains in bull markets, inflation erosion, explicit option premiums. **What it changes for you.** Optionality explains why companies sit on cash and why waiting to invest—once you have capital—costs more than deploying it immediately in bull markets.

**Check it yourself:** Find the current price of an ATM (at-the-money) call option on the S&P 500 (one-year expiry) on your broker. Divide the premium by the strike price. That percentage is what optionality costs you annually.
### 2. Deadlines as commitment devices
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

Deadlines work through scarcity, not motivation. Parkinson's law: work expands to fill time. "Learn about markets" without a deadline becomes an endless 8-hour-a-week task; "learn by Dec 31" forces prioritization. A 90-day deadline works because it is long enough to make progress but short enough to prevent indefinite deferral.

Waiting has measurable costs. If you have 100,000 JPY and delay investing for five years at a 5% average annual return, you lose roughly 35,000 JPY in foregone gains. Higher returns mean steeper losses; at 8–10% annually, the five-year delay costs tens of thousands in opportunity losses. Japan's Nikkei 225 tripled between 2012 and 2021. Waiting five years to enter at 2012 prices meant concrete losses in subsequent gains. Opportunity cost compounds regardless of subsequent deflation or growth.

For learning, the cost is invisible but real. Knowledge gaps left unfilled for months decay: you forget nuances, lose conviction, and restart near-zero when you return. A deadline forces consolidation. By a set date, you test your understanding through simulation or a written allocation plan. You commit to falsifiable claims about markets. This creates urgency and clarity.

But here is the structural problem. Deadlines work as commitment devices only when missing them costs something real. A deadline to "understand markets" is abstract; you incur zero loss if you miss it. A deadline to "have 10,000 JPY deployed by Q2" is concrete: missing it means capital undeployed and gains foregone. Your pattern of abandoning projects suggests the missing element is skin in the game.

Without capital at stake, your learning deadline is low-stakes. The cost of missing it is zero. The mechanism that makes deadlines work—real consequences for procrastination—is absent. A 90-day deadline feels urgent only when it leads to a decision with money deployed. Until then, it is another arbitrary constraint you will likely abandon.

> **Key idea.** Deadlines force prioritization through artificial scarcity; waiting costs money via opportunity cost; the mechanism requires real stakes.

**Hard truth.** Deadlines work only when missing them costs something real. Learning without capital deployed has no cost. Without money at stake, expect to abandon this deadline like the others.

**What it changes for you.** Deadlines force progress only when money is at stake. Your learning deadline gains teeth when tied to capital deployment, not before.

**Check it yourself:** Look up the Nikkei 225 closing price on Jan 1, 2015 and Jan 1, 2020, then Jan 1, 2020 and Jan 1, 2025. Calculate the value of a 100,000 JPY investment at the 2015 price by 2020, versus a 2020 investment by 2025. The difference quantifies the opportunity cost of waiting five years.
### 3. Exchanges are the counterparty you forgot
<sub>`crypto` · Crypto — *What is actually happening, underneath the price?*</sub>

An exchange balance is a database entry, not a coin you hold. When you deposit bitcoin, the company records that it owes you that amount, and the bitcoin sits in wallets whose keys it controls. You are an unsecured creditor. Whether that claim is backed depends entirely on how the company keeps its books and keys. This is the same arrangement as a bank deposit, except there is usually no deposit insurance to fall back on.

Mt. Gox handled most bitcoin trading in 2013. Over several years it lost roughly 850,000 BTC, about 7% of the supply then in existence, through theft and mismanagement. It filed for bankruptcy in February 2014 and froze withdrawals. Creditors queued as claimants. Repayments through the trustee began in 2024, a decade later, with claims valued at 2014 prices.

FTX, in November 2022, had the same structure with different wiring. Customer deposits were moved to Alameda Research, its sister trading firm, which used them. Bankruptcy filings put the shortfall at roughly $8 billion. No hacker was involved; the transfers were internal.

Japan's answer is regulatory. Exchanges need FSA registration under the Payment Services Act, must segregate customer assets, and must keep most of them in offline storage. The rules tightened after Coincheck lost roughly ¥58 billion of NEM in 2018. Segregation is a legal requirement, not a guarantee.

The mechanism to remember: any exchange that fills your order instantly is also running its own ledger. Your balance is only as good as its key controls and its management's honesty. Ask what the exchange would have to do to pay every customer at once, and whether it could.

> **Key idea.** An exchange balance is a claim on a company's ledger, not a coin in your hand; its custody practices are the real asset.

**Hard truth.** Recoveries are slow and partial: Mt. Gox creditors waited about ten years and were paid on 2014 valuations. Japan's deposit insurance generally does not cover crypto. Small balances queue behind the same lawyers as large ones.

**What it changes for you.** In Tokyo, FSA registration and the key-holding arrangement decide whether your exchange balance is custody or an unsecured loan to the company.

**Check it yourself:** Find the FSA's list of registered crypto-asset exchange service providers (金融庁 暗号資産交換業者 登録一覧) and confirm your exchange appears on it. Then read its terms for how customer assets are held: segregated, commingled, or unspecified.
