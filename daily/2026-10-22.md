### 1. Real estate: leverage wearing a safety costume
<sub>`investing` · Investing — *What actually produces returns, and what only looks like it does?*</sub>

Take a ¥30m flat bought with ¥3m of your own money and a ¥27m loan. A 10% fall in price removes ¥3m, which is all of your equity, before costs. A 10% rise adds ¥3m, a 100% gain on equity. Leverage works in both directions. The building does not.

The headline figure is gross rental yield: annual rent divided by price. Subtract vacancy, management fees, repair reserves, property tax (固定資産税), insurance and loan interest. On a ¥27m loan at 2%, year-one interest alone is about ¥540,000. Net cash yield is often a fraction of the headline and can be negative.

It looks stable because units are priced by appraisal or occasional sale, not daily. Appraisal-based indices show smoother paths than transaction-based ones, a well-documented smoothing effect. Volatility is lower on paper, and losses are not marked until someone sells.

Long-run cross-country studies, such as Jordà et al. (2019), put total housing returns in advanced economies broadly near equity returns over the 20th century. Treat that as a rough magnitude. It describes the asset, not a leveraged buyer's cash return.

Tax follows the instrument. Rental income is 不動産所得, taxed progressively and combined with salary. Property held over five years and sold is 長期譲渡所得 at 20.315% separate taxation. Brokerage is capped by law: on a ¥30m resale, the cap is roughly ¥1.06m including consumption tax, paid by each side. Confirm all of this on the NTA and MLIT sites, since rules change.

> **Key idea.** Real estate's returns come mostly from leverage, and its low reported volatility comes from infrequent pricing, not from stability.

**Hard truth.** Sellers earn on the sale, not on your outcome. A 10% fall can erase your entire down payment. For someone with no capital, this is mostly out of reach, and the loan is the part that can hurt you.

**What it changes for you.** Your salary is already one income bet; a mortgage stacks a second, leveraged bet on one building and makes your job the backstop for both.

**Check it yourself:** Using a ¥30m flat bought with ¥3m down and a ¥27m loan, compute what share of your ¥3m equity remains after a 10% price fall, before any costs.
### 2. Valuation: multiples
<sub>`financing` · Financing — *Where does capital come from, and what does it cost?*</sub>

A multiple is a price divided by a flow. The common one is the P/E: share price divided by earnings per share. A P/E of 20 means you pay 20 yen for each yen of annual profit, so the earnings yield is 1/20, or 5%. That yield is the number to compare with alternatives, such as a government bond paying whatever the 10-year JGB yields today.

A multiple is not a measurement of cheapness. It is a compressed forecast. In the simplest model, price equals next year's earnings divided by (required return minus expected growth). Hold the price fixed and raise the growth assumption, and a P/E that looked rich becomes reasonable. The multiple hides the assumptions that produced it.

Multiples are also circular. The comparables used to call a stock cheap are priced by the same market. When a whole sector re-rates, every company looks fairly valued against its peers. Relative valuation tells you where something sits within a market, not whether the market is priced sensibly. Denominators get chosen too: forward earnings, adjusted earnings, EBITDA. Each is picked to make the number look better.

For the whole market, the long-run record is the anchor. Robert Shiller's cyclically adjusted P/E for the S&P 500 has averaged roughly 17 since 1871 and peaked near 44 in late 1999. Extreme starting valuations have historically been followed by lower ten-year returns, but a few cycles give wide error bars, not a timing tool.

> **Key idea.** A multiple is a price divided by a flow, so it is a compressed forecast of growth and risk, not a measure of cheapness.

**Hard truth.** Cheap-looking multiples are published free to everyone, so the obvious value signal is already in prices. Low P/E stocks are often cheap because earnings are falling. Value trailed growth in the US for much of the 2010s. Expect long underperformance before any premium appears.

**What it changes for you.** Any equity index fund you hold is priced by these multiples, so the earnings-yield gap over JGBs is the premium the market offers for taking equity risk.

**Check it yourself:** Find today's 10-year JGB yield in the Ministry of Finance's published table and a current trailing P/E for TOPIX from a dated source. Invert the P/E to an earnings yield and compute the gap in percentage points. Note the dates of both figures.
### 3. Exchange rates and purchasing power
<sub>`intelligence` · Intelligence — *How does money actually work?*</sub>

Purchasing power parity (PPP) says exchange rates should, over the long run, drift until the same basket of goods costs the same in two currencies. The Economist's Big Mac index is the crude test. Divide the Big Mac's yen price by its dollar price to get the rate the burger implies, then compare that with the actual USD/JPY rate. In recent editions the yen has looked undervalued against the dollar by roughly 35 to 45 percent (approximate; the figure changes each edition).

The mechanism that moves the rate in the short run is different. Exchange rates are set mostly by capital flows, and those follow interest-rate gaps and expectations. When Japanese policy rates sat near zero while US rates were several points higher, money flowed toward dollars and the yen weakened. Burger prices do not move the rate within a year.

For purchasing power, the relevant calculation is local price times rate. A yen salary buys Japanese goods at Japanese prices. Imports, energy, and anything priced in dollars rise in yen when the yen weakens. Japan's consumer prices rose roughly 2 to 4 percent a year from 2022, and real wages were negative in most months of 2022 to 2024 according to the MHLW monthly labour survey. Nominal pay rising did not make your basket cheaper.

PPP gaps can persist for a decade. The Big Mac comparison ignores tariffs, rents, wages and what you actually buy, so treat it as a direction, not a forecast.

> **Key idea.** Purchasing power depends on the exchange rate and local prices together, and the rate can stay far from fair value for years.

**Hard truth.** Trading currency toward fair value mostly loses for retail: ESMA-mandated broker disclosures show 74-89% of retail CFD accounts lose money. Currency is roughly zero-sum before spreads and swaps. Without capital, your realistic exposure is as a cost, not a return.

**What it changes for you.** Your salary is in yen, but the USD-priced cloud, AI and software tools you buy are not; a weaker yen raises that bill with no change to your pay.
