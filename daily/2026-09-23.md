### 1. Commodity money
<sub>`history` · History & Stories — *Who did this before, and what happened?*</sub>

When cowrie shells became money in West African trade around the 1600s, no king issued them. Merchants simply agreed: these shells mean value. A Portuguese trader arriving in Benin knew cowrie shells mattered because everyone around him treated them as irreplaceable goods. The shells worked as money for three reasons.

First, they were scarce. Cowries come from specific ocean depths and weren't lying on every beach. You couldn't manufacture a thousand overnight. This scarcity created the friction needed for trade—if shells were abundant as sand, they'd be worthless.

Second, they were recognizable. A cowrie shell looks like a cowrie shell. No counterfeiting with painted pebbles or clay; the real thing has specific weight, gloss, and texture. Every trader from Timbuktu to Zanzibar knew exactly what they were accepting.

Third, they were divisible and portable. One shell was a unit. Five shells fit in a pouch. Unlike cattle (which existed as money in many cultures but required herding) or salt (which dissolved in rain), shells didn't degrade and didn't need infrastructure.

The key insight: money emerges from agreement on scarcity, not from law. Governments eventually replaced cowrie shells with stamped metal and then paper because those offered scale—you can issue millions of coins faster than collecting shells. But the mechanism was identical. When Roman soldiers received their pay in denarii, or when modern miners accept Bitcoin, the same logic holds. The user community collectively enforces three rules: this item is hard to copy, everyone recognizes it, and you can break it into smaller amounts. No issuer survives without these three properties.

Understanding this explains why most alternative currencies fail. A startup coins a digital token nobody recognizes. Another invents a local scrip that only one store accepts. Both violate the scarcity-recognizability-divisibility test. Real money—whether shells or code—solves for all three at once.

> **Key idea.** Money forms when users agree something is scarce, recognizable, and divisible—government doesn't create, only enforce.

**Why it matters to you.** As a software engineer in Tokyo with limited capital, your side income depends on users recognizing value instantly—or the project dies when you move to the next idea.

**Ask yourself:** Each time you've abandoned a side income project after a few weeks, did potential customers fail to recognize its value—or did you move on before they could?

### 2. The emergency fund
<sub>`management` · Management — *How do I run the money I have?*</sub>

An emergency fund is not an investment or a step toward wealth. It's insurance that prevents you from being forced to sell assets in a panic or to borrow at bad terms when vulnerable.

How much you need depends on your situation. Conventional wisdom suggests three to six months of essential expenses. For someone on a salaried job in Tokyo, calculate your baseline monthly spend: rent, utilities, food, transport. If that's ¥200,000, then three months is ¥600,000. If your side income is irregular or you have family obligations—common in Japan with aging parents—lean toward six months.

Where you hold it is critical. Not in stocks; the time horizon is too short and volatility is unaffordable. A savings account is safer, though Japanese deposit rates have been historically low. Compare accounts at major banks, or consider a money market fund if fees are low. The criterion: you must access the money without penalty, and its value cannot drop.

What counts as an emergency is specific. A laptop failure, sudden medical bill, family crisis, or income gap—not a holiday or new gadget. The fund exists to keep you solvent when your normal income stops or a large bill arrives.

For your situation, this fund is load-bearing. Your scattered-focus pattern means that without a buffer, a ¥400,000 repair or two months of flat side income forces you back to the salaried job—and the side project dies. If six months of baseline expenses are saved, you can absorb a setback without abandoning the income stream you're building. The fund buys the short-term runway to pursue irregular income, which moves you toward independence.

The corrective myth: an emergency fund is not 'the first step to getting rich.' It's preventing ruin.

> **Key idea.** Emergency funds prevent panic decisions; they buy runway for side projects without forcing you back to salaried-work-only mode.

**Why it matters to you.** An emergency fund protects your irregular side income experiments from sudden expenses that would collapse them into salary-only focus.

**Ask yourself:** Has an unexpected large expense ever killed momentum on a side project you were building—and would ¥600,000 in savings have prevented your scattered-focus pattern from pausing it?
