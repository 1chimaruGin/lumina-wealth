### 1. Where money comes from
<sub>`intelligence` · Intelligence — *How does money actually work?*</sub>

When you take a loan from a bank, the bank doesn't hand you money from someone else's savings account. Instead, it creates the loan and deposit simultaneously. Your loan account appears as an asset on the bank's books; your deposit appears as a liability. The deposit is new — it didn't exist before.

This is true at scale too. When a government issues a bond that a bank buys, the bank creates a new deposit for the government and records the bond as an asset. When a business borrows to build a factory, the loan and the construction company's deposit are created together. The money supply expands with each loan, not because cash moves around, but because new deposits come into existence.

Banks don't need dollar-for-dollar deposits to make loans. A bank with £10 million in reserves might make £50 million in loans, creating £50 million in new deposits. The deposits are real — spendable, transferable, withdrawable — but they exist only because the bank believed the borrower would repay. This is fractional reserve banking.

The consequence is counterintuitive. Banks create most of the money supply through lending. The central bank controls this indirectly via interest rates and reserve requirements, but commercial banks create the bulk of deposits through lending. This is why recessions happen: if banks stop lending, new deposits stop being created, and the money supply contracts even though the central bank is standing ready.

Understanding this changes how you think about debt, currency, and economic cycles. Money isn't fixed from above — it's continuously created and destroyed by banks' willingness to lend and borrowers' willingness to borrow.

> **Key idea.** Bank lending creates deposits; the money supply expands when banks lend and contracts when they stop.

**Why it matters to you.** As lending creates money, your future side income could support borrowing to start now—removing the 'need capital first' barrier.

**Ask yourself:** When you've set aside a side income idea for lack of capital, did you check whether the first earnings could support borrowing — or did you assume the capital had to pre-exist?

### 2. Central banks and the policy rate
<sub>`intelligence` · Intelligence — *How does money actually work?*</sub>

A central bank controls the policy rate—the overnight rate at which banks lend to each other—by buying and selling securities to adjust how much cash banks hold. The Federal Reserve, Bank of Japan, and European Central Bank deploy this single lever to anchor every other interest rate downstream: mortgages, credit cards, business loans. When the Fed raised its policy rate from near zero in March 2022 to above 5% by late 2023, commercial banks quickly raised their lending rates. That's what central banks control.

What they don't control is what happens next. A central bank influences lending demand, but cannot force it. In 2008, when Lehman Brothers collapsed, the Fed cut its policy rate to near zero. Credit still froze. Banks had no appetite to lend despite zero borrowing costs; borrowers had no appetite to borrow. The rate signal broke because fear overwhelmed incentives.

The Bank of Japan discovered this boundary over decades. Beginning in February 1999, it held rates near zero to fight deflation and sluggish growth. For more than two decades, rates stayed flat. Inflation barely moved. This is the liquidity trap: once rates hit zero, the central bank's primary tool stops working. You cannot lower rates below zero in normal systems, so you have spent your ammunition. Japan's experience—twenty years of near-zero rates, persistent low inflation, weak wage growth—shows that cheap money alone doesn't create demand.

Inflation itself sits partly outside central bank control. Raising rates makes borrowing expensive, which can cool spending and reduce price pressure. But the lag is long—six months to eighteen months before inflation data responds—and the effect is blunt. Supply shocks (sudden oil spikes), wage dynamics (labor scarcity), or import costs can overpower what a central bank can do with rates. A central bank pushes conditions, not outcomes. It shapes incentives but cannot command them.

> **Key idea.** A central bank commands its policy rate but only influences credit flow, lending demand, and inflation; zero rates break the mechanism.

**Why it matters to you.** Central bank rate cycles take 6–18 months to reshape Tokyo's startup and freelance income opportunities; missing the timing costs real yen.

**Ask yourself:** You abandon projects when payoffs seem distant (your present_bias pattern). Since BoJ rate changes take 6–18 months to reshape markets, how would you use that timeline to set interim milestones that keep you engaged until the macro shift actually hits?
