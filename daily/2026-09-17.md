### 1. How venture capital actually works
<sub>`financing` · Financing — *Where does capital come from, and what does it cost?*</sub>

Venture capital doesn't work the way most people imagine. VCs don't fund good ideas—they fund portfolios of risky bets because they need outliers.

Here's the structure: Limited partners (insurance companies, pension funds, family offices) invest roughly $100 million into a single VC fund. The VC firm takes a 2% management fee annually (that's $2 million just to operate) plus 20% of any profits they generate, called carry. After nine years, the fund must return capital or shut down. This creates an absolute constraint: the fund needs massive wins to cover the fees and meet limited partners' expected return.

The VC firm receives thousands of applications. It funds maybe 50–100 companies per fund. Statistically: about 30–40% fail entirely; 50% return 1x–3x of invested capital; and 1–2 companies return 20x–100x. Those outliers return the entire fund. If one company reaches $1 billion valuation and the VC owned 25% at investment, that's $250 million from a $100 million fund. That single exit covers all the failures and all the modest returns.

This has two hard implications. First: VCs cannot fund stable, profitable small businesses. A company returning 3x in five years looks like failure given their fee structure and timescale. Second: you almost certainly cannot raise VC capital if you're building from zero capital in Japan. VCs fund founders with networks, prior exits, or extraordinary credentials. A $250,000 check barely justifies their overhead—they prefer $2 million+ checks to unknown founders.

If you grasp this, you realize building side income in Tokyo cannot follow the VC playbook. You need a model that generates short-term cash without requiring either VC capital or eight years of unprofitable growth. That means bootstrapping, not pitching.

> **Key idea.** VC funds need outlier returns because their fee structure demands it; ordinary growth doesn't survive portfolio math.

**Why it matters to you.** You cannot raise VC capital from Japan with zero startup capital. Understanding VC economics reveals why your constraints require a different business model.

### 2. An automation architecture
<sub>`management` · Management — *How do I run the money I have?*</sub>

Most people manage money reactively — balance appears in an account, they decide what to do with it. As an engineer, you already know reactive systems are fragile. The alternative is architecture: define flows so the right thing happens by default, and you only intervene when exceptions occur.

Start by mapping your salary destination. In Japan, your employer deposits salary into your primary bank account. From there, you can set up standing orders (定期振込) to split it automatically: emergency fund to one account, any savings goal to another, remainder stays for immediate spending. Each yen has a job the moment it lands.

The key is visibility without action. You're not managing each transfer — you're observing that it happened. This is different from traditional automation advice, which focuses on "set and forget." You still check. You still see money moving. But the movement is deterministic, not reactive.

Why this matters for your constraints: You have no spare capital but you can earn control. Each standing order is a small system that works while you sleep. With 8 hours a week available, automation saves those hours for work that produces income, not money busywork. When side income arrives in a separate account, the same architecture applies — split it by the same rules. No decision fatigue.

The trap is over-engineering early. Start with two flows: emergency fund and everything else. A three-bucket system is common but adds decision complexity. Get one layer working, observe it for a month, then refine.

For scattered focus, make one automation stream your rule. Don't set up three different "optimization experiments" simultaneously. One flow, one feedback loop, one month of observation. When that stabilizes, then consider the next layer.

> **Key idea.** Define account flows so the right behavior happens by default, eliminating daily money decisions and freeing time for income work.

**Why it matters to you.** Automation eliminates repetitive account maintenance, redirecting your 8 hours/week toward earning side income instead of managing daily transfers.

**Ask yourself:** You have scattered_focus. How many different money-automation setups are you currently running or tinkering with—and which one actually works hands-off without your weekly intervention?
