### 1. Debt came first
<sub>`history` · History & Stories — *Who did this before, and what happened?*</sub>

Your earliest ancestors didn't need coins to borrow grain. In Sumer, around 3000 BCE, temple administrators recorded loans of barley on clay tablets—the oldest known accounting. A farmer could walk to the temple, request grain to plant, and leave owing a percentage back at harvest. The temple carved marks into clay to track the debt, creating the first systematic record of who owed what, and why. Tallies—notched wooden sticks or clay tokens—worked the same way across cultures: one notch per unit, split between creditor and debtor so both held identical proof. For millennia, debt was managed without a single coin in circulation.

What's striking is how this solved a real problem: survival requires resources you don't yet have. Coinage came later—the first standardized coins appeared in Lydia (modern Turkey) around 650 BCE—because debt systems were already working. Coins didn't invent lending; they just made it portable and standardized.

But debt systems created a secondary problem. Borrowers who fell behind could lose land, family, freedom. By the time Hebrew law formalized the jubilee—a 50-year reset where all debts dissolved and slaves went free (Leviticus 25)—the imbalance had become unbearable. The jubilee was not mercy; it was crisis management. Without it, creditors would own everything and everyone would serve them.

This isn't history. It's a mechanism. Debt lets you access resources faster than you can save. That speed matters when survival is at stake. It still matters when you're building. But the speed creates obligation, and obligation without release creates a different kind of poverty: the kind where you're perpetually catching up.

> **Key idea.** Debt existed before coinage because people always need resources faster than they can save—creating immediate access at the cost of future obligation.

**Why it matters to you.** When building side income with no capital, you'll face offers trading immediate access for future obligation; history shows this trap is ancient and rarely optional.

**Ask yourself:** Where in your current side-income plan have you committed to something you can't easily exit from—and how does your scattered_focus pattern make that risky?

### 2. Debt versus equity
<sub>`financing` · Financing — *Where does capital come from, and what does it cost?*</sub>

When you need money to start or grow something, you face a choice: borrow it or give up ownership. These paths cost completely differently.

Debt means you promise to repay a fixed amount plus interest. A bank loan, a credit card advance, or bonds all work this way. The cost is explicit: if you borrow 100,000 JPY at 5% annually, you owe 5,000 JPY per year in interest alone, regardless of whether your venture succeeds or fails. You keep full ownership and control, but you're contractually obligated to repay even if the project flops. This is why businesses with steady cash flow prefer debt—a bakery with reliable daily sales can manage predictable loan payments. But it's brutal if your income becomes unreliable.

Equity means you trade ownership for capital. A venture capitalist invests 100,000 JPY and gets, say, 10% of your company. They profit only if the business grows—they lose if it fails. The cost to you is invisible at first but enormous over time: you've given away 10% of every future dollar the business earns. If your side project eventually makes 10 million JPY annually, that 10% equity stake is worth 1 million JPY per year to the investor, not the 5,000 JPY they'd earn from a 5% loan. Equity investors also usually demand influence—board seats, veto rights, strategic input. You lose autonomy.

The irony: startups love equity because it doesn't drain cash today. But they pay for it later through dilution. Mature businesses love debt because interest is fixed and predictable. Neither is universally cheaper—it depends on your risk and growth rate.

> **Key idea.** Debt drains cash flow now; equity dilutes ownership later. Neither is cheaper universally—your growth rate and cash position decide.

**Why it matters to you.** Side income ideas need funding. With no capital of your own, understanding debt vs. equity costs prevents you funding ventures you'll abandon.

**Ask yourself:** When you've abandoned projects before, which would have felt worse six months later: owing monthly debt to a bank, or having given equity to an investor who now owns a piece of your failed venture?
