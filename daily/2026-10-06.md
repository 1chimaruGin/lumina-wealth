### 1. Lock-ups, vesting and forced patience
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

Vesting schedules lock you out of your own equity for a defined period, typically 4 years with a 1-year cliff: nothing for year one, then 25%, then 1/36 of the remainder each month for three years. You own the shares, but cannot sell them until the schedule releases them. This is deliberate: it removes the option to sell at the worst moments.

Holding is involuntary when you have no choice. Someone with fully liquid equity decides daily whether to hold or sell; in bear markets, anxiety often tips the scales toward selling. Someone with a 1-year cliff does not face the decision for 12 months; by the time they can sell, the worst panic has usually passed. At IPO lock-up expiry, restricted insiders often profit more than those who could sell immediately. The wealth gap is documented: restricted equity holders in successful companies accumulate more value than comparable peers with liquid equity or cash bonuses. But this advantage only exists if the company succeeds; restricted equity in a failed startup is worth zero.

The mechanism is what economists call a "commitment device"—a constraint that solves a problem you have (premature selling caused by anxiety) by removing your ability to choose. You cannot override good long-term decisions with short-term panic.

This is only relevant if you own equity. For you, that means receiving it from an employer or buying into a restricted offering yourself. Most Tokyo salaried employees do not receive meaningful equity grants; it is more common at startups and multinational tech offices. If you do receive RSUs or restricted stock from an employer, the vesting schedule is the cost of that equity: you cannot sell until the schedule allows it. That inability to sell is also the only mechanism that prevents you from panic-selling.

> **Key idea.** Forced illiquidity prevents panic selling better than discipline; vesting schedules work because they remove the choice.

**Hard truth.** This only works if you receive equity. Most Japanese salaried employees do not. Even if you do, vesting locks you in—you cannot access funds if you need them. It only benefits you if the company succeeds; restricted equity worth zero teaches nothing.

**What it changes for you.** For a salaried engineer, relevant only if your employer grants equity—currently rare in Tokyo; most of your peers likely receive only salary.

**Check it yourself:** If you receive equity grants, find your offer letter and calculate: what percentage of your grant is liquid today based on the cliff and vesting schedule? If not, check your employer's benefits page: do they offer RSUs or restricted stock to any employees?
### 2. Compounding needs time more than rate
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

$10,000 at 5% annually for 30 years reaches $43,219. The same $10,000 at 15% annually for 10 years reaches $40,454. Time beats rate—mediocre returns win. This is taught everywhere as proof that patience compounds wealth. It's also almost entirely irrelevant to you right now.

Compounding only works if you have capital. You don't—you have a salary. Salary is linear income, not exponential growth. A salaried engineer earning roughly ¥6M per year accumulates ¥6M per year. After 10 years, ¥60M. After 30 years, ¥180M (before tax, living costs, or anything else). That's addition, not compounding.

If you invest ¥600,000 annually at 5% over 30 years, you end with roughly ¥40M from ¥18M of deposits. The gain from compounding is ¥22M—real money. But your salary over the same 30 years is ¥180M. The returns from compounding are dwarfed by the income from work. The growth is dominated by the flow of salary deposits, not by investment magic.

The uncomfortable part arrives in your 40s. Once you've accumulated meaningful capital and salary growth plateaus, compounding becomes the engine. Until then, your return on salary—through promotion, job switching, and skill development—almost certainly exceeds any market return. A 10% raise is a 10% return on your current earnings. Market returns of 5–7% are weak by comparison. Optimize for salary and skill, not market timing.

Compounding only benefits people who already have capital. You're playing the accumulation game. Treat it like a job: maximize income, minimize living costs, move the money to investments. The compounding lecture is a luxury for people 15 years ahead of where you are.

> **Key idea.** Time multiplies returns exponentially only if capital exists first; without capital, salary accumulation is linear.

**Hard truth.** Compounding favors people with existing wealth. Your salary is linear income. Achieving meaningful capital takes a decade of salaried work. Market returns of 5–7% cannot compete with salary growth and returns from career advancement during that phase.

**What it changes for you.** Career returns dwarf market returns during accumulation. Focus on salary growth and skill depth for the next decade; market compounding begins later.

**Check it yourself:** Calculate: 10% of your annual salary. Then calculate: 7% of your total accumulated investments so far. Compare the two numbers. Which is larger?
### 3. Revenue-based financing
<sub>`financing` · Financing — *Where does capital come from, and what does it cost?*</sub>

Revenue-based financing (RBF) is a way to get capital without debt or equity. A lender provides money upfront and you repay it as a percentage of your monthly revenue until they've recovered their principal plus a return. Platforms like Clearco and Pipe offer this.

The key difference from debt: you have no fixed monthly payment. If you earn ¥500,000 in a month, you might pay 5% (¥25,000); if you earn ¥50,000, you pay ¥2,500. This protects you during slow periods. The key difference from equity: the funder doesn't own part of your company. You keep full control and all future profits after repayment.

RBF beats debt when your revenue is unpredictable. A lender might want ¥30,000 monthly regardless of whether you made ¥80,000 or ¥20,000 that month. RBF aligns repayment with actual cashflow. It beats equity when you're confident your business will work. With equity, an investor owns part of your company forever; with RBF, you owe a percentage of revenue for a limited time. Once you've repaid, all future revenue is yours alone.

RBF works best for subscription businesses or e-commerce with predictable, recurring revenue. It doesn't work for one-off consulting or lumpy projects—lenders need to forecast what you'll repay reliably.

The catch: RBF lenders want proof of concept first. They typically require months of revenue history and unit economics that already work. You can't walk in with an idea; you need paying customers. Understanding RBF now helps you plan: once your side project has revenue, you have a third option beyond 'bootstrap forever' or 'dilute your equity.'

> **Key idea.** Trade a percentage of future revenue for capital now, keep your equity, repay only when you earn.

**What it changes for you.** Once your side project has revenue, RBF gives you capital to scale without diluting equity or risking fixed debt payments.




## 2 · Film

### The Founder
<sub>John Lee Hancock · 2016 · `financing` · film</sub>

McDonald's value came not from hamburger quality or brand but from Kroc's control of real estate under each franchise. He shifted from extracting a percentage of franchisee sales to extracting fixed monthly rent, making franchisees permanently dependent. This created asymmetric leverage: Kroc profited passively regardless of franchisee success, risk, or product quality.

> **The one idea.** Control the bottleneck, not the commodity. Kroc won by owning land, not burgers—franchisees paid rent whether profitable or not.

**Verdict.** Worth ~2 hours for the mechanism it teaches. Better as instruction than entertainment. Watch for the moment Kroc shifts from service provider (taking sales %) to landlord (extracting fixed rent). That structural insight is the entire economic lesson.

**Caveat.** Dramatizes Kroc's insight as sudden epiphany (it was gradual). More critically: inverts the popular reading. Celebrated as self-made hustle, but teaches building asymmetric systems where you extract rent while others bear risk. Moral is structural ruthlessness, not entrepreneurial inspiration.
