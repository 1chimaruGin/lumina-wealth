### 1. Opportunity cost
<sub>`intelligence` · Intelligence — *How does money actually work?*</sub>

Opportunity cost is the return of the best alternative you did not choose. It never appears on a bill, which is why people ignore it, and why it usually decides the outcome.

Money makes it measurable. ¥1,000,000 at 5% a year is ¥1,050,000 after one year and about ¥2,653,000 after twenty (1.05^20 ≈ 2.653). Fees work the same way. A fund charging 1.5% a year instead of 0.5% does not cost you 1% once. It removes that 1% from every future year's compounding base, so the gap grows with the balance and is invisible on any single statement.

Time is the other cost. Eight hours a week is roughly 400 hours a year. Hours spent researching trades are hours not spent on skills that could raise your pay, or on rest. Estimate their yen value by dividing your annual salary by your annual working hours. You pay this cost even if you never place an order.

Markets show how large the gap can get. Barber and Odean (2000) found that the most active US retail households earned several percentage points a year less than the market over 1991 to 1996. Barber and colleagues (2014) found that only about 1% of Taiwanese day traders were reliably profitable after costs. These figures are approximate; check the papers.

The method is mechanical. For any choice, write the return of the best alternative, including the default of doing nothing. Then compare it with the option you are considering, after fees, tax and your time. Skipping that comparison means you are only seeing half the bill.

> **Key idea.** Every choice costs the best alternative you gave up, and that cost is usually invisible, so count it deliberately.

**Hard truth.** For someone with zero capital, the main cost is hours, and hours are your only input. Roughly nine in ten US active funds trailed their index over 15 years (S&P SPIVA, approximate). Professionals with full-time research mostly lost too, so a part-time trader starts further behind.

**What it changes for you.** Your eight weekly hours have a yen value; compare it against what any stream or study path would realistically return before committing to either.

**Check it yourself:** Find the 信託報酬 (annual fee, %) in the 目論見書 or fund page of any Japanese investment trust. Calculate 1,000,000 × 1.05^20 and 1,000,000 × (1.05 − f)^20, where f is that fee as a decimal. Subtract the second result from the first to see what that fee costs over 20 years at a 5% gross return.
### 2. Bonds: lending, and what you are pricing
<sub>`investing` · Investing — *What actually produces returns, and what only looks like it does?*</sub>

A bond is a loan you can sell. You pay a price, receive fixed coupons, and get principal back at maturity. Yield is the return you lock in only if you hold to maturity and every payment arrives. Prices and yields move in opposite directions: when market yields rise, existing bonds with lower coupons must fall in price until their yield matches. Three separate bets sit inside one instrument.

First, duration, which measures sensitivity to yield. Modified duration approximates the percentage price change for a one-point move in yield. A bond with duration 9 falls about 9% if yields rise one point. In 2022 the Bloomberg US Aggregate index fell about 13%, its worst year since the index began in 1976. Nobody defaulted; this was pure rate risk.

Second, credit: the spread over a government bond of similar maturity. It covers expected losses plus a risk premium. Long-run default rates are roughly 0.1% a year for investment-grade issuers and roughly 3 to 4% for high yield, approximate figures that swing with recessions. The spread is not free income; it is payment for losses that cluster in bad years.

Third, inflation. Real yield equals nominal yield minus expected inflation. A fixed coupon is a bet that inflation stays below what today's price already assumes. Inflation hits real returns on every coupon, whether or not any issuer fails.

Japan adds a twist. Yen government bonds carry almost no default risk, but their duration risk is fully yours. When the Bank of Japan ended negative rates in March 2024, long JGB prices moved down as yields rose, even though the state never missed a payment. Foreign bonds hedged into yen roughly earn your home short rate, because covered interest parity offsets the rate gap. The yield quoted on a US bond is not the yield you keep.

> **Key idea.** A bond is three bets, rate, credit and inflation, and its quoted yield only pays you if you survive each one.

**Hard truth.** A bond fund never matures, so its quoted yield is an estimate, and fees come straight off it. Returns above government rates are payment for duration or credit risk, mostly held by institutions with cheaper funding. Without capital, expect a modest, taxed, inflation-exposed return.

**What it changes for you.** If your variable home loan and savings already depend on Japanese rates, a yen bond fund adds more of the same rate bet rather than diversifying it.

### 3. Japan: taxes on side income
<sub>`management` · Management — *How do I run the money I have?*</sub>

The 20万円 line is a filing trigger, not an allowance. If you are salaried and your non-salary income is 200,000 yen or less after deducting necessary expenses, you do not file a 確定申告 for income tax on that income, provided nothing else requires a return. The test uses profit, not revenue: 300,000 yen of fees minus 150,000 yen of documented costs counts as 150,000. Above the line, you must file, and tax applies to the whole amount, not only the excess. Residence tax (住民税) is separate: the filing exemption does not remove the duty to declare side income to your municipality.

The category sets the mechanics. 事業所得 means business conducted repeatedly, with intent to profit, at a scale that looks like a business. It permits 青色申告 with a special deduction of up to 650,000 yen under double-entry bookkeeping with electronic filing, and losses can offset salary income. 雑所得 is the residual bucket; occasional or hobby-scale work usually lands there. It gets no 青色 deduction. Classification is decided by facts about your activity, not by what you call it.

Income in categories such as 雑所得 and 事業所得 stacks onto your salary and is taxed at your marginal rate: national income tax runs 5% to 45%, plus roughly 10% residence tax. Listed shares are the exception: flat 20.315% separate taxation applies to them, and crypto gains fall under progressive 雑所得. A rate that holds for one instrument does not transfer to another.

The 確定申告 window for the prior year is generally mid-February to 15 March. Choosing 普通徴収 for side income lets you pay its residence tax directly rather than through your employer, but it does not reduce the tax owed. Confirm current dates, the 20万円 rule, and the 青色 deduction on the NTA (国税庁) site and with your ward office, since these change.

> **Key idea.** The 20万円 line tests profit after expenses, not revenue, and it is a filing trigger, not a tax-free allowance.

**Hard truth.** Side income stacks on salary, so each extra yen is taxed at your marginal rate plus residence tax. Typical spare-time work often does not meet the 事業所得 test, which means the 65万円 deduction and loss offset usually stay out of reach.

**What it changes for you.** Your salary sets your marginal rate, so side income is taxed on top of it, and the 20万円 test uses profit after expenses, not revenue.

**Check it yourself:** Take 300,000 yen of side revenue with 120,000 yen of documented costs. Work out the figure the 20万円 test uses, then confirm on the NTA site whether a 確定申告 is required in that case and whether the municipal residence-tax declaration still applies.
