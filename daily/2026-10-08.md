### 1. Cash on the sidelines has a cost
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

When you hold cash waiting for a market correction or a better entry price, you're holding a position that typically returns less than the alternative. Here's the math: Japanese bank accounts currently earn 0.02% to 0.1% annually, depending on the bank. The Nikkei 225 has returned approximately 6-7% annually over the long term (though with significant volatility). The opportunity cost of waiting is roughly that 6-7% spread per year.

Suppose you have 500,000 JPY you plan to invest, but you're waiting for a 15% pullback that "should be coming soon." Five years pass without a crash. The market averages 5% per year. Your cash has now cost you approximately 140,000 JPY in foregone gains. The pullback comes for two weeks and recovers before you act. Either way, you've paid the cost.

This sharpens in real time. A 5% annual return is roughly 0.4% per month. Waiting one month for a "better setup" costs you, in expectation, 0.4% of your capital. Waiting six months costs about 2.4%. Over years, the bill compounds.

The mechanism is straightforward. Cash is safe but nearly worthless—it doesn't earn. Equities are volatile but have historically paid a premium. Waiting trades one risk (drawdowns) for another (missing gains). Most people underestimate the second because losses are visible and opportunity costs are invisible.

There is one conditional: if you genuinely have evidence (not hope) that a crash is imminent, waiting can win. But market timing requires skill most people don't have. The base rate is poor. Studies show most traders underperform a simple buy-and-hold strategy by 1-3% annually due to timing decisions. Waiting for a "perfect entry" usually means entering much later, or not at all.

> **Key idea.** Holding cash to time the market costs you the historical equity premium, typically 6-7% yearly, whether you win the timing bet or not.

**Hard truth.** You lack capital now, so this applies to your future. But the hard part: waiting for perfect entry costs compound returns. Timing the market is difficult; studies show most traders underperform buy-and-hold by 1-3% annually. Delayed entry usually costs more than the pullback would gain.

**What it changes for you.** As a salaried engineer, once you begin saving from salary, every month delayed compounds. Waiting for better entry will cost more than you gain.

**Check it yourself:** Find your bank's current savings rate (MUFG, Rakuten, SBI). Calculate one year of growth on 100,000 JPY. Compare to the Nikkei 225 return over the same year. The difference is what waiting cost you.
### 2. Asset allocation decides most of your outcome
<sub>`investing` · Investing — *What actually produces returns, and what only looks like it does?*</sub>

In 1986, analyst Gary Brinson studied large US pension funds and found that asset allocation—the stock-bond split—explained roughly 90% of the variation in returns across different portfolios, while individual security selection accounted for roughly 5% and market timing for nearly nothing. Later research confirmed this pattern.

The mechanism is direct. Assume stocks return 8% annually and bonds 3% (historical approximations). A portfolio 70% stocks and 30% bonds earns roughly 6% annually. A portfolio 30% stocks and 70% bonds earns roughly 4%. Over 20 years, ¥1,000,000 at the first rate becomes approximately ¥3.2M. At the second rate, ¥2.2M. The gap is ¥1M—a return entirely from the allocation decision, not from picking better stocks.

By contrast, which specific stocks you own within the 70% allocation matters far less. Owning Toyota instead of Sony, or choosing one index fund over another, might shift your annual return by 0.2 or 0.3 percentage points. This is noise.

Why this gap exists: stock prices move primarily on systematic risk—the broader market—and secondarily on idiosyncratic risk, that company's specific situation. Systematic risk dominates. Your allocation decision captures systematic risk. Security selection chases the smaller idiosyncratic piece.

The uncomfortable implication: most investors spend energy hunting undervalued stocks or backing funds when they should spend it on one decision: given your time horizon and genuine risk tolerance, what split between equities and bonds is correct for you? That decision determines 90% of outcome. The stock you pick is secondary.

For you now—with no capital deployed—this is theoretical. The knowledge yields no immediate return. But build the thinking habit now, before capital appears. When it does, you will already prioritize allocation over stock-picking.

> **Key idea.** Asset allocation—the stock-bond split—explains roughly 90% of return variation; security selection is secondary.

**Hard truth.** Asset allocation matters only with capital to deploy. You have none. A ¥1,000 portfolio split 70/30 versus 30/70 earns ¥20 annual difference. This is not income, not a return on your time, not actionable.

**What it changes for you.** Understanding allocation before you have capital means you will avoid chasing stock tips when income finally becomes available to invest.

**Check it yourself:** Calculate: ¥1,000,000 earning 6% annually for 20 years becomes ¥3.2M. The same amount earning 4% becomes ¥2.2M. The ¥1M difference is pure allocation advantage. Verify with any financial calculator.
### 3. Diversification and what it cannot protect you from
<sub>`investing` · Investing — *What actually produces returns, and what only looks like it does?*</sub>

Diversification removes idiosyncratic risk — the earnings miss, fraud, or management failure of a single company. It does nothing for systemic risk: the market-wide shocks that move asset prices down together.

When you own ten stocks in different sectors, normal volatility works as theory predicts: stock A down 30%, stock B up 15%, the diversified portfolio down far less. This works in stable years. It fails completely in crisis.

In March 2020, when COVID hit markets, the Nikkei 225 fell roughly 20% over five weeks. The critical detail: correlations between individual stocks moved from approximately 0.4 in January to near 0.9 by mid-March. Your ten Japanese stocks, your bonds, your emerging market holdings—all fell in lockstep. Your diversified portfolio fell almost as much as if you had held a single market index. Your diversification did nothing when you needed it most.

The 2008 financial crisis showed the same pattern, worse. From August 2008 to March 2009, correlations between U.S. equities and bonds shifted from mildly negative to near zero. Bonds stopped hedging stocks when you needed the hedge most. Anyone trusting a 60/40 stock-bond mix as "diversified" learned it offered no protection.

Systemic crashes happen on predictable base rates: roughly one 10%+ correction per year, one 20%+ every five years, one 50%+ every 30 years. You will live through several episodes.

Diversification compresses volatility in quiet years. But it is only an idiosyncratic-risk tool. Against the crash it looks designed to prevent, it barely works. You cannot diversify away market risk with more diversification. You can only reduce the amount of money you deploy into risky assets—an entirely different choice.

> **Key idea.** Diversification removes company-specific risk only; in crisis, correlations approach one and it protects against nothing systemic.

**Hard truth.** Diversification fails exactly when you need it. With zero capital to deploy, you cannot exploit the opportunities crashes create—the mechanism through which portfolios recover. This cost falls entirely on people without reserves.

**What it changes for you.** As a salaried engineer with no spare capital, diversification offers no safety in market crashes; capital reserves matter more than portfolio composition.

**Check it yourself:** Pull historical price data for Nikkei 225 and a Japanese government bond index from Yahoo Finance Japan; calculate correlation for January 2020 (normal) versus March 2020 (crash) to verify the rise from ~0.3 to ~0.8.

### Moneyball
<sub>Bennett Miller · 2011 · `investing` · film</sub>

Baseball markets systematically misprice players because scouts and teams collectively rely on the same cultural intuitions about talent. A budget-constrained team uses statistical analysis to identify undervalued players and builds competitively on minimal capital. The film dramatizes that valuation insight works operationally—they become competitive and reach the playoffs—but then ends without a championship, proving that being right about pricing does not determine victory.

> **The one idea.** Shared cognitive biases in a market create pricing gaps; analysis finds them but doesn't guarantee winning.

**Verdict.** Worth the hours for showing how systematic blindness creates market mispricing. Watch for the mechanism (unanimous reliance on the same heuristics) not the sports drama. Effective as instruction because the compelling narrative embeds the economics in memory; that's where its teaching value lives.

**Caveat.** Popular misreading suggests 'use data and win,' but the film shows 'correct valuation ≠ victory'—the actual lesson. Also hides implementation hardship and organizational resistance. Once markets recognize an arbitrage (as baseball did), it closes. This was 2002-specific inefficiency, not a universal market law.
