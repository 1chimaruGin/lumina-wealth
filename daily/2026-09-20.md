### 1. What money actually is
<sub>`intelligence` · Intelligence — *How does money actually work?*</sub>

Money does three distinct jobs: it's a unit of account, a medium of exchange, and a store of value. The order you list them in is not accidental. Each one is harder to build than the last, and crucially, something can work perfectly as money while failing catastrophically at the third task.

Start with unit of account. Before money, people used barter, which demands a "double coincidence of wants" — you have chickens, I have fish, but you want rice and I want cloth. We're stuck. Money solves this by establishing a shared standard that prices everything. The moment a community agrees "we measure value in shells" or "we measure value in yen," thousands of prices crystallize instantly. You now know exactly what your chickens are worth without negotiating each trade individually.

Medium of exchange follows naturally. Because everything has a price in that standard, you can trade your chickens for shells, then shells for rice, then rice for cloth. Money moves between people smoothly because everyone understands its value.

Store of value is the hardest. Money should preserve your purchasing power over time, but it often fails. Weimar hyperinflation (1923) destroyed the medium of exchange function — you couldn't store value in marks for more than days. Yet the unit of account function persisted: prices remained quoted in marks even as the currency's value collapsed. The hierarchy is real.

History shows this order repeatedly. Shells, beads, and cattle never held value perfectly — they rotted, broke, or escaped — yet they dominated as money in their societies because they solved the unit-of-account problem spectacularly. Everyone could see and value a cowry shell without debate. Only later did societies worry about whether the money retained value.

Modern money (like the yen) does all three reasonably well, which is why it seems natural to bundle them. But understanding that they're separate functions reveals something crucial: a payment system or earning stream can be real money for you even if the broader world questions whether it stores value.

> **Key idea.** Money is first a shared standard for pricing, second a tool for trading, and third a way to hold wealth — each harder than the last.

**Why it matters to you.** Most side-income ideas sound great in theory (unit of account) but never deliver actual cash (medium of exchange). This lesson teaches you to spot which actually will.

**Ask yourself:** Looking back at your last abandoned side project: was the earning opportunity actually illusory, or did you leave because reaching the first payment required more weeks of unpaid work than your present_bias could tolerate?

### 2. Loss aversion
<sub>`psychology` · Psychology — *How do I behave with money?*</sub>

The pain of losing money cuts deeper than the joy of gaining the same amount. This asymmetry was first documented systematically in 1979 by psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky in their prospect theory research. When they asked people to choose between a sure loss of ¥10,000 or a 50% chance of losing ¥20,000, most picked the gamble—even though the expected value was worse. The reverse: offered a sure gain of ¥10,000 or a 50% chance of ¥20,000, people chose safety. The principle: losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains feel good.

This matters acutely to selling decisions. If you bought an asset (stock, currency, a completed project) and it dropped in value, loss aversion pulls you to hold it, hoping recovery will erase the paper loss. Conversely, when something gains value, you're tempted to sell early, locking in the win to avoid the risk of loss. This pattern—holding losers and selling winners—destroys returns over time.

The mechanism is partly psychological (your brain treats loss as a threat) and partly accounting-based: you're anchored to your purchase price and treat anything below it as a loss to avoid. Crucially, this doesn't require actual cash loss. If you spent three weeks building a feature that isn't gaining traction, the time already spent feels like a loss, and cutting it loose triggers the same pain as a cash loss.

In practice, the best traders and entrepreneurs train themselves to ignore sunk costs (time, money already gone) and decide purely on future value: "If I didn't already own this, would I buy it now at this price?" Loss aversion tricks you into answering "yes" to losers and "no" to winners—the opposite of optimal. Recognition is half the fix: notice when you're holding onto a project or decision because of past investment rather than future potential.

> **Key idea.** Losses feel about twice as painful as equivalent gains feel good, causing you to hold losers and sell winners.

**Why it matters to you.** Loss aversion delays your side-income discovery—you either cling to stalling projects or flee them too fast, wasting your eight limited hours weekly.

**Ask yourself:** Think back to when you switched between your last two side projects: which felt stronger—the pull of the new idea, or the push from hours already spent?
