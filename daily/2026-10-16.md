### 1. Patience is the edge actually available to you
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

Holding a position for 20 years costs roughly 1/20th the taxes and fees of rebalancing monthly. On a ¥100,000 position, a single trade costs roughly ¥99 on major Japanese brokers (Rakuten, IB). Rebalance 12 times per year and you're paying ~1.2% annually in trading fees alone. For capital gains on Japanese-listed equities, add 20.315% tax on profits (verify current rates with NTA, as they change; other instruments like crypto are taxed differently).

Vanguard research showed that the average investor lags the market by 1–2% annually—not because markets are rigged, but because investors trade too much, buying high and selling low, while paying transaction costs and taxes. Over 20 years, a 2% annual drag compounds to roughly 30–40% total underperformance. Holding eliminates both fees and most tax events.

The edge is clear and old: compound growth erases timing mistakes. Hold for 20 years and you benefit from returns on returns, compounding invisibly. Trade monthly and every trade is a tax event and a fee event, chipping away at your growth.

But here is the hard part: this edge only compounds money you already own. You have zero capital. That makes patience theoretical for you right now. The edge is future-facing.

The cost of delaying deployment (once you have capital) is real: ¥100,000 invested at roughly 7% annual returns (historical average for global equities, not guaranteed) becomes ~¥385,000 over 20 years. Delay one year and you lose roughly ¥25,000 in final value. But you cannot incur that cost until you have capital to delay.

Your current edge is discipline, not patience. Build the habit of holding now, in theory, so when capital arrives you already know how to sit still through downturns. Most retail investors crack and trade. If you train yourself to be different before real money is on the line, you'll have an edge.

> **Key idea.** Holding beats trading; every trade is a tax event and a fee event. This edge only compounds capital you already own.

**Hard truth.** Patience only compounds capital you deploy. With zero capital, this edge is deferred. Literature on long-term investing assumes you have money to hold—you don't yet. Years learning about this now are sunk cost unless they build psychological readiness for later.

**What it changes for you.** Your constraint now is capital, not discipline. Holding matters later. Build the habit of patience before you have money to lose it on trading.

**Check it yourself:** Pick your intended broker and position size. Calculate annual fee drag: (per-trade fee) × (12 monthly trades) ÷ (position size) = annual drag %. For ¥99 fee on ¥50,000 position, that's 2.4% drag—before you earn any returns.
### 2. Expectancy: edge times frequency minus costs
<sub>`markets` · Markets & Trading — *Who is on the other side, and why do they expect to win?*</sub>

Expectancy per trade = (win rate × average win) − (loss rate × average loss) − round-trip cost. Multiply by the number of trades in the period. Frequency does not create an edge. It multiplies whatever the sign already is.

Hypothetical example: a strategy wins 45% of the time, averaging +2.0%, and loses 55% of the time, averaging −1.5%. Gross expectancy: (0.45 × 2.0) − (0.55 × 1.5) = 0.90 − 0.825 = +0.075% per trade. That looks like a small edge. Now charge a round-trip cost of 0.20% for the bid-ask spread crossed twice, slippage and fees. Net expectancy: −0.125% per trade. At 200 trades a year, that is about −25% of capital before compounding. The strategy only works on paper that ignores costs.

Notice the proportions. The gross edge was 0.075%, and costs were 0.20%, almost three times larger. Costs are charged on every trade whether it wins or loses, so a high-frequency approach pays its toll more often.

Who is on the other side? Usually market makers and institutions that see more order flow, pay tighter spreads and execute faster. Your strategy has to beat their costs and information, not just the gross number.

The base rates are unkind. Barber and Odean (2000) studied US discount-brokerage households from 1991 to 1996. The most active traders earned about 11.4% a year, against 17.9% for the market. A 2019 study of Brazilian day traders by Chague and colleagues found that roughly 97% of those who kept trading for more than 300 days lost money.

Most people never measure the inputs: win rate, payoff size and cost. They remember the winners.

> **Key idea.** A strategy makes money only if its average net return per trade, after all costs, times trade count is positive; frequency amplifies the sign.

**Hard truth.** Without an informational or cost advantage, the expected net result of retail trading is negative. The apparent edge is usually a measurement error: unrecorded costs and remembered wins. Most people trading this way lose money, and you are unlikely to be the exception.

**What it changes for you.** Any short-term trading you do costs you evenings and spread on every trade, so judge it by net expectancy after costs, not by how often you win.

**Check it yourself:** Look up the current bid and ask for a Japanese stock you would consider trading, and the commission schedule of a broker you could use. Compute the round-trip cost (spread plus a buy and a sell commission) as a percentage of a 500,000 JPY position.
### 3. Present value and discounting
<sub>`intelligence` · Intelligence — *How does money actually work?*</sub>

A ¥1,000,000 payment due in ten years is worth ¥1,000,000 divided by (1 + r) to the power of 10, where r is the return available on money of equal risk. At r = 1%, it is worth ¥905,287 today. At 2%, ¥820,348. At 5%, ¥613,913. The exponent amplifies small differences: moving from 2% to 5% removes ¥206,435 of value, about a fifth of the face amount.

The rate is not a preference. It is the return the market demands for bearing that specific risk. A government bond that pays on schedule carries a low r; a company's future profits, which may never arrive, carries a high one. So a share price is the present value of expected future cash flows. Change r and the price moves even when the company's cash flows do not change at all. That is why bond prices fall when yields rise, and why long-dated assets are the most sensitive.

The same arithmetic runs forwards as compounding. ¥100,000 at 5% for ten years becomes about ¥162,890. Discounting and compounding are one formula read in two directions, so any 'cheap' future payment is only cheap relative to the rate you insist on. Choosing that rate is the whole argument: a rate of 0% makes ¥1,000,000 in 2036 equal to today's ¥1,000,000, which is almost never the right assumption.

Price is the discounted sum of expected cash flows, so it can stay low for decades when either the cash flows or the rate disappoint. The Nikkei 225 closed at 38,915 on 29 December 1989 and did not regain that closing high until 22 February 2024, about 34 years later, in nominal yen.

> **Key idea.** A future payment is worth its face value divided by what equally risky money earns, compounded over the wait.

**Hard truth.** The rate is the market's consensus, so discounting gives no edge. Beating it after fees is hard: SPIVA US data show roughly nine in ten active large-cap funds trailing their index over fifteen years (approximate). Without savings you cannot buy the average either.

**What it changes for you.** Pay raises and bonuses arriving over years are worth less than their headline yen, so compare any multi-year offer by present value.

**Check it yourself:** Look up the Japanese 10-year government bond yield for a recent date on the Ministry of Finance's interest rate data page (財務省 金利情報), then compute ¥1,000,000 ÷ (1 + yield)^10. Report the result and the yield you used.

### Liar Game
<sub>Shinobu Kaitani · 2005 · `psychology` · manga</sub>

The series stages a sequence of rule-bound games in which players negotiate, promise and accuse while the organiser fixes every payout in advance. It dramatises why cooperation looks stable when defection is visibly costly and collapses once one player can lie without consequence, which is the prisoner's dilemma with cheap talk and debt-style stakes. The drama turns on which rules actually bind speech and which leave it free, so the mechanism is about enforceability more than deception.

> **The one idea.** Cooperation is only as stable as the cost of defecting: when rules make lying detectable and punished, trust becomes rational; when they don't, one defector collapses the whole arrangement.

**Verdict.** Worth the hours as entertainment-first intuition for the prisoner's dilemma, cheap talk and defection in finite games, not as instruction about markets or money. Watch for the moment a rule defines what counts as a lie or a binding promise, and ask whether the defector's payoff is capped; that is where the game theory…

**Caveat.** Games are finite, fully specified and designer-enforced, so endgame logic is far cleaner than real markets. Single clever moves decide outcomes, and protagonists win where real players rarely do. Read as a deception manual, it inverts: its structure rewards enforceable commitments, not lies.
