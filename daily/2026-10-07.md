### 1. How much of Buffett's wealth arrived after 60
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

Warren Buffett's net worth at age 60 in 1990 was approximately $3.6 billion. By age 93 in 2023, his peak wealth reached approximately $138 billion. Roughly 97% of his wealth accumulated after his 60th birthday. The mechanism: $3.6 billion compounding at 15% annually for 33 years produces returns that dwarf any salary-based savings.

Buffett did not become smarter after 60. Compound returns on large capital simply exceed human earning and saving rates. A $3.6 billion base earning 15% annually generates $540 million in returns—more than most people earn in lifetimes.

Buffett's advantage: he started at age 11, deployed substantial capital by age 30, and benefited from approximately 60 years of US equity appreciation. The S&P 500 delivered roughly 10% nominal annual returns (1926–2023), though Berkshire outperformed.

For a salaried engineer in Tokyo with zero capital today: the compound interest mechanism remains true, but starting conditions are reversed. Japanese equities averaged roughly 3–4% real annual returns over three decades. If you save ¥2 million yearly—an aggressive target—and invest at 5% real returns, after 35 years you accumulate approximately ¥150 million in today's money. Buffett's ¥400 billion (1990 equivalent) growing at 15% becomes ¥2 trillion.

The harsh asymmetry: compound interest is a multiplier on existing capital, not a generator of capital from zero. Buffett's wealth curve is exponential because the base was large. Your wealth curve, starting from salary, will be roughly linear for decades before any exponential phase begins. You must save aggressively and wait longer than Buffett to reach a capital base large enough for compounding to dominate your wealth growth.

> **Key idea.** Compound returns multiply large capital, but starting near-zero means time alone cannot replicate exponential wealth growth within one career.

**Hard truth.** Buffett required $3.6 billion capital and 60 years of prior accumulation to reach $138 billion. You have zero yen and cannot replicate his returns. Decades of salary-based saving will produce linear growth at best, not exponential wealth.

**What it changes for you.** Shows why salary-based wealth in Tokyo compounds slowly; decades of saving create capital, but exponential growth requires starting capital.

**Check it yourself:** Look up Buffett's estimated net worth in 1990 and 2023. Calculate wealth created after age 60 as a percentage of his total wealth. Verify it exceeds 95%.
### 2. Diocletian's Edict on Maximum Prices
<sub>`history` · History & Stories — *Who did this before, and what happened?*</sub>

Diocletian, Roman Emperor from 284–305 AD, watched his currency collapse. The denarius had been progressively debased throughout the century: where it once held silver as its primary component, by Diocletian's reign it was mostly base metal with a thin silver coating. Prices reflected this collapse in currency value: wheat, oil, and cloth all surged. In 301 AD, he issued the Edict on Maximum Prices—an empire-wide decree with an extensive price list, attempting to freeze goods and services at levels roughly 10–15 years prior.

It failed within weeks.

Merchants stopped bringing goods to market. Why sell grain at the legal price when you cannot cover production costs or pay workers? Hoarding exploded. Those with spare capital locked goods away, betting on the edict's collapse. Black markets emerged instantly—higher prices, but at least prices that kept trade moving.

Diocletian had diagnosed the symptom, not the disease. Prices were high because the denarius was nearly worthless. His edict did not create new silver or give the currency value. It simply made legal trade unprofitable. The edict was abandoned within a year or two, but the damage was done: market channels fractured, trust eroded, and ordinary people lost access to goods at any price.

Three observations: First, price controls work temporarily only when the underlying currency is stable and supply elastic. Second, when governments panic about price, they almost always attack the symptom rather than the cause—in Diocletian's time, currency debasement; in modern terms, monetary expansion. Third, and most brutal: people prefer to not trade than to trade at a loss. The edict did not bring prices down. It brought trade to an end.

> **Key idea.** When governments freeze prices below cost, suppliers disappear. The market stops working before prices come down.

**Hard truth.** Diocletian's price controls destroyed legal markets within weeks. Those without capital to access black markets lost access to goods entirely. The shortage was worse than the inflation. Price controls starve supply faster than they lower prices.

**What it changes for you.** Price controls reveal which government policies attack symptoms, not causes—crucial for understanding whether inflation or deflation policy will actually work.

**Check it yourself:** Search for 'Roman denarius silver content 300 AD' and compare to 280 AD in academic sources—the dramatic decline explains the price rises Diocletian tried to freeze.
### 3. Real versus nominal
<sub>`intelligence` · Intelligence — *How does money actually work?*</sub>

Nominal is what the number says. Real is what it buys.

Your salary increases 3% annually. Nominal gain: 3%. But if inflation runs 2%, your real gain is 0.97% (1.03 ÷ 1.02 - 1). If inflation hits 3%, you gained zero in purchasing power—your actual income fell. If inflation is 4%, you lost ground, even though the offer letter showed a raise.

This distinction destroys most salary negotiations and investment analysis. It is the single most expensive mistake people make about their own finances.

Japan's inflation averaged 0.3% annually from 1995 to 2019. It accelerated to 2.5% by 2022 and 2.2% in 2023 (national CPI). During those low-inflation years, a software engineer getting 2% annual raises was actually getting 1.7% real gains—barely ahead. But the real cost came when inflation shifted: if you negotiated based on nominal gains and didn't track CPI, you walked into years where a 2% raise meant a real loss.

The second hard problem: you cannot observe real returns without subtracting inflation yourself. Brokerages, employers, government statistics—all report in nominal terms. A brokerage statement showing +7% annual gain looks strong until you subtract 5% inflation: +2% real, a cost you had to calculate yourself. Most people never do.

For international assets, a third layer exists: currency. A stock returning +5% in USD but the yen appreciating 2% against the dollar means your yen-denominated gain is only 2.9% real in yen terms (before Japanese tax). Again, nominal looks different from real.

The mechanism is this: deflation is rare and painful. Inflation is the standard. Any nominal return below inflation is a real loss. Any salary growth below inflation is a pay cut disguised as a raise. Nobody tells you this because employers benefit—they raise salaries nominally (good headlines) while inflation silently erodes your purchasing power.

> **Key idea.** Nominal returns are what the number says; real returns subtract inflation. Most people track only nominal, missing the actual loss.

**Hard truth.** Nominal wage growth below inflation is a pay cut. Without capital, you cannot invest your way out of it. Employers benefit from this gap. Understanding real returns requires doing the math yourself—nobody profits by showing you the truth.

**What it changes for you.** Your salary negotiations hinge on this. A 2% raise with 3% inflation is a real pay cut, invisible without the math.

**Check it yourself:** Look up Japan's CPI for the last three years on jstat.go.jp (national CPI, monthly data). Compare it to your actual salary growth year-on-year. Calculate: (salary year 2 / salary year 1 − 1) minus (CPI year 2 / CPI year 1 − 1). Is your real growth positive or negative?
