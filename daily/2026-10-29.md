### 1. Lump sum versus dollar-cost averaging
<sub>`investing` · Investing — *What actually produces returns, and what only looks like it does?*</sub>

Lump sum means investing all of a sum on day one. Dollar-cost averaging (DCA) means investing the same sum in equal instalments, typically over six to twelve months. Vanguard's 2012 study, "Dollar-cost averaging just means taking risk later", tested both against rolling historical periods in the US, UK and Australia, using a 60/40 stock-and-bond portfolio and a 12-month DCA schedule. Lump sum came out ahead about two-thirds of the time. DCA is usually sold as a safer way to invest a windfall.

The mechanism is arithmetic. Equity markets have risen in more months than they have fallen, so money waiting to be invested earns close to nothing instead of the market's drift. On a 12-month schedule, the average dollar sits idle for about six months. That forgone drift is DCA's cost. DCA is not a return strategy. It narrows the spread of outcomes, reducing losses if the market falls just after you invest, and it equally reduces gains if it rises.

Whether that cost is worth paying depends on what you are insuring against. The data cannot answer that part. It can only price the insurance.

Read the sample carefully. Those windows were mostly rising markets, and a historical average is not a forecast. Lump sum's losses in the worst windows are larger than DCA's, and a single percentage hides that spread. Look at the full distribution before treating two-thirds as a verdict.

Both approaches hold the same asset. They differ only in when the market risk is taken on. Expense ratios and taxes are paid regardless of which schedule you choose.

> **Key idea.** DCA does not raise expected return; it trades expected return for lower regret, and the price is measurable.

**Hard truth.** Lump sum's bad case is real: buying the Nikkei 225 at its December 1989 peak meant waiting until February 2024 to break even, on price alone. Neither method adds return; both only change when you take the risk.

**What it changes for you.** Your salary surplus arrives monthly, so it is already dollar-cost averaged; the lump-sum question only arises with bonuses or savings you hold as cash.

**Check it yourself:** Find the Nikkei 225 closing levels for the last trading day of 1989 and for 22 February 2024 in JPX or Nikkei index history. Compute the annualised price-only return across that period, excluding dividends.
### 2. Pricing your work
<sub>`management` · Management — *How do I run the money I have?*</sub>

Three methods, each answering a different question.

Cost-plus: price equals delivery cost plus a margin. It answers 'what stops me losing money?' It ignores what the buyer will pay, so it underprices valuable output and overprices commodity work.

Market rate: price equals what comparable sellers charge. It answers 'what will the buyer accept?' This is the default for most freelance work because it is visible. Japanese agencies publish rate ranges, but these are advertised figures, not transaction data. Treat them as asking prices, not medians.

Value-based: price equals a share of the gain the buyer receives. It answers 'what is the outcome worth to them?' It carries the highest margin and the hardest justification, because the buyer must accept your estimate of their gain.

Engineers underprice because they start from cost, which is hours, and hours cap income. Marn and Rosiello (Harvard Business Review, 1992) estimated that a 1% price improvement, with volume unchanged, raised operating profit by roughly 8.7% for a median S&P 500 company. That is one illustrative model, so treat the figure as approximate. The direction is the robust part: small price changes move profit far more than equal-sized cost cuts.

At 8 hours a week, your only levers are rate and scope. A higher rate on identical hours is pure margin.

Regulation adds a floor. Japan's Freelance Act (特定受託事業者に係る取引の適正化等に関する法律, in force 1 November 2024) prohibits buyers from 買いたたき, setting unreasonably low prices. It constrains the buyer's conduct, not the price you can ask. Confirm the current text on the JFTC site.

> **Key idea.** Price is set by what the buyer can get elsewhere and what the outcome is worth to them; your cost and hours only set the floor.

**Hard truth.** Most solo sellers get market rate or less. Above-market pricing needs scarce skill, a track record, and a buyer who can measure results. I cannot cite a reliable base rate for how often this succeeds in year one; assume rarely.

**What it changes for you.** Your 8 weekly hours cap any hourly model, so each yen of rate is the lever that matters; check your employer's side-work rules before pricing anything externally.

**Check it yourself:** Work out how many hours a week you would need at ¥5,000 to match your income at 8 hours a week at ¥7,500, over 48 working weeks. Then compare that with 8.
### 3. Options: what you are actually buying
<sub>`markets` · Markets & Trading — *Who is on the other side, and why do they expect to win?*</sub>

An option is a contract that gives its buyer the right, not the obligation, to buy (a call) or sell (a put) an asset at a fixed strike price on or before an expiry date. The buyer pays a premium up front, and that premium is the buyer's maximum loss. The seller keeps the premium and takes on the obligation.

The premium has two parts. Intrinsic value is what the option would pay if exercised now: a call struck at 100 on a stock trading at 110 has 10 of it. Time value is the rest, the price of the chance that the stock moves further before expiry. Only time value decays, and that decay is what the seller collects.

Decay is not linear. For an at-the-money option, a common approximation is value ≈ 0.4 × price × volatility × √(years to expiry). Take a 100 stock with 30% annual volatility: one year gives about 12, three months about 6, one month about 3.5. Value falls fastest near expiry. The buyer pays this decay every day, whether or not the stock moves. The approximation holds only near the money; deep in or out of the money, behaviour differs.

Who is on the other side? Mostly market makers and institutions that sell options and hedge by trading the underlying. They collect a variance risk premium: on average, implied volatility (what options price in) has exceeded the volatility that subsequently occurs. Coval and Shumway (2001) found that at-the-money S&P 500 futures straddles, buying both a call and a put, lost money on average. The buyer must make the move exceed the premium and costs, before a deadline.

> **Key idea.** An option is a priced right with a deadline; the buyer pays time decay to the seller for the chance of a large move.

**Hard truth.** Buyers need a large move before expiry; many short-dated options expire worthless (figures vary by study). Selling is the same bet from the other side, needing margin you cannot post. Without capital, it is a fast way to lose a fixed sum.

**What it changes for you.** For you, options are a pricing lesson, not an income route; reading free chains costs nothing, but Japanese tax treatment differs by product and venue, so confirm with NTA.

### Winning the Loser's Game
<sub>Charles Ellis · 1998 · `investing` · book</sub>

Ellis argues that once professional investors dominate trading, active management becomes a loser's game: before costs, the average dollar earns roughly the market return, so after fees and trading costs it earns less. Winning therefore depends on avoiding unforced errors rather than out-skilling rivals, in contrast to the older winner's game where skill decides outcomes. His prescriptions are low costs, long horizons, indexing for the core holdings, and treating the policy asset mix as the main decision.

> **The one idea.** Most investors lose through costs and self-inflicted mistakes, not through lack of brilliance; cutting both is the controllable edge.
