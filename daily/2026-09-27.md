### 1. Timing and patience are opposites
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

Timing and patience describe opposite positions, but most investors conflate them into a single false activity: "wait for the right moment, then hold." That's neither.

Timing is the prediction that you can identify when prices will move and act on that prediction. Patience is the decision to hold regardless of price and wait for the underlying business to compound. They are mutually exclusive.

The evidence on timing is direct. Retail investors who trade actively underperform buy-and-hold index returns by 3-4% annually, after fees and taxes. The S&P 500 illustrates the cost of trying: over the past 30 years, missing just the 10 best days cut annualized returns from approximately 10% to 4%. Those 10 days were unannounced and unpredictable in advance.

The mechanism destroying returns is multi-part. Each trade costs transaction fees: a typical buy or sell in Japan incurs 0.10-0.20% in costs. Four trades per year eats 0.4-0.8% of performance before tax. In Japan, capital gains on listed securities incur 20.315% flat tax (源泉徴収 on 上場株式); confirm against current NTA guidance as rates change. The compounding effect of paying tax less frequently is substantial. Worse, the act of constant monitoring triggers poor decisions: research in behavioral finance documents that investors who check portfolios daily make worse decisions than those checking quarterly. They sell low after panic and buy high after euphoria.

Patience produces the opposite outcome. It requires holding through losses, price declines, bear markets. There is no daily feedback. There is no visible progress for months. But if the underlying company or index increases in real value, compounding works alone.

You cannot optimise for both. Activity-seeking leads to timing. Patience leads to returns. The conflict is not intellectual—it is behavioral.

> **Key idea.** Timing predicts price moves; patience ignores them. Most who try timing destroy returns via fees, taxes, and decisions driven by daily price changes.

**Hard truth.** Patience produces no daily wins—months pass with no feedback. Timing destroys 3-4% annually through fees, taxes, and poor decisions. You cannot optimize for both. The pattern you choose now, without capital, will dictate your results once capital arrives.

**What it changes for you.** Salary compounds automatically without daily decisions; market timing requires constant activity but destroys returns via fees, taxes, and decisions driven by daily price movement.

**Check it yourself:** Calculate: (brokerage buy-sell fee × 4 trades per year) ÷ investment amount × 100 = annual % cost. This is the tax-free cost of timing, before any wrong calls.
### 2. Who is on the other side of your trade
<sub>`markets` · Markets & Trading — *Who is on the other side, and why do they expect to win?*</sub>

When you place a buy order on the Tokyo Stock Exchange, a market maker profits on the spread before you earn a yen on the trade itself. For Nikkei 225 constituents, the spread is typically 1–2 yen per share. For less liquid stocks, 10–100 yen is common. A 50 yen spread on a 1000 yen stock is 5%. If the stock appreciates 5%, the spread has already consumed your entire gain.

The person on the other side of your trade is often not another investor—it is a market maker, an algorithmic trader, or an institution. They expect to win not by predicting direction but by exploiting structural advantages:

Latency: High-frequency traders execute in microseconds. Your retail order takes 100–500 milliseconds to reach the exchange. Informed traders have already repositioned.

Information: Institutions pay for Bloomberg terminals ($24,000 per year), real-time earnings feeds, and alternative data. Public news is already priced in by the time it reaches you.

Costs: Institutional investors pay 0.01–0.05% in commissions and receive price improvement. Retail investors in Japan typically pay 0.1–0.5% or flat fees—higher in percentage terms on smaller trades. These costs compound over a year.

The outcome is measurable. Studies on retail day traders consistently show 90–97% lose money. This is not emotion or lack of discipline. It is the mathematical result of a cost disadvantage and an information disadvantage playing out simultaneously across thousands of trades.

If your counterparty is another retail trader, neither of you has an advantage beyond luck. One will cover the market maker's spread by selling at the ask when they should have waited.

> **Key idea.** Your counterparty has lower costs, faster information, or both—and the spread is their profit whether you win or lose.

**Hard truth.** Market makers profit on spreads alone; institutions exploit information and latency edges. Retail traders lose 90–97% of day trading attempts. Without capital or institutional access, you are not investing—you are transferring money to firms with structural advantages.

**What it changes for you.** As a salaried engineer with zero capital, trading is not your competitive advantage; understanding why most traders lose is more valuable than trying to be the exception.

**Check it yourself:** Check the bid-ask spread on any TSE-listed stock on your trading platform. Calculate round-trip cost: (spread × 2) ÷ current price × 100 = percentage cost per trade. For a 1000 yen stock with 2 yen spread: (2 × 2) ÷ 1000 × 100 = 0.4% per trade. Ten trades = 4% in costs alone.
### 3. Time in the market versus timing the market
<sub>`time` · Time & Patience — *What does waiting actually cost, and what does it buy?*</sub>

Missing the 10 best trading days in the S&P 500 over 20 years would have cut your return from roughly 10% annualized to 5%—halving your wealth gain. This statistic underpins the argument that market timing is futile; you should stay invested.

But the mechanics reverse the claim. Those 10 best days clustered within weeks of crashes: March 2009 (financial crisis bottom), March 2020 (COVID crash), November 2022 (inflation peak). The study measures what happens if you accidentally miss days. It does not measure what happens if you time both exit and re-entry correctly—because that is hard. If you had successfully stayed out through crashes, you would have automatically missed most rebounds. The study is right about the outcome; it is wrong about why: most people fail not because timing is impossible, but because they panic-trade.

Second, the study is S&P 500 only. The Tokyo Stock Exchange returned approximately zero from 1990 to 2010. Time in market during Japan's lost decade meant years of losses, not gains. Which market you select matters more than your entry timing.

Third—and this matters most—you have zero spare capital. If you invest salary regularly into an index, you are already solving the timing problem. You enter at 12 to 24 different prices per year through dollar-cost averaging. The 'time in market versus timing the market' debate assumes you have a lump sum to deploy. It is irrelevant to you.

Fourth: tax structure in Japan makes frequent trading expensive. Listed equity capital gains face 20.315% tax. Day-trading gains classified as 雑所得 reach 45% tax. Transaction costs stack on top. The tax differential (roughly $2,400 on $10,000 of gains) exceeds what most timing strategies produce.

The hard truth: people fail at timing because they panic-sell and panic-buy, crystallizing small losses into permanent ones while paying tax and fees. The arithmetic is correct. The application is wrong for people like you—salary-based investors already have timing solved through averaging.

> **Key idea.** Most people fail at market timing due to panic-trading and taxes, not because timing is impossible.

**Hard truth.** Zero spare capital makes this debate moot. Salary-based investing already solves timing through averaging. Most failures are panic-trades turning small losses permanent via taxes. Japan's Nikkei was flat for 30 years despite buy-and-hold.

**What it changes for you.** Salary-based monthly investing already solves entry timing through averaging. Whether to day-trade depends on tax efficiency, not market-timing ability.

**Check it yourself:** Calculate the after-tax return on $10,000 of gains under Japan's 20.315% listed-equity capital gains tax versus 45% 雑所得 tax. The difference (roughly $2,400) exceeds what most market-timing strategies produce after transaction costs.

### The Psychology of Money
<sub>Morgan Housel · 2020 · `psychology` · book</sub>

The book argues that financial success depends less on technical knowledge or asset selection and more on emotional discipline, patience, and behavioral consistency. It contends that luck plays a larger role than people admit, that time horizons matter more than timing decisions, and that avoiding catastrophic losses outweighs chasing gains. Personal finance outcomes hinge on psychology—emotional tolerance for volatility, willingness to hold through downturns, understanding your own risk tolerance—not on investing expertise.

> **The one idea.** Behavioral consistency and emotional discipline over decades outweighs any technical strategy or stock-picking skill.

**Verdict.** Worth 5–6 hours for the psychology angle; insufficient if mechanics are your focus. Read chapters 1–7 (core ideas), skim chapter 8 onward (extended examples). Better as supplement than primary source on how markets work.

**Caveat.** Silent on market mechanics, market structure, crypto, and financial history depth. Conflates luck so heavily readers may underestimate learning actual finance. Bias toward passive indexing without comparing alternatives. Motivational coaching rather than instruction for mechanical depth.
