# The endowment effect

The endowment effect is the gap between what you will pay for something and what you demand to sell it back. Knetsch (1989): students who received mugs later refused to trade them at prices that non-owners readily accepted. Ownership inflated the mug's value by roughly 2x in their heads, independent of any rational basis.

The mechanism is loss aversion. Neuroscience shows people feel losses about 2.5 times more sharply than equivalent gains. Owning something makes giving it up register as a loss. Your brain opposes the sale more fiercely than it opposes a missed purchase.

Ideas and unfinished projects suffer the same bias. The moment you internalize 'this is my project,' its value inflates in your head. A half-built side project feels worth protecting. A startup idea feels like property. Abandoning it feels more costly than the time you spend on it—even when you could charge a client more per hour for that same work.

This pattern persists across decades and contexts: eBay sellers overprice relative to market clearance; negotiators inflate their own team's contribution; hiring managers overvalue candidates they already know. Ownership consistently raises valuation by 50% or more.

You report dropping projects and chasing new ideas, but captured ideas stick—not from merit, but because you own them. You experience loss when considering abandonment that a non-owner would not feel.

With 8 hours weekly and no spare capital, time is your only renewable resource. Every hour anchored to a mediocre project is an hour not learning markets, which is your stated priority. Loss aversion locks you into sunk costs longer than rational economics would predict.

**Key idea.** Once you own an idea, loss aversion raises its value in your head by 2x—locking you into projects the market values lower.

**Hard truth.** Endowment bias is consistent and broad-based—most people never escape it. For zero-capital people, this is brutal: time is your only asset, and loss aversion locks it into low-ROI projects. Escaping it requires external discipline most people lack.
