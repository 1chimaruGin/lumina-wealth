# Time in the market versus timing the market

Missing the 10 best trading days in the S&P 500 over 20 years would have cut your return from roughly 10% annualized to 5%—halving your wealth gain. This statistic underpins the argument that market timing is futile; you should stay invested.

But the mechanics reverse the claim. Those 10 best days clustered within weeks of crashes: March 2009 (financial crisis bottom), March 2020 (COVID crash), November 2022 (inflation peak). The study measures what happens if you accidentally miss days. It does not measure what happens if you time both exit and re-entry correctly—because that is hard. If you had successfully stayed out through crashes, you would have automatically missed most rebounds. The study is right about the outcome; it is wrong about why: most people fail not because timing is impossible, but because they panic-trade.

Second, the study is S&P 500 only. The Tokyo Stock Exchange returned approximately zero from 1990 to 2010. Time in market during Japan's lost decade meant years of losses, not gains. Which market you select matters more than your entry timing.

Third—and this matters most—you have zero spare capital. If you invest salary regularly into an index, you are already solving the timing problem. You enter at 12 to 24 different prices per year through dollar-cost averaging. The 'time in market versus timing the market' debate assumes you have a lump sum to deploy. It is irrelevant to you.

Fourth: tax structure in Japan makes frequent trading expensive. Listed equity capital gains face 20.315% tax. Day-trading gains classified as 雑所得 reach 45% tax. Transaction costs stack on top. The tax differential (roughly $2,400 on $10,000 of gains) exceeds what most timing strategies produce.

The hard truth: people fail at timing because they panic-sell and panic-buy, crystallizing small losses into permanent ones while paying tax and fees. The arithmetic is correct. The application is wrong for people like you—salary-based investors already have timing solved through averaging.

**Key idea.** Most people fail at market timing due to panic-trading and taxes, not because timing is impossible.

**Hard truth.** Zero spare capital makes this debate moot. Salary-based investing already solves timing through averaging. Most failures are panic-trades turning small losses permanent via taxes. Japan's Nikkei was flat for 30 years despite buy-and-hold.
