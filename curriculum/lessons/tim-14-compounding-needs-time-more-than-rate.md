# Compounding needs time more than rate

$10,000 at 5% annually for 30 years reaches $43,219. The same $10,000 at 15% annually for 10 years reaches $40,454. Time beats rate—mediocre returns win. This is taught everywhere as proof that patience compounds wealth. It's also almost entirely irrelevant to you right now.

Compounding only works if you have capital. You don't—you have a salary. Salary is linear income, not exponential growth. A salaried engineer earning roughly ¥6M per year accumulates ¥6M per year. After 10 years, ¥60M. After 30 years, ¥180M (before tax, living costs, or anything else). That's addition, not compounding.

If you invest ¥600,000 annually at 5% over 30 years, you end with roughly ¥40M from ¥18M of deposits. The gain from compounding is ¥22M—real money. But your salary over the same 30 years is ¥180M. The returns from compounding are dwarfed by the income from work. The growth is dominated by the flow of salary deposits, not by investment magic.

The uncomfortable part arrives in your 40s. Once you've accumulated meaningful capital and salary growth plateaus, compounding becomes the engine. Until then, your return on salary—through promotion, job switching, and skill development—almost certainly exceeds any market return. A 10% raise is a 10% return on your current earnings. Market returns of 5–7% are weak by comparison. Optimize for salary and skill, not market timing.

Compounding only benefits people who already have capital. You're playing the accumulation game. Treat it like a job: maximize income, minimize living costs, move the money to investments. The compounding lecture is a luxury for people 15 years ahead of where you are.

**Key idea.** Time multiplies returns exponentially only if capital exists first; without capital, salary accumulation is linear.

**Hard truth.** Compounding favors people with existing wealth. Your salary is linear income. Achieving meaningful capital takes a decade of salaried work. Market returns of 5–7% cannot compete with salary growth and returns from career advancement during that phase.

**Check it yourself:** Calculate: 10% of your annual salary. Then calculate: 7% of your total accumulated investments so far. Compare the two numbers. Which is larger?
