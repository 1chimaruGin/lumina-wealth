# An automation architecture

Most people manage money reactively — balance appears in an account, they decide what to do with it. As an engineer, you already know reactive systems are fragile. The alternative is architecture: define flows so the right thing happens by default, and you only intervene when exceptions occur.

Start by mapping your salary destination. In Japan, your employer deposits salary into your primary bank account. From there, you can set up standing orders (定期振込) to split it automatically: emergency fund to one account, any savings goal to another, remainder stays for immediate spending. Each yen has a job the moment it lands.

The key is visibility without action. You're not managing each transfer — you're observing that it happened. This is different from traditional automation advice, which focuses on "set and forget." You still check. You still see money moving. But the movement is deterministic, not reactive.

Why this matters for your constraints: You have no spare capital but you can earn control. Each standing order is a small system that works while you sleep. With 8 hours a week available, automation saves those hours for work that produces income, not money busywork. When side income arrives in a separate account, the same architecture applies — split it by the same rules. No decision fatigue.

The trap is over-engineering early. Start with two flows: emergency fund and everything else. A three-bucket system is common but adds decision complexity. Get one layer working, observe it for a month, then refine.

For scattered focus, make one automation stream your rule. Don't set up three different "optimization experiments" simultaneously. One flow, one feedback loop, one month of observation. When that stabilizes, then consider the next layer.

**Key idea.** Define account flows so the right behavior happens by default, eliminating daily money decisions and freeing time for income work.

**Ask yourself:** You have scattered_focus. How many different money-automation setups are you currently running or tinkering with—and which one actually works hands-off without your weekly intervention?
