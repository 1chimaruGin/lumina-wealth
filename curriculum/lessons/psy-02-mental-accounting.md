# Mental accounting

Mental accounting is the tendency to treat money differently depending on its source, intended use, or how you acquired it. Richard Thaler formalized this in the 1980s through experiments showing that the origin of money shapes spending decisions far more than rational economics predicts. A ¥5,000 bonus is rarely used like ¥5,000 from your regular salary; a ¥2,000 bill found on the street enters a different mental category than ¥2,000 you earned through work.

This happens because your brain assigns money to separate "accounts" even though money is fungible—one yen is one yen, regardless of source. Someone who refuses to spend ¥50,000 from savings on a meal will happily do so from a bonus. A tax refund disappears on discretionary purchases while an equivalent paycheck increase gets saved. The "jar problem" captures this: putting money in jars labeled "rent," "groceries," or "emergency" makes the same money feel constrained or available depending on which container it occupies.

The mechanism is psychological. Your brain creates spending rules for each mental account—a savings "jar" feels protected from casual withdrawal, while found money feels like license to spend. This is partly protective: mental accounts prevent you from rationalizing away your entire emergency fund. But they also create economic inefficiency. You might leave money in a low-interest account labeled "vacation" while paying credit card interest on "general" spending, even though moving money between accounts costs nothing and currency is identical.

For side income specifically, mental accounting cuts both ways. If you mentally segregate earnings from freelance work, consulting, or side projects as "growth money," you're more likely to reinvest them. But you might also treat side income as "found money" and spend it reflexively, when reinvestment would accelerate your goal. The critical question is not whether you earn side income—it's which mental account you place it in.

**Key idea.** People assign money to separate mental accounts by source and use, creating spending rules that override fungibility—the core mechanism behind why bonuses feel different from salary.

**Ask yourself:** Your present_bias preference for immediate wins will test your first side income. Will you mentally treat it as 'fun money to spend now' or 'capital to reinvest'?
