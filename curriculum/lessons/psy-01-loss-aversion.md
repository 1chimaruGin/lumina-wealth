# Loss aversion

The pain of losing money cuts deeper than the joy of gaining the same amount. This asymmetry was first documented systematically in 1979 by psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky in their prospect theory research. When they asked people to choose between a sure loss of ¥10,000 or a 50% chance of losing ¥20,000, most picked the gamble—even though the expected value was worse. The reverse: offered a sure gain of ¥10,000 or a 50% chance of ¥20,000, people chose safety. The principle: losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains feel good.

This matters acutely to selling decisions. If you bought an asset (stock, currency, a completed project) and it dropped in value, loss aversion pulls you to hold it, hoping recovery will erase the paper loss. Conversely, when something gains value, you're tempted to sell early, locking in the win to avoid the risk of loss. This pattern—holding losers and selling winners—destroys returns over time.

The mechanism is partly psychological (your brain treats loss as a threat) and partly accounting-based: you're anchored to your purchase price and treat anything below it as a loss to avoid. Crucially, this doesn't require actual cash loss. If you spent three weeks building a feature that isn't gaining traction, the time already spent feels like a loss, and cutting it loose triggers the same pain as a cash loss.

In practice, the best traders and entrepreneurs train themselves to ignore sunk costs (time, money already gone) and decide purely on future value: "If I didn't already own this, would I buy it now at this price?" Loss aversion tricks you into answering "yes" to losers and "no" to winners—the opposite of optimal. Recognition is half the fix: notice when you're holding onto a project or decision because of past investment rather than future potential.

**Key idea.** Losses feel about twice as painful as equivalent gains feel good, causing you to hold losers and sell winners.

**Ask yourself:** Think back to when you switched between your last two side projects: which felt stronger—the pull of the new idea, or the push from hours already spent?
