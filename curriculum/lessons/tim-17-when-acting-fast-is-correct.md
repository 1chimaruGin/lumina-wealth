# When acting fast IS correct

Credit card debt in Japan legally maxes at 18% APR (確認: verify on your statement). At 18% compounded monthly, a ¥1,000,000 balance costs approximately ¥196,000 in interest in year one. In year two, it costs more—interest compounds on itself. By year five, without any payment, the original million grows to approximately ¥2,440,000. The cost of waiting multiplies exponentially.

Japanese fixed-income returns for comparison: savings accounts ~0.1%, 10-year JGBs ~1.2%, equities (Nikkei 225, long-term) ~8–10% nominal. None beat 18%. The closest is equities, and they carry downside risk; 18% is guaranteed cost, not hoped-for gain.

Mechanism: if you have capital that could either pay debt or invest, paying debt is mathematically superior. A ¥500,000 debt at 18% costs ¥90,000 annually in interest. The same capital invested in equities at 10% return would earn ¥50,000 pre-tax, or roughly ¥40,000 post-tax (20.315% on Japanese equities in a standard taxable account). Paying debt saves ¥90,000; investing nets ¥40,000. The advantage is ¥50,000 per year, and it compounds: next year's ¥90,000 is even larger.

The principle applies to positions, not just debt. You set a rule to sell a stock at ¥10,000. It falls to ¥8,000. Waiting for recovery "costs" you ¥2,000 of capital that could be deployed elsewhere, plus the opportunity cost of that capital earning 3–5% annually in a safer instrument. Every month you hold a losing position you've decided to exit extends your cost.

Speed is correct when the cost of delay—measured in compound interest or compounding opportunity cost—exceeds your transaction cost (trading fees, emotional friction, tax on exit). For debt above 15%, speed is almost always right. For positions that violate your rule, speed is nearly always right: the math does not reward waiting.

**Key idea.** When compound interest works against you, speed is correct. Waiting on debt or a broken position compounds your cost, not your gains.

**Hard truth.** Compound interest on debt is invisible—you don't watch ¥90,000 leave your account annually; you just see debt persist. Invisibility is why people wait. But the cost is compounding.

**Check it yourself:** Find a credit card statement (yours, a friend's, or from your bank's website). Calculate: (monthly interest charge shown) × 12 ÷ (balance shown that month) = APR. Verify it matches the quoted APR on the card's terms.
