# Cost of capital

The concept of "cost of capital" is that money is never free—even your own. When a venture capitalist invests $100,000 in a startup, they expect roughly 30% annual returns. Why not 10% or 50%? Because they're pricing what that capital could earn elsewhere: perhaps 5% in bonds or 8% in index funds. The difference—the spread between what you got and what you could have gotten—is the true cost of deploying capital to this opportunity instead of that one.

This applies directly to you, even without borrowing. When you invest three weeks of evenings building a product, you're not spending yen, but you're spending time with market value. At your backend skill level, freelance work pays roughly ¥2,500 to ¥3,500 per hour. Three weeks at 20 hours weekly is 60 hours—worth ¥150,000 to ¥210,000 in foregone earnings. That's your implicit capital cost. If that product generates zero income for three months, you've burned ¥150,000+ in opportunity. This explains your pattern of abandoning unfunded projects: you're not impatient, you're calculating cost correctly.

Why does capital cost anything? Because money compounds over time. A yen today can earn interest, deploy to a venture, or sit in bonds. A yen next year is worth less. A venture capitalist quoting 30% required return isn't greedy—they're pricing what capital costs to tie up: its earnings elsewhere, plus a risk premium for total loss. You see that number; you're reading the market's statement of what capital costs right now.

Inflation adds another layer. Japan's inflation averaged 2–3% annually since 2023—modest globally but real locally. Savings lose purchasing power; borrowed money nominally costs less, but real cost adjusted for inflation remains substantial.

The practical takeaway: don't feel guilty for refusing projects with slow payoff. A project demanding 60 hours with no income for six months costs you ¥150,000+ in opportunity. Reject it. But one that nets ¥150,000 in two months? That cost is justified. You're not scattered; you're allocating scarce resources to the highest-return stream, which your focused pattern demands.

**Key idea.** Capital always has a cost: the return you forfeit by deploying it here instead of there.
