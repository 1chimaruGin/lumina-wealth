# Commodity money

When cowrie shells became money in West African trade around the 1600s, no king issued them. Merchants simply agreed: these shells mean value. A Portuguese trader arriving in Benin knew cowrie shells mattered because everyone around him treated them as irreplaceable goods. The shells worked as money for three reasons.

First, they were scarce. Cowries come from specific ocean depths and weren't lying on every beach. You couldn't manufacture a thousand overnight. This scarcity created the friction needed for trade—if shells were abundant as sand, they'd be worthless.

Second, they were recognizable. A cowrie shell looks like a cowrie shell. No counterfeiting with painted pebbles or clay; the real thing has specific weight, gloss, and texture. Every trader from Timbuktu to Zanzibar knew exactly what they were accepting.

Third, they were divisible and portable. One shell was a unit. Five shells fit in a pouch. Unlike cattle (which existed as money in many cultures but required herding) or salt (which dissolved in rain), shells didn't degrade and didn't need infrastructure.

The key insight: money emerges from agreement on scarcity, not from law. Governments eventually replaced cowrie shells with stamped metal and then paper because those offered scale—you can issue millions of coins faster than collecting shells. But the mechanism was identical. When Roman soldiers received their pay in denarii, or when modern miners accept Bitcoin, the same logic holds. The user community collectively enforces three rules: this item is hard to copy, everyone recognizes it, and you can break it into smaller amounts. No issuer survives without these three properties.

Understanding this explains why most alternative currencies fail. A startup coins a digital token nobody recognizes. Another invents a local scrip that only one store accepts. Both violate the scarcity-recognizability-divisibility test. Real money—whether shells or code—solves for all three at once.

**Key idea.** Money forms when users agree something is scarce, recognizable, and divisible—government doesn't create, only enforce.

**Ask yourself:** Each time you've abandoned a side income project after a few weeks, did potential customers fail to recognize its value—or did you move on before they could?
