# Who is on the other side of your trade

When you place a buy order on the Tokyo Stock Exchange, a market maker profits on the spread before you earn a yen on the trade itself. For Nikkei 225 constituents, the spread is typically 1–2 yen per share. For less liquid stocks, 10–100 yen is common. A 50 yen spread on a 1000 yen stock is 5%. If the stock appreciates 5%, the spread has already consumed your entire gain.

The person on the other side of your trade is often not another investor—it is a market maker, an algorithmic trader, or an institution. They expect to win not by predicting direction but by exploiting structural advantages:

Latency: High-frequency traders execute in microseconds. Your retail order takes 100–500 milliseconds to reach the exchange. Informed traders have already repositioned.

Information: Institutions pay for Bloomberg terminals ($24,000 per year), real-time earnings feeds, and alternative data. Public news is already priced in by the time it reaches you.

Costs: Institutional investors pay 0.01–0.05% in commissions and receive price improvement. Retail investors in Japan typically pay 0.1–0.5% or flat fees—higher in percentage terms on smaller trades. These costs compound over a year.

The outcome is measurable. Studies on retail day traders consistently show 90–97% lose money. This is not emotion or lack of discipline. It is the mathematical result of a cost disadvantage and an information disadvantage playing out simultaneously across thousands of trades.

If your counterparty is another retail trader, neither of you has an advantage beyond luck. One will cover the market maker's spread by selling at the ask when they should have waited.

**Key idea.** Your counterparty has lower costs, faster information, or both—and the spread is their profit whether you win or lose.

**Hard truth.** Market makers profit on spreads alone; institutions exploit information and latency edges. Retail traders lose 90–97% of day trading attempts. Without capital or institutional access, you are not investing—you are transferring money to firms with structural advantages.

**Check it yourself:** Check the bid-ask spread on any TSE-listed stock on your trading platform. Calculate round-trip cost: (spread × 2) ÷ current price × 100 = percentage cost per trade. For a 1000 yen stock with 2 yen spread: (2 × 2) ÷ 1000 × 100 = 0.4% per trade. Ten trades = 4% in costs alone.
