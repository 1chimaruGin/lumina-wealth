# How venture capital actually works

Venture capital doesn't work the way most people imagine. VCs don't fund good ideas—they fund portfolios of risky bets because they need outliers.

Here's the structure: Limited partners (insurance companies, pension funds, family offices) invest roughly $100 million into a single VC fund. The VC firm takes a 2% management fee annually (that's $2 million just to operate) plus 20% of any profits they generate, called carry. After nine years, the fund must return capital or shut down. This creates an absolute constraint: the fund needs massive wins to cover the fees and meet limited partners' expected return.

The VC firm receives thousands of applications. It funds maybe 50–100 companies per fund. Statistically: about 30–40% fail entirely; 50% return 1x–3x of invested capital; and 1–2 companies return 20x–100x. Those outliers return the entire fund. If one company reaches $1 billion valuation and the VC owned 25% at investment, that's $250 million from a $100 million fund. That single exit covers all the failures and all the modest returns.

This has two hard implications. First: VCs cannot fund stable, profitable small businesses. A company returning 3x in five years looks like failure given their fee structure and timescale. Second: you almost certainly cannot raise VC capital if you're building from zero capital in Japan. VCs fund founders with networks, prior exits, or extraordinary credentials. A $250,000 check barely justifies their overhead—they prefer $2 million+ checks to unknown founders.

If you grasp this, you realize building side income in Tokyo cannot follow the VC playbook. You need a model that generates short-term cash without requiring either VC capital or eight years of unprofitable growth. That means bootstrapping, not pitching.

**Key idea.** VC funds need outlier returns because their fee structure demands it; ordinary growth doesn't survive portfolio math.
