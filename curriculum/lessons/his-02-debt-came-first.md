# Debt came first

Your earliest ancestors didn't need coins to borrow grain. In Sumer, around 3000 BCE, temple administrators recorded loans of barley on clay tablets—the oldest known accounting. A farmer could walk to the temple, request grain to plant, and leave owing a percentage back at harvest. The temple carved marks into clay to track the debt, creating the first systematic record of who owed what, and why. Tallies—notched wooden sticks or clay tokens—worked the same way across cultures: one notch per unit, split between creditor and debtor so both held identical proof. For millennia, debt was managed without a single coin in circulation.

What's striking is how this solved a real problem: survival requires resources you don't yet have. Coinage came later—the first standardized coins appeared in Lydia (modern Turkey) around 650 BCE—because debt systems were already working. Coins didn't invent lending; they just made it portable and standardized.

But debt systems created a secondary problem. Borrowers who fell behind could lose land, family, freedom. By the time Hebrew law formalized the jubilee—a 50-year reset where all debts dissolved and slaves went free (Leviticus 25)—the imbalance had become unbearable. The jubilee was not mercy; it was crisis management. Without it, creditors would own everything and everyone would serve them.

This isn't history. It's a mechanism. Debt lets you access resources faster than you can save. That speed matters when survival is at stake. It still matters when you're building. But the speed creates obligation, and obligation without release creates a different kind of poverty: the kind where you're perpetually catching up.

**Key idea.** Debt existed before coinage because people always need resources faster than they can save—creating immediate access at the cost of future obligation.
