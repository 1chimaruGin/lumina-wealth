# Central banks and the policy rate

A central bank controls the policy rate—the overnight rate at which banks lend to each other—by buying and selling securities to adjust how much cash banks hold. The Federal Reserve, Bank of Japan, and European Central Bank deploy this single lever to anchor every other interest rate downstream: mortgages, credit cards, business loans. When the Fed raised its policy rate from near zero in March 2022 to above 5% by late 2023, commercial banks quickly raised their lending rates. That's what central banks control.

What they don't control is what happens next. A central bank influences lending demand, but cannot force it. In 2008, when Lehman Brothers collapsed, the Fed cut its policy rate to near zero. Credit still froze. Banks had no appetite to lend despite zero borrowing costs; borrowers had no appetite to borrow. The rate signal broke because fear overwhelmed incentives.

The Bank of Japan discovered this boundary over decades. Beginning in February 1999, it held rates near zero to fight deflation and sluggish growth. For more than two decades, rates stayed flat. Inflation barely moved. This is the liquidity trap: once rates hit zero, the central bank's primary tool stops working. You cannot lower rates below zero in normal systems, so you have spent your ammunition. Japan's experience—twenty years of near-zero rates, persistent low inflation, weak wage growth—shows that cheap money alone doesn't create demand.

Inflation itself sits partly outside central bank control. Raising rates makes borrowing expensive, which can cool spending and reduce price pressure. But the lag is long—six months to eighteen months before inflation data responds—and the effect is blunt. Supply shocks (sudden oil spikes), wage dynamics (labor scarcity), or import costs can overpower what a central bank can do with rates. A central bank pushes conditions, not outcomes. It shapes incentives but cannot command them.

**Key idea.** A central bank commands its policy rate but only influences credit flow, lending demand, and inflation; zero rates break the mechanism.

**Ask yourself:** You abandon projects when payoffs seem distant (your present_bias pattern). Since BoJ rate changes take 6–18 months to reshape markets, how would you use that timeline to set interim milestones that keep you engaged until the macro shift actually hits?
