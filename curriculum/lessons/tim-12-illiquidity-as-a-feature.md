# Illiquidity as a feature

The average equity investor realizes 10–15% annual losses through panic selling—exiting at market bottoms and missing recoveries. Illiquidity prevents this through mechanics, not willpower. If your money sits in a 企業年金 (corporate pension) with no withdrawal until age 60, you cannot sell when panic hits. You are forced to hold through the crash.

The global equity market declined approximately 57% from peak to trough during the 2007–2009 financial crisis. Retail investors who panic-sold near the bottom in March 2009 locked in losses. By 2013, the market had recovered; by 2020, it was up roughly 250% from the bottom. An investor structurally unable to access funds—trapped by illiquidity—captured that entire recovery. One who could sell chose to exit and never recovered.

The return from illiquidity is the return from NOT making that choice. For Japanese savers, retirement accounts in 企業年金 or iDeCo (locked until age 60) create that friction. Research suggests retail accounts in liquid instruments turn over at 70–100% annually, while locked pension accounts typically turn over at 5–15% annually—roughly a 5–10x difference in trading frequency. Each extra trade introduces timing risk and transaction costs. The locked account's forced hold is an edge.

But illiquidity requires capital to lock up. You cannot benefit from an inability to access money you do not have. This is wealth protection for those who already saved, not a tool for building wealth from zero.

The mechanism is real: forced holding prevents catastrophic market-timing errors. But illiquidity costs access; if you need funds before expiration, you pay penalties. This is only a feature if stable income and time horizons align with the lock.

**Key idea.** Forced inability to access money prevents panic selling; that structural discipline is a measurable return most investors do not count.

**Hard truth.** Illiquidity only protects those with existing capital who hold through crashes. With zero spare yen, you cannot lock anything away. This advantage is for wealth already accumulated, not for earning a first yen.

**Check it yourself:** Find the published annual turnover rate for a Japanese corporate pension fund (企業年金) in FSA data and for a Japanese retail stock trading account. Subtract. That percentage-point difference is the structural reduction in panic-sell opportunities between the two.
