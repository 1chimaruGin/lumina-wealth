# Debt versus equity

When you need money to start or grow something, you face a choice: borrow it or give up ownership. These paths cost completely differently.

Debt means you promise to repay a fixed amount plus interest. A bank loan, a credit card advance, or bonds all work this way. The cost is explicit: if you borrow 100,000 JPY at 5% annually, you owe 5,000 JPY per year in interest alone, regardless of whether your venture succeeds or fails. You keep full ownership and control, but you're contractually obligated to repay even if the project flops. This is why businesses with steady cash flow prefer debt—a bakery with reliable daily sales can manage predictable loan payments. But it's brutal if your income becomes unreliable.

Equity means you trade ownership for capital. A venture capitalist invests 100,000 JPY and gets, say, 10% of your company. They profit only if the business grows—they lose if it fails. The cost to you is invisible at first but enormous over time: you've given away 10% of every future dollar the business earns. If your side project eventually makes 10 million JPY annually, that 10% equity stake is worth 1 million JPY per year to the investor, not the 5,000 JPY they'd earn from a 5% loan. Equity investors also usually demand influence—board seats, veto rights, strategic input. You lose autonomy.

The irony: startups love equity because it doesn't drain cash today. But they pay for it later through dilution. Mature businesses love debt because interest is fixed and predictable. Neither is universally cheaper—it depends on your risk and growth rate.

**Key idea.** Debt drains cash flow now; equity dilutes ownership later. Neither is cheaper universally—your growth rate and cash position decide.

**Ask yourself:** When you've abandoned projects before, which would have felt worse six months later: owing monthly debt to a bank, or having given equity to an investor who now owns a piece of your failed venture?
