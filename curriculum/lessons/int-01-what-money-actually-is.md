# What money actually is

Money does three distinct jobs: it's a unit of account, a medium of exchange, and a store of value. The order you list them in is not accidental. Each one is harder to build than the last, and crucially, something can work perfectly as money while failing catastrophically at the third task.

Start with unit of account. Before money, people used barter, which demands a "double coincidence of wants" — you have chickens, I have fish, but you want rice and I want cloth. We're stuck. Money solves this by establishing a shared standard that prices everything. The moment a community agrees "we measure value in shells" or "we measure value in yen," thousands of prices crystallize instantly. You now know exactly what your chickens are worth without negotiating each trade individually.

Medium of exchange follows naturally. Because everything has a price in that standard, you can trade your chickens for shells, then shells for rice, then rice for cloth. Money moves between people smoothly because everyone understands its value.

Store of value is the hardest. Money should preserve your purchasing power over time, but it often fails. Weimar hyperinflation (1923) destroyed the medium of exchange function — you couldn't store value in marks for more than days. Yet the unit of account function persisted: prices remained quoted in marks even as the currency's value collapsed. The hierarchy is real.

History shows this order repeatedly. Shells, beads, and cattle never held value perfectly — they rotted, broke, or escaped — yet they dominated as money in their societies because they solved the unit-of-account problem spectacularly. Everyone could see and value a cowry shell without debate. Only later did societies worry about whether the money retained value.

Modern money (like the yen) does all three reasonably well, which is why it seems natural to bundle them. But understanding that they're separate functions reveals something crucial: a payment system or earning stream can be real money for you even if the broader world questions whether it stores value.

**Key idea.** Money is first a shared standard for pricing, second a tool for trading, and third a way to hold wealth — each harder than the last.
